# Paris as Tango's Recurring Savior: Two Waves That Changed Everything

Twice in the twentieth century, tango crossed the Atlantic to Paris and returned home transformed. The first time, between 1907 and 1914, a raw, scandalous dance from the docks and tenement houses of Buenos Aires conquered Europe's cultural capital and gained the respectability that Argentina's own elite had denied it. The second time, on November 11, 1983, a group of aging milongueros — some rescued from butcher shops and gambling dens — took the stage at the Théâtre du Châtelet and saved their art form from extinction. Both times, Parisian validation triggered a global chain reaction. Both times, the dance that returned to Buenos Aires was different from the one that had left. And both times, the encounter revealed as much about Paris as it did about tango — about the city's appetite for sensuality dressed as art, its unique position as the world's cultural arbiter, and a century-old axis between two cities that recognized something essential in each other.

This is the story of those two encounters, the thread that connects them, and the deeper question they raise: why Paris?

---

## How tango crossed the Atlantic the first time

Tango did not arrive in Paris on a single ship or in a single year. It seeped across the ocean through multiple channels — phonograph recordings, sheet music, traveling musicians, and above all, the sons of Argentina's cattle barons making their obligatory European tours.

The earliest documented channel was musical rather than physical. In **1906**, a thousand copies of the sheet music for "La Morocha" — composed by **Enrique Saborido** with lyrics by **Ángel Villoldo** — were loaded aboard the **ARA Presidente Sarmiento**, Argentina's naval training vessel, and distributed at ports worldwide during its circumnavigation. The following year, Villoldo himself traveled to Paris alongside guitarist **Alfredo Gobbi** (father of the later orchestra leader) and Gobbi's wife, singer **Flora Rodríguez**, specifically to make phonograph recordings. Recording technology was more advanced in Europe than in Argentina at the time, and Flora recorded "La Morocha" in Paris in 1907 — likely the first tango vocal captured on European soil. That same year, the **Garde Républicaine** orchestra recorded Villoldo's "El Choclo" and another tango, "Joaquina," marking tango's entry into the French musical mainstream.

But tango was fundamentally a dance, and it needed bodies to travel. The crucial intermediaries were wealthy young Argentine men — the *niños bien* — who encountered tango in the downtown cafés and dance halls of Buenos Aires and carried it abroad. As dancer **Maurice Mouvet** (1889–1927) recalled in his 1915 memoir *Art of Dancing*: "It was a group of young South American boys who really introduced the Tango. While they were frequenting Maxim's, dancing their national dance whenever they could bribe the orchestra to play their music, I went there to dance with them. Unable, very often, to find a girl who knew the Tango, I would dance with one of the men." This was the paradox that would define tango's Parisian career: a dance born in the brothels and slaughterhouse districts of Buenos Aires, transmitted to Paris by the very Argentine aristocrats who would never have danced it at home.

The man who did the most to make tango presentable for European ballrooms was **Camille de Rhynal**, a French dancer, choreographer, composer, and impresario. In **1907**, de Rhynal organized the first tango competition at the **Imperial Country Club in Nice**, working with **Grand Duchess Anastasia Mikhailovna of Russia** to, as he put it, "take away its naughtier, more objectionable features, so it could be presented in a ballroom." That same year, he proposed introducing tango to the London stage through theatre manager George Edwardes, but actress Gabrielle Ray, after a demonstration, agreed with Edwardes that it "could not possibly be presented to a London audience" in its original form. De Rhynal was undeterred. He organized annual dance championships in Paris beginning in **1909**, when his partner **Mado Minty** won the Excelsior newspaper's grand dancing competition. He opened a tango academy, composed tango music — including "El Chichirico: Tango Argentino," published in *Album Musica* in 1911 — and became chief dance instructor at **Magic-City**, the amusement park near the Pont d'Alma that would become tangomania's ground zero.

Mouvet, a New York-born dancer of Belgian parentage raised in Paris, claimed to be "the first professional to perform this dance in Paris" when he danced it with his partner **Leona** at the **Café des Ambassadeurs** in **1908**. Whether or not this claim is strictly accurate — Argentine dancers were performing informally earlier — Mouvet became tango's most visible champion. He performed for **King Edward VII** in Biarritz in April 1910. After marrying **Florence Walton** in 1911, the couple introduced the concept of "Tango Teas" to London and New York and became the template for elegant exhibition dancing. His 1915 book catalogued the "original eight steps of the Tango": Promenade, Cortez, Media Luna, El Paso, L'éventail, Les Ciseaux, El Pados, and the Single Three — a codification that reveals how far the dance had already traveled from its improvisational roots.

Meanwhile, authentic Argentine expertise was arriving. **Enrique Saborido**, composer of "La Morocha," traveled to Paris in **1911** and stayed until 1914, teaching both musicians how to play tango properly and students how to dance it. The Marquise Reské, widow of the famous tenor Jean de Reszké, helped him access high society. **Bernabé Simarra**, who had won numerous dance competitions in Buenos Aires and Montevideo, arrived the same year, recruited by **Mademoiselle Papillon**, a French variety artist who had learned tango with him in South America. Simarra won several Parisian dance competitions in 1912–1914 and taught at both the Excelsior Hotel and de Rhynal's academy.

## 1913, the year the world went tango-mad

By 1913 — the year H.G. Wells called **"the year of the tango"** — the dance had achieved critical mass. The French chronicler Sem observed that "the half of Paris rubs against the other half." Journalist Clément Vautel of *Le Matin* described tango in December 1913 as "a genuine epilepsy that shakes everyone." The satirical magazine *La Vie Parisienne* described a "tangomètre" — a spring worn like a garter to count dance steps. The *Pall Mall Gazette* noted: "They name their dresses Tango, their hats Tango, their dogs Tango."

**Magic-City** became the epicenter. This amusement park, located off the Pont d'Alma, reinvented itself as Paris's most elite tango destination in the spring of 1913. It offered dance lessons, *thés dansants* three days per week from 4 to 7 PM, evening dancing several nights weekly, Friday galas, and holiday balls. Admission was **3 francs** for a tea dance and **10 francs** for galas. Its house composer, **René André**, created "Magic-Tango" in 1913, which was published as sheet music by Marchetti, recorded for Pathé, and produced as a piano roll — the multimedia marketing of its era.

But tango was not confined to one venue. **Bal Tabarin**, **Bal Bullier**, and **Ba-ta-clan** all featured tango by summer 1911. Ba-ta-clan declared in its advertisements: "From now on, the Argentine tango is in fashion here, and we will dance it every night." At Bal Bullier on the avenue de l'Observatoire, painters **Sonia and Robert Delaunay** famously danced the tango; Sonia's 1913 painting *Le Bal Bullier* (exhibited in Berlin as *Tango au Bal Bullier*) captured the whirling scene. Working-class clubs in Montmartre embraced it. Music-hall star **Mistinguett** (Jeanne Florentine Bourgeois, 1875–1956), already famous for the violently erotic *Valse chaloupée* ("Apache Dance") she had debuted with **Max Dearly** at the Moulin Rouge in 1909, danced a tango in a music-hall production as early as 1910, partnered with **Barthélemy Bottallo**, director of the Sorbonne Dance Academy.

The *thé dansant* — the afternoon tea dance — became tango's primary vehicle for social spread. These events combined tea, sandwiches, and cakes with dancing in a setting where smoking and alcohol were prohibited, creating a veneer of respectability that allowed bourgeois women to participate without scandal. As Miss Gladys Beattie Crozier wrote in *The Tango & How to Dance It*: "What could be pleasanter on a dull wintry afternoon, at 5 o'clock or so, than to drop into one of the cheery little Thé Dansant Clubs?" Hotels and restaurants across Paris and London rapidly adopted the format.

The craze leaped to other countries. **Vernon Castle** (William Vernon Blyth, 1887–1918) and **Irene Castle** (née Foote, 1893–1969), an Anglo-American couple who had become "the sensation of Paris" in spring 1912 performing at the **Café de Paris** and the **Ferme du Pré Catalan**, returned to New York and became America's most famous dance couple, popularizing the tango alongside their own Castle Walk. They presented tango as acceptable because they were, as cultural historians note, "young, clean, married, and well-mannered." Irene, dressed by couturier **Lucile** (Lady Duff Gordon), became a fashion icon. Their orchestra was led by **James Reese Europe**, a pioneering African American musician who would later lead the Harlem Hellfighters band.

## The scandal that only made tango more irresistible

The moral panic was swift and international, and it only fueled the craze.

In **November 1913**, **Pope Pius X** declared tango dancing "immoral and off-limits to Catholics." Catholics who danced the tango were instructed to include it in their confessions and perform penance. On **January 16, 1914**, the *New York Times* published a formal condemnation by **Cardinal Basilio Pompili**, writing on behalf of the Pope: "The tango, which has already been condemned by illustrious Bishops, and is prohibited even in Protestant countries, must be absolutely prohibited in the seat of the Roman Pontiff, the centre of the Catholic religion." The specific objections were physical: chests touching, legs intertwining, pelvic contact, the dance's origins in what the Vatican called the world of "barbaric contortions."

A famous story circulated that the Pope witnessed a private tango demonstration and reconsidered, calling it "one of the dullest things imaginable" and suggesting the *furlana*, a Venetian folk dance, as an alternative. This was published by French journalist **Jean Carrière**, correspondent for *Le Temps*, on January 28, 1914. The Vatican denied the story — it was essentially fake news of the Belle Époque — but it sparked a genuine craze for the furlana, dubbed "the dance of the pope" in opposition to tango, "the dance of sin." **Cardinal Léon-Adolphe Amette**, Archbishop of Paris, went further, banning tango teachers from the city outright. By April 1915, five tango instructors in Paris had been issued decrees of expulsion. **Kaiser Wilhelm II** of Germany, on **November 17, 1913**, forbade his officers from dancing the tango while in uniform, describing the dance as *Rinnsteinkind* — "a child of the gutter." The ban was reportedly triggered by the discovery that his daughter-in-law, **Kronprinzessin Cecilie**, was taking tango lessons. Yet despite the imperial prohibition, the **Countess of Schwerin-Löwitz**, wife of the president of the Prussian Parliament, hosted a tango party where diplomats and high-ranking officers danced freely.

As for **Queen Mary of England**, the historical record contradicts the popular narrative of a royal ban. At the **1914 Kenwood House ball** on June 11, hosted by Grand Duke Michael Mikhailovich of Russia, Maurice Mouvet and Florence Walton were invited to perform. Queen Mary **specifically requested a tango demonstration**, as she had never seen one, and indicated she was "delighted" by the approximately 45-minute performance — the first royal command performance by North American dancers.

American theologian **Campbell Morgan** suggested in 1913 that tango confirmed Darwin's theory as "regression from man to ape." Boston Mayor **John "Honey Fitz" Fitzgerald** banned tango from city dance halls in October 1913. U.S. Vice President **Thomas Marshall** denounced it at a Methodist camp meeting. The condemnations were endless — and uniformly ineffective.

In France, one intervention may have helped more than any papal edict. On **October 25, 1913**, poet and Académie Française member **Jean Richepin** delivered a speech arguing that tango's "impropriety" was attributable to the dancers, not the dance itself — a distinction that gave French society intellectual permission to keep dancing. The speech had enormous international impact.

## The sanitization and the fashion revolution

What Paris danced was not, of course, what Buenos Aires danced. The original Argentine tango featured *cortes y quebradas* — the defining movements borrowed from African-Argentine dance traditions. A *corte* (literally "cut") was a sudden, suggestive pause — a break in the standard figures that served as prelude to a *quebrada* (literally "break"), an improvised, semi-athletic contortion in which the leader bent the partner backward at the waist and came into full-body contact, with *no hay luz* — "no light" — between the dancers. As Argentine writer Carlos Octavio Bunge described in 1906: "to break, bend your partner backwards at her waist, and balancing on top of her, come into full body contact and move slowly and rhythmically to the music."

In the European sanitization, these moves were largely eliminated. The close embrace was loosened. The typical tango improvisation was, as one scholar noted, "almost completely missing" in the Parisian version, replaced by encoded, standardized steps taught in academies. The habanera rhythm was emphasized over tango's rawer original pulse. The Buenos Aires newspaper *P.B.T.* protested that "the tango which we have exported and which in France is called *le tango* has nothing traditional in it." The French, unapologetically, called it *le tango parisien*.

De Rhynal drove the sanitization from his academy and his position at Magic-City. The Castles did the same in America. French and Spanish composers — René André, Manuel Sarrablo, Joaquin Cassadó — wrote "Parisian tango" music infused with French and Spanish exoticism rather than authentic Argentine forms. The academic journal *Twentieth-Century Music* notes that references to Bizet's *Carmen* were "extremely common" in descriptions of tango music, suggesting French audiences processed the dance through an existing framework of Spanish exoticism.

Yet tango's impact on fashion was real and revolutionary. The signature color was **Tango Orange** — a vivid yellow-orange. According to fashion lore, a French designer stuck with unsold stock of orange fabric renamed it "Tango Orange" and watched it fly off shelves. The most popular fabric became **Satin Tango** in yellow and orange. "Trendsetters could don the color Tango Orange even if they could not tango dance."

The dance demanded new clothing. **Corsets were cast off** — incompatible with tango's close embrace and active footwork. **Slit skirts** appeared; the *Bystander* of October 8, 1913 noted "the slit skirt, which owes its birth to the craze of the hour." Dresses grew shorter, stopping at mid-calf. Feathers on hats pivoted from horizontal to vertical. Special **tango shoes** appeared — pink-dyed satin slippers survive in museum collections. The convergence with **Paul Poiret's** (1879–1944) fashion revolution was striking: Poiret had eliminated the corset from fashion starting in 1906, introduced the *jupe-culotte* (divided skirt) in 1911, and the hobble skirt in 1910. His looser, corseted-free silhouettes were precisely what tango required, and tango popularized them across the broader market. Couturier **Jean Paquin** specifically designed gowns for tango, shown during "dress parades" at London's tango teas in 1913.

World War I killed the first tangomania. By April 1915, Paris — "the very city that was recognized as having launched the Tango craze" — had turned its back on the dance. Bal Bullier was requisitioned for military uniform production. But tango had already changed the world. And when it returned after the war, it came back in a chastened but permanent form.

## The interwar thread that kept tango alive in Paris

The 1920s and 1930s brought a quieter but deeper Argentine tango presence to Paris. The initial scandal had subsided; tango was now accepted nightlife, and a significant infrastructure of Argentine musicians, nightclubs, and cultural venues established itself in the city.

The central figure was **Manuel Pizarro**, a bandoneonist who arrived in Marseille in **August 1920** and within three months was performing at the cabaret **La Princesse** on **6, rue Fontaine** in Pigalle. When entertainment mogul Elie Volterra renamed the venue **El Garrón** by March 1920, Pizarro formed its house orchestra. With his brothers — Domingo, Salvador, Alfredo, Tanga, José, and Miguel — he maintained several nightclubs from 1923 onward that became launching pads for Argentine tango musicians arriving in Paris. On the ground floor of the same building, **Le Palermo** operated as a sister venue. Together, El Garrón and Le Palermo became, as one historian put it, "the Mecca of tango in Paris" for decades.

**Francisco Canaro** (1888–1964), one of tango's greatest orchestra leaders, performed in Paris with his ensemble in **1925**, playing at the Exposition des Arts Décoratifs and the **Club Florida**, described as "the most aristocratic" establishment in the city. His brother **Rafael Canaro** stayed in France from **1925 to 1939**, leading his own orchestra after 1926 and performing at the Ambassadeurs, Florida, Ermitage, and Claridge. He recorded with France's Odeon label before returning to Argentina at the outbreak of World War II.

The violinist **Eduardo Bianco** (1892–1959) arrived in Europe in 1924 and formed the **Bianco-Bachicha orchestra** with bandoneonist **Juan Bautista Deambroggio** ("Bachicha"). They became fixtures of Parisian nightlife, and Bianco opened a music publishing company in the city. His career later took a dark turn: he performed for Hitler and Goebbels, who preferred tango to jazz, and his composition *Plegaria* became known in concentration camps as the *Tango de la Muerte* — the tango of death. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: The concentration camp association with Plegaria requires further verification from Holocaust scholarship.]

One tragic figure haunts this period: **Eduardo Arolas** (1892–1924), known as "El Tigre del Bandoneón," one of tango's most important early composers. He spent his final years in Paris and died there on **September 29, 1924**, at age 32. His handwritten letters from Paris survive in the Pecourt Collection at UC Santa Barbara.

Then came **Carlos Gardel** — and everything changed.

Born **Charles Romuald Gardès** on December 11, 1890, in Toulouse, France (though his birthplace remains disputed, with some claiming Tacuarembó, Uruguay), Gardel grew up in Buenos Aires's Abasto neighborhood after his mother emigrated when he was about three. By the late 1920s, he was the most famous tango artist alive. His **Parisian debut came in September/October 1928**. On approximately October 2, the newspaper *Le Gaulois* reported his presentation to the Parisian press at the Club Florida, where he performed wearing "a harmonious blue costume with brilliant embroidery, surrounded by four guitarists in grey." The paper called his success "considerable" and described him as "a great artist." *Excelsior* wrote on October 7: "After the evening we'll take you to Florida, to hear the famous South American star Carlos Gardel who has been revolutionizing Paris for several days."

Gardel **sold 70,000 records in his first three months** in Paris. He counted among his friends **Charlie Chaplin, Lucienne Boyer, Mistinguett**, and **Maurice Chevalier**. Between 1931 and 1933, he made several films at **Paramount's Joinville Studios** outside Paris, including *Las luces de Buenos Aires* (1931), *¡Esperáme!* (1932), *Melodía de arrabal* (1932), and *La casa es seria* (1933) — the last of which was lost forever when German troops bombed the Joinville studios in 1940. His lyricist and creative partner **Alfredo Le Pera** joined him during the Paris film period. Gardel's death in a plane crash in Medellín, Colombia, on **June 24, 1935**, at age 44, ended the brightest chapter of tango in interwar Paris.

## The ballroom divergence and the long disconnect

While Argentine musicians kept tango alive in Parisian nightlife, another process was underway: the formalization of a distinct European ballroom tango that would eventually bear little resemblance to its Argentine parent.

The codification began in England. In **October 1922**, the first formal proposal for English tango was made — **30 bars per minute** in 4/4 time, to be danced only to "modern tunes." The decisive moment came at the **1929 "Great Conference"** in England, organized by the Official Board of Ballroom Dancing under chairman **Philip J.S. Richardson**, which decreed that "the first aim of the profession should be to cater for the general dancer and not the expert" and standardized tempos, including tango at **32–34 bars per minute**. By 1930, the "Standard Four" — Waltz, Foxtrot, Tango, Quickstep — were firmly established as the basis of the English Style. In **1934**, dramatic head snaps were introduced to ballroom tango under German influence, a movement "totally foreign to Argentine and Uruguayan tango." The **Imperial Society of Teachers of Dancing** codified syllabi with bronze, silver, and gold levels. In America, **Arthur Murray** standardized a looser version for his chain of dance schools.

The result was two dances sharing only a name. Ballroom tango used staccato, snatching foot movements; Argentine tango glided. Ballroom tango prescribed fixed step sequences; Argentine tango was improvisational. Ballroom tango pulled the upper body apart while connecting at the pelvis; Argentine tango embraced fully at the chest. For European audiences, this standardized version was the only tango they would know for decades.

World War II severed what remained of the Argentine connection. Paris fell to Germany on **June 14, 1940**. Rafael Canaro had already returned to Argentina in 1939. The Argentine musician community largely departed. No specific documentation survives of tango performance during the German occupation of Paris, though dance in general continued at some level in the city's nightlife. After the war, France's massive reconstruction left little room for tango culture. In Argentina, the Golden Age was peaking under Perón in the 1940s — but this flourishing never transmitted to post-war Europe.

Then Argentina itself turned against tango. After Perón's overthrow in **1955**, successive military regimes suppressed the dance through bans on public gatherings that directly limited milongas. As historian **Christine Denniston** documented in *The Meaning of Tango*, laws banning minors from nightclubs were rigidly enforced at tango venues but not at rock clubs — because rock venues could afford bribes while milongas could not. Young men had no incentive to spend three years learning tango when the girls were all at rock shows. The **"Dirty War" (1976–1983)** brought censorship, disappearances, and the association of tango with Peronism made it politically suspect. By the early 1980s, tango was, in the words of legendary lyricist **Enrique Cadícamo**, "a memory."

One extraordinary figure kept tango music — if not the social dance — alive in European consciousness during this period. **Astor Piazzolla** (1921–1992) traveled to Paris in **1954** on a grant from the French government, awarded after his "Buenos Aires Symphony in Three Movements" won the Fabian Sevitzky Award. He studied composition with **Nadia Boulanger**, the most prestigious composition teacher in the world, whose students included Aaron Copland, Philip Glass, and Quincy Jones. Piazzolla was ashamed of his tango background and hid it, presenting only classical compositions. The encounter that changed his life came when Boulanger persisted in asking what instrument he played. As Piazzolla recalled: "I didn't want to tell her that I was a bandoneón player, because I thought, 'Then she will throw me from the fourth floor.' Finally, I confessed and she asked me to play some bars of a tango of my own. She suddenly opened her eyes, took my hand and told me: 'You idiot, that's Piazzolla!'" She recognized that tango was the music he should write — "the music from his heart and not from his head."

In Paris, Piazzolla absorbed counterpoint, heard **Gerry Mulligan's** jazz octet (which inspired him to form his own upon returning to Buenos Aires), and in **1955** recorded a series of tangos with members of the **Paris Opera Orchestra** and jazz pianist **Martial Solal** — sessions arranged by **Yves Baquet** of Editions Universelles and **Marcel Feijóo**, a French tango orchestra leader. He even adopted a new physical stance, playing the bandoneón standing up with his right foot on a chair — previously, bandoneonists played seated.

Piazzolla returned repeatedly to Europe. In **1977**, he performed a memorable concert at the **Olympia** in Paris. His second Quintet, formed in 1978, toured the world for eleven years. On **August 5, 1990**, back in Paris, he suffered the brain hemorrhage that would eventually kill him in Buenos Aires on July 4, 1992. His career is perhaps the ultimate illustration of tango's Paris connection: the city where a French teacher gave him permission to be himself, and the city where, decades later, his body finally failed.

## A salon on the rue des Lombards prepares the ground

The revival did not arrive unannounced. On **November 19, 1981**, a tanguería called **Trottoirs de Buenos Aires** opened at **37, rue des Lombards** in the Les Halles quarter of Paris — the first venue in Europe dedicated exclusively to tango performance. It was founded by a cooperative of 23 associates — French, Argentine, and other South Americans — including painter **Antonio Seguí**, artist **Pérez Celis**, and singer **Susana Rinaldi**. Its godfather (*padrino*) was **Julio Cortázar** (1914–1984), the great Argentine novelist who had lived in Paris since 1951, working as a translator for UNESCO, and who had contributed tango lyrics for an album titled *Trottoirs de Buenos Aires* (1980), set to music by **Edgardo Cantón**.

Cortázar's presence was emblematic of a deeper literary bond between Paris and Buenos Aires. His masterpiece *Rayuela* (*Hopscotch*, 1963) alternates between Paris and Buenos Aires, its protagonist an Argentine intellectual navigating bohemian Paris. **Victoria Ocampo** (1890–1979), founder of the literary journal *Sur*, maintained extensive French connections. **Ricardo Güiraldes**, who had been among tango's earliest ambassadors to Paris before World War I, saw his gaucho novel *Don Segundo Sombra* translated and celebrated in Paris in 1932. The definitive scholarly work on the relationship would be written by **Nardo Zalko**, a Buenos Aires native who spent most of his life in Paris: *Paris–Buenos Aires, Un Siècle de Tango*.

During the Argentine military dictatorship, Trottoirs served as a crucial meeting space for political exiles. Major Argentine artists performed there: the **Sexteto Mayor** inaugurated the venue with a three-week residency, followed by **Rubén Juárez**, **Salgán-De Lío**, **Roberto Goyeneche**, **Raúl Lavié**, and others. A University of Utah thesis by Suan L. Pineda argues the Trottoirs project was "crucial in the genre's second renaissance in France" and constructed "a space in diaspora where an alternate narrative of Argentina" could be articulated during the Dirty War.

For two full years before the show that changed everything, Trottoirs de Buenos Aires had been quietly reawakening Parisian audiences to authentic Argentine tango.

## Segovia and Orezzoli imagine the impossible

**Claudio Gastón Segovia** (August 31, 1933 – December 21, 2025) was a scenic and costume designer who had studied at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes "Manuel Belgrano" and the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes Ernesto de la Cárcova. His first major project, in 1967, was *La raíz y la tierra*, a production featuring **Mercedes Sosa** that toured sixteen Soviet republics and multiple European countries. **Héctor Orezzoli** (1953 – December 5, 1991), nicknamed "Yuyo," had studied literature and psychology at the University of Buenos Aires and drama at the University of Belgrano. He was a stage director and designer of remarkable range. They met in approximately **1972** and formed an inseparable creative partnership.

Segovia had conceived the idea of a tango show as early as 1972–1974. He wanted to present what he called *chic reo* — "elegant roughness" — the beauty of popular culture at its most authentic. He initially hoped to collaborate with Astor Piazzolla, but the partnership never materialized for lack of resources.

The model came from **Flamenco Puro** (1980), their production at the Teatro Lope de Vega in Seville, created after two years of research in Andalucía selecting 25 Romani artists. In **1982**, **Michel Guy** — former French Minister of Culture and founder/director of the **Festival d'Automne** in Paris — asked Argentine theater director **Jorge Lavelli** if he knew of a distinctive show for the festival. Lavelli recommended Segovia. After viewing the Flamenco Puro video, Guy was "dazzled" and offered a slot in the 1983 Festival.

The obstacles were extraordinary. **No producer would invest**. Segovia's mother financed the initial production using the life insurance payout from his father's death. Famous tango singers — **Tita Merello**, **Rosita Quiroga**, **Susana Rinaldi** — declined to participate, believing the show was doomed. Even **Juan Carlos Copes**, who would become the show's choreographer, initially called the idea *un delirio* — "a delirium."

## Finding the cast among the forgotten

Segovia and Orezzoli did not want polished stage performers. They wanted milongueros — social dancers who embodied tango's authentic culture. They recruited six dance couples and one solo female dancer for the original cast, and what they found was a generation of artists that history had discarded.

The most iconic rescue was **Virulazo** — born **Jorge Martín Orcaizaguirre** on October 10, 1926, in Haedo, Buenos Aires province, raised by his railroad-worker grandfather in La Tablada. He had worked from childhood: shoeshine boy, street vendor, slaughterhouse laborer. He got his nickname at 18 from a bocce ball term. Discovered by poet **Celedonio Flores** in the 1940s, he debuted at Café La Armonía on Corrientes Avenue, then danced at the legendary cabarets Chantecler and Tabarís. He won a radio tango competition in 1952. Then rock and roll and the military dictatorships destroyed his world. "We danced for pennies," he recalled. "Only Juan Carlos Copes and I endured."

When **Nelson Avila** tracked him down for the show, Virulazo was working at a **butcher shop**, wearing a blood-covered apron, weighing **126 kilograms** (278 pounds), age 57, with five children and six grandchildren. He had quit tango entirely and was supplementing his income running illegal gambling — *quiniela*, a numbers game. He refused to audition, saying people already knew he was great. When he finally danced for Segovia with his partner **Elvira Santamaría** (born 1929, his childhood sweetheart from La Tablada, reunited with him in 1959), Segovia "couldn't believe what he was seeing — such a man seemed to float, didn't touch the ground." Copes compared them to "Brutus and Olivia" — the Popeye characters — and meant it as the highest compliment.

**Juan Carlos Copes** (May 31, 1931 – January 16, 2021) and **María Nieves Rego** (born September 6, 1934, in Saavedra) were the established headliners. They had danced together since they were teenagers in working-class dance halls — he was 17, she was 14 — and had appeared on The Ed Sullivan Show four times. They had performed on Broadway before, in *New Faces of 1962*. But even they were, by 1983, relics of a vanished era. Copes served as both principal dancer and choreographer.

The other couples brought distinctive styles. **Nelson Avila and Nélida Rodríguez de Aure** were performing at the Michelangelo tango show in San Telmo, known for speed and acrobatic steps. **Héctor Mayoral and Elsa María** danced the simpler, graceful style of the crowded milonga floor. **Carlos and María Rivarola** were salon tango specialists — María recalled receiving Orezzoli's phone call offering a spot: "I almost fainted." **Mónica and Luciano Frías** completed the ensemble. Solo dancer **Cecilia Narova**, a trained vedette with classical and folk dance background, performed the show's dramatic set pieces.

The musical ensemble was anchored by the **Sexteto Mayor**, founded in 1973 by bandoneonists **José Libertella** and **Luis Stazo**. Segovia had approached them at the Trottoirs de Buenos Aires in 1981. Legendary pianist **Horacio Salgán** and guitarist **Ubaldo De Lío** completed the musical forces. Singers included **Roberto Goyeneche** (1926–1994), one of tango's greatest voices, **Raúl Lavié** (born August 22, 1937), whose signature number "Mi Noche Triste" — the famous Contursi tango that Gardel made famous in 1917 — he sang at every performance, and **Jovita Luna** and **María Graña**.

The show's concept was historical: it traced tango's evolution from its nineteenth-century origins through the Golden Age to Piazzolla's nuevo tango. Most dancers **did their own choreography**. For early tango history sections, Segovia and the dancers laid out historical still photographs on a table and reconstructed period dance styles from the images. Every performance opened and closed with **"Quejas de bandoneón,"** the signature piece of **Aníbal Troilo** (died 1975), in homage to the great bandoneonist Segovia had originally hoped to include.

The average age of the cast was **42 years**.

## A cargo plane, an Exocet, and a cold morning at Orly

The entire cast — approximately **60 people** including dancers, musicians, singers, and crew — flew to Paris on an **Argentine Air Force cargo plane** because they could not afford commercial flights. Argentina was in the final convulsions of its military dictatorship; **Raúl Alfonsín** would be elected president on **October 30, 1983**, just twelve days before the premiere, and would take office on December 10. The outgoing junta, a caretaker government installed after the Falklands War debacle, provided a small sum of money and authorization for the flight.

The plane was available because it "flew empty to Paris to pick up replacement equipment and materials" — the cast literally rode as cargo, without services. Dancers later recalled that the plane carried an **unexploded Exocet missile from the Falklands War** in its hold — a detail that is vivid but unverifiable anecdotal testimony. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: The Exocet missile detail comes from dancer recollections as reported in Spanish Wikipedia; it has not been independently confirmed.] **María Rivarola** and **Nélida Rodríguez** served as informal flight attendants. Four tons of set materials — backdrops depicting Buenos Aires street scenes, black suits, bandoneones, evening gowns, period costumes — were also transported. They landed at the **military zone of Orly airport** on a cold autumn morning.

The Argentine embassy's cultural counselor had pitched three cultural projects to Ambassador **Tomás de Anchorena**; the entire **eight-month cultural budget** of the embassy was spent on those six days at the Châtelet.

## Six nights that changed tango's history

The world premiere of *Tango Argentino* took place on or about **November 11, 1983**, at the **Théâtre du Châtelet** in Paris, as part of the twelfth **Festival d'Automne**. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Sources give the premiere date as November 10, 11, or 13; the run appears to have been November 11–17. The BnF catalogue and Festival d'Automne website support November 11.] The theatre seated approximately **2,000–3,000** people, and the run lasted six days.

[Cross-Reference → Key Events: Tango Argentino premiere dates]

Only **250 tickets** had been sold before opening night. Then it sold out. Then it sold out every night. On the first evening, the cast assumed the Argentine expat community had been mobilized, but when it sold out for six consecutive performances — with people standing outside the doors on the final night, listening and trying to peek in — they knew something extraordinary had happened.

The standing ovations were so prolonged and thunderous that Nelson Avila and Nélida Rodríguez stood at the front of the stage and **wept**. The French newspaper **Libération** wrote on November 14, 1983: *"Le tango est entré de nouveau dans Paris"* — "Tango has entered Paris again." **Vogue** covered the phenomenon. The demand was so overwhelming that the show returned for a **second run at the Châtelet in 1984**, followed by a tour of Italy — the Venice Biennale, Milan, Rome — and further European engagements.

What Parisian audiences saw was unlike anything in their experience. These were not young, athletic Broadway performers. They were middle-aged and older milongueros — some heavy, some weathered — dancing with an authenticity, intimacy, and improvisational fire that no ballroom competition had ever hinted at. A 278-pound man floated across the stage. Elderly couples communicated through an embrace that seemed telepathic. The music — played live by some of Argentina's finest musicians — was raw, passionate, and utterly different from the stiff rhythms of international ballroom tango. For European audiences who had known only the codified, head-snapping competition version, it was a revelation: *this* was tango.

## From the Châtelet to Broadway in eighteen months

The path to Broadway ran through an unplanned engagement. In **June 1985**, after a Kennedy Center booking fell through, the show played a **one-week run at New York City Center**. The *Washington Post* captured what happened next: "The show opened on a Tuesday. On Thursday, the papers dished out rave reviews. The rest of the week sold out." Demand was so extreme that the City Center's **upper balcony — closed for forty years — had to be reopened** to accommodate audiences.

Producer **Mel Howard**, who saw the Broadway potential, helped raise money alongside **Donald K. Donald**. After one preview on October 8, *Tango Argentino* opened at the **Mark Hellinger Theatre** on **October 9, 1985**. It ran for **198 performances**, closing on March 30, 1986, with a total gross of **$6,237,247** and an average ticket price of $33.89. The production performed entirely in Spanish.

[Cross-Reference → USA: Broadway Impact for what happened after Paris success]

The *New York Times* called it "the season's improbable hit," noting it "violated the Broadway dictum that musicals be elaborate, expensive, and stocked with supple young bodies. 'Tango Argentino' wasn't even in English." Journalist Samuel G. Freedman captured the cultural moment: "From dance schools to fashion shows to the media, Tango Argentino has stirred the metropolitan imagination. *Vanity Fair* magazine discoursed on tango history. *Women's Wear Daily* devoted a centerfold to tango togs. *The New Yorker* published a Tango Argentino cartoon. Tango mania, quoth *Vogue*."

The celebrity audience was staggering: **Frank Sinatra, Madonna, Brooke Astor, Mikhail Baryshnikov, Helen Gurley Brown, Henry Kissinger, Claus von Bülow, Jackie Onassis, Oscar de la Renta, Paloma Picasso**. Producers had initially booked only five weeks, worried the show might not last. **Bill Blass** promoted black velvet and silk crepe as "the tango look" in *W* and *Vogue*. **Luchow's restaurant** sold a $52.50 tango dinner-show package — 2,000 were sold in two weeks. **Roseland Dance Hall** hosted "the world's largest tango class" with three couples from the show.

The show received three **Tony Award nominations** at the 40th ceremony in 1986 — Best Musical, Best Direction (Segovia and Orezzoli), and Best Choreography (Copes, Nieves, and the full cast) — but won none. The Lincoln Center **Performing Arts Library filmed the show in 1986** to preserve it as a historical document.

Nelson Avila's commitment embodied the cast's spirit: before opening night on Broadway, he broke his arm, had doctors split the cast and hinge it at the elbow, and danced with a broken arm for the entire run.

## The revival ripples outward

The impact was immediate and measurable. **Dance instruction demand tripled overnight** in New York after the Broadway premiere. **Sandra Cameron**, owner of a Greenwich Village dance school, told reporters: "We're all tango daft." Cast members were asked to teach wherever the show was performed, creating nuclei of tango dancers in city after city. **Robert Duvall** took lessons from cast members. **Paul Pellicoro** provided a Manhattan dance center for performers to teach students between shows.

The show toured for approximately **fifteen years** with original cast members, visiting Japan in 1987, returning to the Châtelet in 1989, premiering in London at the **Aldwych Theatre in 1991** — where **Princess Diana** attended the première, went backstage to support an anti-AIDS campaign, and was so taken with tango that she asked dancers to teach her. It did not arrive in Argentina until **September 16, 1992**, at the Teatro Gran Rex in Buenos Aires — nine years after conquering Paris.

The 2011 reunion performance at the Obelisco in Buenos Aires, with surviving original artists including María Nieves and Raúl Lavié, confirmed the show's historical status. A **Broadway revival** ran at the Gershwin Theatre from November 16, 1999, to January 9, 2000, earning a Tony nomination for Best Revival.

The show spawned an industry. **Luis Bravo** debuted **Forever Tango** in 1990, taking three couples from the original cast. **Miguel Ángel Zotto** and **Milena Plebs**, who had joined the cast, founded their company **Tango x 2** in 1988. **Nora and Raul Dinzelbacher** settled in San Francisco in 1988 specifically to meet the demand for tango instruction created by the show's touring. **Daniel Trenner** began "Bridge to the Tango Tours" in the late 1980s, earning the nickname "the Johnny Appleseed of Tango" by bringing more than 1,500 visitors to Buenos Aires during the 1990s. **Richard Powers** started the Stanford Tango Weeks in 1991, running for seven years with 32 instructors.

In the 1990s, **Gustavo Naveira** — whose first tango teachers were **Rodolfo and Gloria Dinzel** from the Tango Argentino cast — and **Fabián Salas** founded the Tango Investigation Group in Buenos Aires, applying dance kinesiology principles to analyze the physics of tango movement and creating what became known as tango nuevo. Naveira insisted it was not a separate style: "Tango Nuevo is everything that has happened with the tango since the 1980s."

Dance critic **Laura Falcoff** wrote in 2003: "If today you can find milongas in Verona or Alaska... the person responsible is Tango Argentino." The Argentine Chamber of Deputies declared in 2011 that the show "was the starting point for the entire world wanting to dance, sing, teach, perform, or at least see and hear tango. As a consequence, young orchestras began to form and milongas were opened here and around the world."

On **September 30, 2009**, tango was inscribed on the **UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity** — a joint nomination by Argentina and Uruguay, decided in Abu Dhabi. It was a long-term downstream effect of the revival that began on a stage in Paris twenty-six years earlier.

## Why Paris, both times

The question of why Paris — not London, Berlin, or New York — served as tango's portal to the world on two occasions separated by seventy years demands more than a shrug. Borges, when asked why the Argentine tango was so popular in interwar Paris, answered with characteristic honesty: "I don't know." But the evidence points to several structural factors that made Paris uniquely receptive.

**The Buenos Aires–Paris axis was a century old.** After independence from Spain in 1816, Argentina's elite deliberately rejected Iberian influence and turned to France as a model of civilization. The ideological framework was established by **Domingo Sarmiento's** *Facundo* (1845), which privileged European — especially French — culture as the antidote to South American "barbarism." Buenos Aires was rebuilt using Haussmannian urban planning: wide boulevards, parks, French architectural styles designed by architects like **Pierre Benoit** and **Jules Dormal**. Avenida Alvear acquired over 100 Parisian-style palaces between 1890 and 1920. The city earned the nickname *La París de América*. In the other direction, the French coined the phrase *riche comme un Argentin* — "rich as an Argentine" — and in **1913, Argentina was richer than France or Germany** per capita. Wealthy Argentine families sent their sons to Paris routinely. The cultural pipeline was built into the infrastructure of both cities.

**Paris positioned itself as the arbiter of global culture** in ways no other city did. The pattern of validating non-European art forms was already well established before tango arrived. **Diaghilev's Ballets Russes**, which premiered in Paris in 1909, had demonstrated that a non-French art form could be consecrated as modern art through Parisian success. The tango craze "overlapped almost perfectly with the explosive first years of the Ballets Russes," as one Cambridge University scholar noted, "a dance troupe that originated, like the tango, outside France and, also like the tango, wowed Parisians with its exoticist displays of virtuosity and vibrant sexual excess." **Josephine Baker's** arrival in 1925 with *La Revue Nègre* at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées would extend the pattern. In each case, Paris served as the stage where non-European cultural forms acquired the legitimacy that their places of origin had denied them.

The mechanism was consistent: **the boomerang effect**. After Paris embraced tango in 1913, the Argentine elite reversed its rejection. *La Nación* had declared that year: "To accept it as ours, because it was so labeled in Paris, would be to fall into the most despicable servility." But by 1917, upper-class Argentines were learning to dance it. After Paris embraced Tango Argentino in 1983, the social dance form that had nearly died under the dictatorship was reborn worldwide and fed back to Buenos Aires, where milongas proliferated through the 1990s and beyond.

**French attitudes toward sensuality in art** made Paris more receptive than London or New York. The tango's sexual suggestiveness that provoked bans across American cities and British discomfort was, in Paris, part of its appeal. Almost all social dances fashionable in Paris between 1900 and 1914 came from the Americas — the cakewalk, the maxixe, the tango — reflecting what one scholar calls a sense that "the 'modern' twentieth-century body would be an American one." A French countess at a tango demonstration could quip "Don't you have to be lying down to dance that?" without the remark becoming a moral crisis. Tango was processed through an existing framework of Spanish exoticism — references to Bizet's *Carmen* were ubiquitous — that made it culturally legible to French audiences in a way it was not in Protestant northern Europe.

**In 1983, specific political conditions amplified Paris's receptivity.** President **François Mitterrand's** appointment of **Jack Lang** as Minister of Culture in 1981 had transformed French cultural policy. Lang **doubled the culture budget** in his first year — from 2.97 billion to 5.99 billion francs — and broadened the Ministry's scope to include popular culture and non-European performance traditions previously excluded. His 1982 attack on "American cultural imperialism" at a UNESCO conference positioned France as a champion of global cultural diversity, creating an ideological space that welcomed arts like tango. The **Festival d'Automne**, founded in 1972 and dedicated to international contemporary arts, provided the institutional platform. And the Trottoirs de Buenos Aires had been priming Parisian audiences for two years.

Segovia himself understood the dynamic perfectly. As he told *Le Temps*: **"Paris has always played a great role in the history of tango. At the beginning of the century, its Parisian success gave tango its letters of nobility in Argentina. Decades later, the history repeated itself with Tango Argentino."**

## What each wave meant for tango's survival

The two Parisian encounters bookend tango's century. The first made it global. The second saved it from death.

In 1913, tango arrived in Paris as a disreputable import from the slums of Buenos Aires and departed as a worldwide phenomenon that changed fashion, challenged morality, and gave the twentieth century one of its defining dances. The price of this transformation was sanitization: the raw *cortes y quebradas* of the arrabal were smoothed into the standardized steps of *le tango parisien*, and eventually into the stiff head-snaps of international ballroom competition. But without Paris, tango might never have left the Río de la Plata.

In 1983, tango arrived in Paris on a military cargo plane, performed by aging dancers whom their own country had forgotten, financed by a dead man's life insurance. It departed as the spark that ignited a global revival now encompassing **over sixty countries**, more than **300 weekly milongas in Buenos Aires alone** with **35,000 practitioners**, a UNESCO heritage designation, and a tango tourism industry generating hundreds of millions of dollars annually. The show proved that authentic tango — danced by real milongueros with real bodies and real histories — could move audiences more profoundly than any polished production. As Virulazo told the *Washington Post* during the Broadway run: "People have smothered us with attention. They treat us like artists."

Héctor Orezzoli died of a heart attack in Manhattan on **December 5, 1991**, at age 38, with much of his vision still unfolding. Virulazo died of lung cancer on **August 2, 1990**, at 63. Juan Carlos Copes died of COVID-19 on **January 16, 2021**. Claudio Segovia lived to see the full scope of what he had set in motion, passing on **December 21, 2025**, at 92 — honored as a *Ciudadano Ilustre* of Buenos Aires just days before his death. María Nieves, who recalled that "the first time I danced the tango, it entered my skin through my feet, passed from my skin to my blood and through my blood to my heart," celebrated her ninetieth birthday in September 2024.

The thread connecting 1913 to 1983 is Paris itself — a city that twice recognized in tango something the rest of the world had missed, and by recognizing it, made it impossible to ignore. Historian Matt K. Matsuda described tango's appeal in Belle Époque Paris as "a grasping for energies of revival in a degenerating old world." Seventy years later, with tango itself degenerating, Paris grasped again. Both times, the city's embrace was not entirely innocent — it was filtered through exoticism, through the French appetite for the exotic body as modern spectacle, through the power dynamics of a cultural capital conferring legitimacy on an art form from the periphery. But both times, the effect was salvation.

Carlos Gavito (1942–2005), who joined the show's later touring cast and became one of tango's most celebrated dancers, offered the simplest summary of what those milongueros did on stage in Paris and around the world: "We are painters. We paint the music with our feet." What Paris provided, twice, was the canvas.
