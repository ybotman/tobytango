## Summary 1: The Big Four and Pugliese's Survival
**Sources:** 104

Tango's near-extinction between 1955–1983 saw all three of the "Big Four" orchestra leaders besides Pugliese die just before or during military rule, with functioning orquestas típicas dropping from hundreds to single digits, while Pugliese—imprisoned by every successive regime—kept the art alive through his cooperative orchestra's defiant red carnation tradition, surviving long enough to see tango's global revival sparked by the 1983 Paris premiere of *Tango Argentino*.

## Summary 2: Not Banned But Crushed
**Sources:** 101

Argentine tango was never explicitly banned but was effectively crushed over nearly 30 years (1955–1983) through successive military regimes' curfews, gathering restrictions, censorship of lunfardo lyrics, and persecution of artists — with tango's deep association with Peronism and working-class culture making it politically suspect, destroying a mentor system overnight and creating a generational knowledge gap that took decades to recover from.

**Key facts to extract:**
- Never explicitly banned (important nuance)
- Mechanisms: curfews, gathering restrictions, censorship of lunfardo, artist persecution
- Peronism association = politically suspect
- Mentor system destroyed overnight
- Generational knowledge gap

## Summary 3: Converging Forces
**Sources:** 122

Tango's decline (1955–1983) was driven by converging forces: military dictatorships banned public gatherings (indirectly boosting rock over milongas), competing genres like rock nacional replaced tango as Argentina's most popular music, economics forced large orquestas típicas to downsize into duos and quintets, and Piazzolla's nuevo tango — which transformed tango into "music to listen to, not to dance to" — split the community into piazzolistas and antipiazzolistas while achieving international success that eluded him at home.

**Key facts to extract:**
- Gathering bans boosted rock (indoor/private) over milongas (public/social)
- Rock nacional replaced tango as most popular
- Economic pressure: orquestas → duos and quintets
- Piazzolla: "music to listen to, not to dance to"
- Community split: piazzolistas vs. antipiazzolistas
- Piazzolla's international success vs. domestic resistance

## Summary 4: Revival From Abroad
**Sources:** 130

Between 1955-1983, tango nearly died in Argentina as rock and roll, Rock Nacional, folklore revival, and cumbia captured youth culture, while the 1983 Paris premiere of Segovia and Orezzoli's "Tango Argentino" show—featuring forgotten milongueros like Virulazo (found working in a butcher shop)—paradoxically reignited the global tango revival from abroad before it returned home, where young Argentines only embraced their grandparents' dance after it was validated in Europe.

**Key facts to extract:**
- Competing genres: rock and roll, Rock Nacional, folklore revival, cumbia
- Virulazo found working in butcher shop
- Revival paradox: started abroad, returned home
- European validation needed for young Argentines to embrace tango
- "Grandparents' dance" perception

## Summary 5: Venue Collapse
**Sources:** 107

Buenos Aires tango dancing nearly went extinct from 1955–1983 as military repression, rock music, and social stigma caused virtually all milongueros to stop for 20–30 years, reducing hundreds of active venues to single digits and the dancer community to a tiny remnant before the 1983 revival.

**Key facts to extract:**
- Milongueros stopped for 20–30 years
- Venues: hundreds → single digits
- Social stigma as factor (not just political)
- "Tiny remnant" of dancer community

## Summary 6: Underground Survival and Revival Details
**Sources:** 84

Tango survived its "dark years" (1955-1983) through a tiny core of aging milongueros dancing in underground neighborhood venues, while military juntas devastated the dance through curfews, bans on gatherings of 3+ people, and selective enforcement that shut down milongas but allowed rock clubs — with the revival sparked by the November 1983 Paris premiere of "Tango Argentino" at the Théâtre du Châtelet, where dancers including 278-pound Virulazo (rescued from working in illegal gambling) flew on a military cargo plane and sold out every performance.
