# La Investigación: the decade that decoded tango

**Between roughly 1995 and 2005, a small group of young Argentine dancers did something no one had attempted in tango's century-long history: they systematically dismantled the dance's movement vocabulary, reduced it to a handful of structural principles, and rebuilt it as an analytical framework.** The result reshaped how tango is taught on every continent. Led by Gustavo Naveira and Fabián Salas, later joined by Mariano "Chicho" Frumboli, this informal research collective — known simply as *la investigación* (the investigation) — replaced rote memorization of hundreds of named sequences with a generative grammar of movement. Their work made tango explicable, exportable, and accessible to dancers worldwide. It also ignited the most heated debate in tango's modern era: whether the dance's essence can survive being understood.

The investigators themselves never claimed to invent new steps. They insisted every movement they catalogued came from the Golden Age milongueros they studied. What was new was the *methodology* — and the uncomfortable question it raised about what gets lost when intuition becomes analysis.

## The roots: Cochabamba 444 and the Buenos Aires crucible

The physical birthplace of the investigation was Buenos Aires's San Telmo neighborhood. **Club General Belgrano, at Cochabamba 444** — three blocks from Plaza Dorrego — had operated as a community social club for decades before becoming one of the city's first open tango practice sessions in the early 1990s.

**[HITM: The exact founding date of the Cochabamba sessions is uncertain. Sources place it between 1991 and 1993. The task framework suggests 1987, but no corroborating evidence for that earlier date was found. A CasaSanTelmo.com article referencing "eighteen years ago" — likely written around 2009–2011 — places the founding around 1991–1993. Tango Voice (2011) confirms "the early 1990s."]**

The format was deliberately informal. A **1.5-hour lesson** preceded a **4-hour practica** running until 2:30 AM on Thursday and Friday nights. Admission was *a la gorra* — pass the hat, donations only. The bar was staffed by club management committee members who cooked homemade empanadas; Teresa served moscato wine under chandeliers with yellow bulbs that gave, as one visitor wrote, "an aura of antiquity that takes you back to Buenos Aires in its Golden Age." Tables were large and communal. Strangers shared them. The *cabeceo* — the traditional nod-across-the-room to invite a dance — was not enforced.

The first professor at these sessions was **Pepito Avellaneda** himself, the legendary milonguero. Subsequent teachers included Efraín Ordoñez, Mingo Pugliese, and — critically — **Gustavo Naveira with his then-partner Olga Besio**. What Proyecto San Telmo later wrote about the venue captures its significance: "There learned and practiced a lot of the professionals who are dancing all around the world. There a lot of tango dance teachers investigated and perfected their techniques."

**[HITM: The relationship between the Cochabamba 444 practica and the formal "Tango Investigation Group" is not entirely clear from available sources. TangoForge does reference "the Cochabamba Investigation Group," suggesting the two were at least strongly associated.]**

## The core investigators and the milongueros who taught them

### Gustavo Naveira: the architect

Born **August 12, 1960**, in Comodoro Rivadavia, Patagonia, Gustavo Naveira came to tango almost genetically. His parents met dancing milonga; his grandfather danced tango. "When I was a child I was nurtured with tango," he told TodoTango. "Tango was in my life before my birth."

He played guitar from age 7 to 20, studying at the conservatory for **13 years** (per his own website; Wikipedia erroneously states 3). He studied folk dancing, enrolled at the National Dance School for classical and modern dance, and took a year of Spanish dance — "always out of curiosity rather than for a professional interest." He studied **economics at the University of Buenos Aires**, where a dance course revealed his affinity for movement. There he found his first practice partner, Olga Besio, through the university's folkloric ballet.

Around 1981, at roughly age 20, he began studying with **Rodolfo and Gloria Dinzel** — pioneering tango pedagogues who had developed their own systematic "Dinzel System" covering over 3,600 catalogued tango figures. "I was one of his best students," Naveira recalled, "and he took special notice of me and worked me very hard." The Dinzels' analytical temperament clearly influenced Naveira's later drive to systematize the dance.

From there, Naveira sought out the old masters directly. **Pepito Avellaneda**, the world-famous milonguero known for his creativity and exceptional milonga dancing, "gave me a lot," Naveira told TodoTango. He also studied with **Antonio Todaro** — perhaps the most creative tango dancer of his generation, whose school in Flores with Raúl Bravo produced half the professional dancers of the next generation — and **Gerardo Portalea**, the Villa Urquiza master who embodied elegance and musicality. Portalea's four principles — "personality, elegance, *compás*, and the final figure" — became raw material for Naveira's later analysis. **Virulazo** (Jorge Martín Orcaizaguirre), the 126-kilo milonguero who "seemed to float" despite his weight, also taught him.

By the mid-1980s, tango was experiencing a renaissance. Argentina had returned to democracy in 1983, and restrictions on social life eased. Naveira began teaching at the San Martín Cultural Center, where demand stunned him. "People who taught photography had five students, painting had ten, guitar had fifteen," he remembered. "People who taught tango dancing had fifty, seventy, a hundred students." One of his first classes drew **500 students packed into a tiny room**.

### Fabián Salas: the co-architect

Born in **1963 in La Plata**, Fabián Salas trained as a lawyer, moved to Buenos Aires in 1988, and by 1992 had become one of the first professional touring tango teachers in history. He performed with orchestras led by Berlingieri, Basso, Mores, and Sexteto Mayor, and joined the cast of "Tango Pasión." In **1995**, he and Naveira formally launched the **Tango Investigation Group** — a dedicated analytical research project that would later transform into the **Cosmotango** organization.

Salas was Naveira's essential intellectual partner. Where Naveira brought musical training and deep study of the old masters, Salas brought legal-analytical rigor and a performer's understanding of what could be taught. Together, they applied **principles of dance kinesiology from modern dance** to analyze the physics of tango movement. Their breakthrough was a shift in pedagogical philosophy: from teaching *what* to dance toward teaching *how* to dance.

"What did we invent? Nothing," Salas said in a 2001 interview. "Everything was already there. Even what in the beginning we called 'alterations' and then, later, 'changes in direction.' But we didn't invent them. They were there." What the investigators brought was a way of *explaining* what tangueros had been doing intuitively for a century.

### Chicho Frumboli: the prodigy

Mariano "Chicho" Frumboli, born **September 21, 1970**, came from an entirely different world. His father was a fine arts professor who listened to Piazzolla. Chicho got his first **drum set at age 13** and studied at the David Lebón Music School. He played drums for 14 years and studied theater for 8 years with actress Cristina Banegas. He hated tango.

"I was into rock-and-roll; I had long hair and played the drums," he recalled. "I hated tango, I didn't like it one bit, I couldn't even listen to it." Then in **1993**, a theater classmate dragged him to a class with **Ricardo Barrios and Victoria Vieyra**. "I embraced my dance partner for the first time and I got goose bumps." He never stopped.

Between 1994 and 1998, he studied intensively with Barrios and Vieyra while also working with **Tete Rusconi**, the legendary milonguero whose mantra was "Dance the music. Because the music is the tango." From Tete, Chicho absorbed what he later called a milonguero sensibility: "I was a milonguero. I came from studying with Tete and Maria, which was a style that took into great consideration the physical connection with the person you found yourself dancing with in the moment."

Victoria Vieyra then introduced Chicho to Naveira's world. The impact was instantaneous. "Gustavo had developed a structure to the dance that I had never seen before," Chicho remembered. "Gustavo and Fabián Salas had a practice group where they researched these new forms and they invited me to participate." The collision of Chicho's milonguero foundation, his drummer's musicality, and Naveira's analytical framework produced something extraordinary: **"In one month-and-a-half I learned what I hadn't learned in two years."**

Naveira's perspective confirmed the magnitude: "With Chicho, around this time, with only six months of study, he was amazing as a tango dancer. He started working with us — with Fabián and me — and we said, 'Well, we have to get him to really join us in this work, or else we have to break his legs!'" Naveira would later say simply: **"Chicho was absolutely the best dancer I ever saw."**

Other key participants in the investigation included **Luciana Valle** (part of the group 1995–1997, contributing to what became the "universal language of tango instruction"), **Norberto "El Pulpo" Esbrés** (who developed the concept of suspension and extrapolated classical barrida vocabulary up the leg), and **Giselle Anne**, a classical ballet graduate of the National Dance School who brought academic analytical rigor. Women with formal dance training played a critical role: as Carolyn Merritt documented, "female tangueras with backgrounds in classical ballet, modern dance, and other dance forms could identify and verbalize the mechanics within the embrace."

## The analytical framework: three steps to explain everything

The investigation's central achievement was reducing tango's seemingly infinite variety to a small number of structural principles. Before the 1990s, tango pedagogy relied on rote imitation. Mingo Pugliese's syllabus alone catalogued **more than 900 different moves**. Students memorized sequences without understanding why movements worked.

### Every step is one of three

The investigators demonstrated that **all tango movement reduces to three fundamental step types:**

- **Cruce adelante** (front cross) — the foot crosses in front of the body's midline
- **Apertura** (open/side step) — the foot steps to the side
- **Cruce atrás** (back cross) — the foot crosses behind the body's midline

Fabián Salas described the breakthrough: "We came up reading the Tango only through: Open, Back, and Front steps and that's it. To 2 sides, that makes 6 — that's all we have in Argentine Tango: **6 different steps**."

This was revolutionary because it replaced catalogues of named figures with a **generative grammar**. If you understood these three step types, combined for both dancers, in both systems (parallel and crossed), on either foot, you could derive every possible tango figure — not by memorization but by structural logic. A 2018 paper in the *Journal of Mathematics and the Arts* independently validated the framework, confirming that "only four variables are required to explain the diversity of tango figures: step type, step direction, step style, and intrusion mode."

### The turn code (*código de giro*)

The investigation's second breakthrough was recognizing that **the turn is the fundamental structure of all tango**. "One day we came up with this realization that the fundamental structure in Argentine Tango was the turn," Salas recounted. "But you can't see it until you look for it."

The walking code (*código de marcha*) — the basic linear sequence predating tango itself — follows a predictable pattern: apertura, cruce atrás, apertura, cruce adelante, repeating. The **turn code** (*código de giro*) applies this same sequence to circular trajectories, inserting pivots. One complete turn conventionally takes four steps: **cruce atrás → apertura → cruce adelante → pivot → apertura → pivot**. The half-turn (*medio giro*) — the three consecutive steps without pivots: cruce atrás → apertura → cruce adelante — became a foundational teaching tool because of its simplicity.

The code can be entered from any point in the sequence. As long as the direction of the turn is maintained, the same leg always crosses and the other always opens. This single principle explains all turns in tango.

### *Alteraciones* and *cambios de dirección*

Initially, the investigators labeled certain "strange" sequences *alteraciones* — moments where a front cross followed a back cross, or an apertura followed another apertura, breaking the expected turn code order. They later realized these were simply **changes of direction of the turn**, not anomalies. Salas explained on his website that they then reserved the term *alteración* for structural breaks in the walking code itself, renaming the directional shifts *cambios de dirección*. By the time they clarified the terminology, however, the word *alteraciones* had spread among teachers worldwide "without knowing its exact meaning or structure."

### Parallel and crossed system

The terminology for the stepping relationship between partners went through a documented evolution. Early on, the investigators used **"even/uneven"** (*par/impar*). By the mid-1990s, this became **"parallel/crossed"** and later **"normal/crossed."** In parallel system, partners mirror each other (leader's right with follower's left). In crossed system, they step simultaneously on the same side. The *contrapaso* — a weight change by one partner without the other — toggles between systems.

This vocabulary matters because it named something that had existed intuitively for decades but lacked description, giving teachers worldwide a shared language for something previously communicated only through physical demonstration.

### Axis analysis and the off-axis vocabulary

The investigators systematized the concept of axis — the dancer's relationship to their own center of balance — into a framework distinguishing **on-axis** work (body upright, balance self-contained) from **off-axis** work (center of balance shared between partners).

They catalogued three categories of off-axis movement:

- ***Volcada*** (from *volcar*, to tip over): the follower tilts inward toward the leader, transferring weight onto the partner during a dynamic inward-falling movement
- ***Colgada*** (from *colgar*, to hang): the follower displaces outward, hanging weight away from the leader, counterbalanced on a shared axis
- ***Soltada*** (from *soltar*, to release): the partners temporarily disconnect, executing independent movements before reconnecting

**Systematic off-axis work was the investigation's most visibly original contribution.** While some degree of leaning existed in earlier tango — Carlos Gavito's *el puente*, Ricardo Vidort's subtle leans — no one had previously mapped the full structural space of off-axis possibilities. Salas has been called "the Father of Volcadas and Colgadas" for this reason. As Tango Voice noted: "What tango nuevo has contributed to tango in terms of original movements have been off-axis movements. One of the defining characteristics of tango nuevo is the exploration of shifts of axis."

### Leading from the center

The investigation's final structural insight was that **the lead originates from the torso**, making the style of embrace structurally irrelevant. If communication between dancers flows through the solar plexus — the center — then the same movements can be executed in close embrace, open embrace, or without arms at all. This principle drew from **Contact Improvisation**, the contemporary dance form developed by Steve Paxton in the 1970s, which emphasizes shared weight and rolling points of contact.

Naveira addressed this directly in his 2009 essay: "There has been much recent discussion on the problem of the embrace, dividing the dance into open or closed style, which is also a matter of great confusion. **This simple and clumsy division between open and closed is often used by those who try to deny the evolution of the dance, to disguise their own lack of knowledge.** Today it is perfectly clear that the distances in the dance have a much greater complexity than a simple open or closed."

## The "nuevo" question: what the investigators actually said

Perhaps no term in modern tango generates more confusion than *tango nuevo*. The investigators themselves spent years trying to correct a misunderstanding they considered fundamental.

In **July 2009**, Naveira published an essay titled "New Tango" in Virginia Gift's book *Tango, A History of Obsession*. His position was unequivocal:

> "There is great confusion on the question of the way of dancing the tango: call it technique, form, or style. **The term tango nuevo is used to refer to a style of dancing, which is an error.** In reality, Tango Nuevo is everything that has happened with the tango since the 1980s. It is not a question of a style."

> "Tango Nuevo is not one more style; it is simply that tango dancing is growing, improving, developing, enriching itself, and in that sense we are moving toward a new dimension in tango dancing."

Chicho was blunter. In a **March 2008 interview** with the Australian Tango Dance Research Circle, he traced the label to commercial interests: "Tango Nuevo does exist, but it has for a very long time... Copes was dancing a new tango, Miguel Ángel Zotto had a new tango. **To think that 'Tango Nuevo' is something that occurred only 10 years ago is a commercial exploitation that we owe to the festival organizers.** I don't think I am doing 'Tango Nuevo.' I feel that I am dancing tango."

The investigators' position can be summarized in three propositions. **What was new:** the analytical methodology itself, systematic off-axis exploration, and the demonstration that embrace style is structurally irrelevant. **What was not new:** the movements themselves, all derived from Golden Age milongueros. As Naveira and Salas insisted to researcher Carolyn Merritt: "They did not introduce any new steps into the dance's vocabulary. What they did was mine the annals of the dance's history to revive movements that had fallen out of practice." And **what the label actually means:** not a style but an era. As Naveira told Merritt, the term means "nothing, absolutely nothing" to him — "What we have is a new situation in the world relative to tango. This new situation is much more interesting than the tiny idea of a new style."

## Practica X and the movement's peak

By 2004, the investigation's influence had reshaped Buenos Aires's tango geography. The old milonga codes — gendered seating, *cabeceo*, strict dress, traditional tandas with cortinas — were being challenged by a generation raised on the investigators' framework.

**Practica X**, founded in **2004** by **Pablo Inza, Raúl Masciocchi, and Gabriel Bortnik**, became the physical symbol of this peak. Located at **Sitio Palermo**, a large hall in the Palermo Hollywood neighborhood, it offered enough space that dancing in a *ronda* (the counterclockwise line of dance) was not obligatory. TangoForge called it "the mecca of Nuevo Tango." It was among the busiest practicas in Buenos Aires history.

**[HITM: Contrary to what some sources suggest, Practica X was NOT located at Club Villa Malcolm. Villa Malcolm (Córdoba 5064, Villa Crespo) — described in a January 2009 blog as "the temple of the tango nuevo movement" — hosted separate events including El Motivo practica (organized by Luciana Valle) and TangoCool. Both venues were central to the nuevo scene, but they were distinct locations.]**

The codes at Practica X were deliberately relaxed: no *cabeceo*, no gender-segregated seating, casual dress, exhibitions welcomed. A 1.5-hour lesson preceded the 4-hour practica. Music was organized into tandas but without cortinas — and roughly **90% of the recorded music was classic Golden Age tango**, puncturing the assumption that *nuevo* dancers reject traditional music. What distinguished the practica was not the music but the movement vocabulary and the social openness.

Meanwhile, the **CITA festival** (Congreso Internacional de Tango Argentino), co-founded by Fabián Salas in **1999**, became the investigation movement's primary international platform. Running annually in Buenos Aires, typically in March, CITA grew into what its organizers called "the world's longest-running annual tango event," featuring 90-plus classes, 50-plus performances, and 70-plus maestros. Still active as of 2026, it bridged the Buenos Aires scene and the global tango community.

The international festival circuit — exploding across Europe and the Americas from the early 2000s — carried the investigation's methodology worldwide. Chicho toured Europe from the late 1990s, first with Victoria Vieyra, teaching the nuevo approach in France and Belgium. Naveira's and Salas's constant international travel meant, as Chicho ruefully noted, that he "stayed behind with all of this information, practicing, and waiting for them to return." The 2001 Argentine economic crisis, which devalued the peso, opened Buenos Aires to unprecedented foreign tango tourism, accelerating the exchange. Non-Argentine dancers who learned the framework at festivals brought it home to Berlin, New York, Portland, and dozens of other cities.

## The retrenchment: when the investigators stepped back

The reversal came faster than anyone expected. According to anthropologist **Carolyn Merritt**, whose 2012 book *Tango Nuevo* (University Press of Florida) documented the movement through fieldwork conducted between 2005 and 2010, **between August 2008 and November 2009, the youth tango scene in Buenos Aires made a dramatic retrenchment to close embrace and traditional music.** By September 2011, Practica X — the mecca — was gone.

The causes were multiple. The **2008 global financial crisis** likely played a role, though this connection is not explicitly documented in available sources. Market saturation and the dilution of the teaching created what Chicho called a generation of technically capable but emotionally disconnected dancers. And the founders themselves grew disillusioned with what their work had produced.

Chicho's landmark interview with **Milena Plebs in El Tangauta magazine (No. 182, December 2009)** stands as the investigation movement's most devastating self-critique. His words are worth quoting at length:

> "They are completely lost! I learnt with the last great milongueros, I took the information directly from them. Those who are starting to dance don't have this experience, they learn instead from an intermediate generation that I am a part of; we are a nexus between these old dancers and those who are younger. **The problem is that we missed something in the teaching, I take total responsibility, and other colleagues should do so as well. I can't pass on what I have learned.** I was crazy about creating, because I saw a new vein in the evolution of the movement. I threw myself into that, and I lost the way to be able to pass on the tango essence that I have very much inside."

> "Currently, not only has the essence been lost but the weight of the dance as well, its density and importance. To me, this new tango lost a bit of the respect for what tango is."

> "Before, people worked with precision and a particular aesthetic, in a functional and mechanical way that gave it a form, and a style. Making a movement or taking a step implied an expression of the entire body."

Asked whether the flavor of the old milongueros' dancing — "the indescribable flavor in the way they moved" — was lost, Chicho answered simply: "Yes."

He described the commercial corruption: "Today you do a *volcada* and a *colgada* and it is the same because they are there, commercially speaking, in the same package. People do a *volcada* because it's more eye-catching. They are not going to make a *sandwichito* to enjoy that moment, but whatever shows them more and better."

And then the confession that haunts the movement: **"Today I'm a milonguero again. I'm against the people who do not *cabecear*, who don't have codes or respect. The value of tango has been diluted."**

Practica X moved from spacious Sitio Palermo to the smaller Viejo Correo in Caballito in August 2010. By December 2009, even at the mecca, **80% of dancers were already dancing close-embrace milonguero style**, with only 20% dancing nuevo. The label had become something the founders rejected and the dancers abandoned — while the framework itself had already been absorbed, invisibly, into how nearly everyone was learning to dance.

## The paradox of essence and analysis

The investigation's legacy is simultaneously enormous and unresolved. Its framework — the three fundamental steps, the turn code, parallel and crossed systems, the axis vocabulary — is now embedded in tango teaching worldwide, used by traditional and contemporary teachers alike. As one festival program noted: "Gustavo is probably the teacher who has influenced tango-dance teaching the most, in all its styles and worldwide. He managed to clarify concepts about the structure of the dance."

Naveira's own verdict, from his famous quote, captures both the ambition and the gap: **"Something that tango always lacked is an intellectual analysis of how it works, how it is built and how we take part in that process. Now we have a fairly wide knowledge of how it is."** Yet when pressed on what tango *is* beyond structure, he acknowledged the limits: "It is very easy to say 'tango is a feeling,' because no one really knows what a feeling is. The 'feeling,' the 'energy,' the 'passion,' all those things are difficult to understand."

The old milongueros Pepito Avellaneda's distinction remains: "There are those who learn to make money. And others that learn because they like dancing." Tete Rusconi's letter, distributed in milongas before his death in January 2010, carried the same warning: "I believe that many are disguising tango as something that it isn't. Tango is music, and it doesn't begin with steps."

The traditionalist critique — that the investigation produced "acrobatics, not tango," was "dangerous on social floors," and disconnected the dance from its music and embrace — was never entirely wrong. As Tango Voice documented, "the transference of tango nuevo to the pista of the milonga has been a major cause of conflict, because tango nuevo and tango de salón abide by different concepts of the use of space." Ricardo Vidort's dictum remains provocative: "Milongueros can perform on stage, but stage performers cannot dance in the milonga."

Yet the framework works. It made tango explicable to dancers from other disciplines. It created a shared vocabulary across continents and languages. It opened a dance that had been transmitted through decades of milonga attendance to anyone willing to study its logic. Naveira's children — Ariadna Naveira and Federico Naveira — now carry the framework forward on the international festival circuit. Fabián Salas still runs CITA. Chicho, back in close embrace, still dances.

The deepest lesson of *la investigación* may be this: **it proved that tango's structure could be decoded, while simultaneously demonstrating that the thing that makes tango *tango* — the thing Chicho said he "can't pass on" — lives somewhere the analysis cannot reach.** The framework explains how the dance works. What the milongueros had was why it matters.

---

## Conclusion

The Tango Investigation Era left an analytical architecture that now underlies virtually all professional tango instruction, even among teachers who would never identify as "nuevo." The three fundamental steps, the turn code, the parallel/crossed terminology, and the axis vocabulary have become the grammar through which tango is discussed and transmitted globally. This was the investigators' achievement, and it was substantial.

The paradox is that the movement's most eloquent voices eventually turned against its consequences. Chicho's confession — "I can't pass on what I have learned" — acknowledges a failure not of intellect but of transmission: that the essence absorbed from the last great milongueros could not survive decomposition into teachable units. Naveira's insistence that *tango nuevo* describes an era, not a style, was both technically correct and practically futile; the world had already decided what the label meant.

What endures is the framework itself — a tool, not a style. Like music theory, it explains without replacing. It makes the dance's logic visible without exhausting its meaning. The investigators decoded tango's body. Its soul remains, as Pepito Avellaneda would have said, a matter of whether you learned because you liked dancing.

**Volcada**: Off-axis movement where the follower tilts inward toward the leader, transferring weight in a dynamic inward-falling displacement. From *volcar* (to tip over). Systematized by the Investigation Group; Fabián Salas sometimes credited as originator of systematic exploration.

**Colgada**: Off-axis movement where the follower displaces outward, hanging weight away from the leader, counterbalanced on a shared axis. From *colgar* (to hang). Categories include side, back, circular, and front colgadas.

**Soltada**: Movement where partners temporarily disconnect, executing independent movements before reconnecting. From *soltar* (to release). Includes arm wraps, partial separations, and complete disconnections.

**Código de giro** (Turn Code): The fundamental circular structure of tango identified by the Investigation Group. Sequence: cruce atrás → apertura → cruce adelante (with pivots inserted for full turns). Can be entered from any point. Discovered as "the fundamental structure of all tango" by Naveira and Salas.
