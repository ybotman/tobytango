# The night America discovered the real tango

On the evening of October 9, 1985, a show opened at the Mark Hellinger Theatre on Broadway that would, within a decade, seed a new dance culture across the United States. **"Tango Argentino" was not supposed to be a hit.** It had one set, four bandoneóns, no English dialogue, and a cast whose average age was 42 — including a 58-year-old former butcher with a 38-inch waistline. The producers booked it for five weeks. It ran for 198 performances, earned three Tony nominations, and grossed $6.2 million. More importantly, it shattered a seventy-year misconception. For the first time, Americans saw what Argentine tango actually looked like — and it bore almost no resemblance to the stiff, rose-in-teeth parody they knew from ballroom studios and Hollywood films. What followed was a grassroots cultural transplantation unlike anything in American dance history: a decade in which a handful of pioneers — teachers, organizers, pilgrims — carried an endangered Argentine art form into living rooms, Greek restaurants, and university ballrooms from Manhattan to Portland, building communities one milonga at a time.

## A show that broke every Broadway rule

"Tango Argentino" was conceived in 1974 by two Argentine set designers, **Claudio Segovia** and **Héctor Orezzoli**, who wanted to stage an authentic portrait of tango's world — not a sanitized export but the real dance as it lived in the barrios of Buenos Aires. The show took nearly a decade to assemble. Juan Carlos Copes, the most famous tango dancer alive, initially thought the idea was "crazy," doubting that ego-driven tango artists could collaborate. But Segovia was relentless, and gradually he recruited a cast that represented the full spectrum of Argentine tango: the elegant stage virtuosity of **Copes and María Nieves**, who had been partners since adolescence in the 1940s; the acrobatic speed of **Nelson Avila and Nélida Rodriguez de Aure**; the scholarly precision of **Gloria and Rodolfo Dinzel**; the salon authenticity of **Héctor and Elsa María Mayoral** with their small, playful steps; and the raw milonguero truth of **Virulazo** (Jorge Martín Orcaizaguirre) and **Elvira Santamaría**, the oldest couple in the cast and married for 26 years. Music was provided by **Sexteto Mayor**, the legendary ensemble led by José Libertella and Luis Stazo, with arrangements by the great Horacio Salgán. Every performance opened and closed with "Quejas de bandoneón" — the signature piece of Aníbal Troilo, who had died in 1975.

The show premiered at the **Théâtre du Châtelet** in Paris on November 11, 1983, during the Festival d'Automne. The entire cast had flown from Buenos Aires on an Argentine Air Force cargo plane — literally as cargo, with no passenger services. The first performance received multiple standing ovations. Nelson and Nélida wept at the front of the stage after their solo, which only intensified the crowd. Every show sold out. The production toured Italy in 1984, returned to Paris, then moved through Canada and the United States in 1985, including a crucial one-week engagement at New York's City Center in June that happened almost by accident — a Kennedy Center booking had fallen through. The City Center opened on a Tuesday. By Thursday, the papers had published rave reviews. The rest of the week sold out. As publicist Marilyn Levine described it: "It was, like, mania, it caught on so fast. The crowd that came was the most 'in' people. The Paloma Picassos. Jackie Onassis, Oscar de la Renta. These people sniff out a trend."

That June buzz led to the Broadway opening in October. Critics were astonished. The New York Times called it "the unlikeliest hit of the season," noting that the show "violated the Broadway dictum that musicals be elaborate, expensive, and stocked with supple young bodies." Samuel G. Freedman wrote that "what *The Boy Friend* did for the Charleston and the flapper's chemise, *Tango Argentino* has done for slit skirts, silk scarves and male dominance, at least on the dance floor." The Washington Post reported that in three months, at roughly 80% capacity, **more than 140,000 people** saw the show. Frank Sinatra attended. Madonna attended. Mikhail Baryshnikov, watching from the audience, said tango was "a dance of indescribable beauty, with the finesse of ballet and the fire of flamenco." Robert Duvall came, fell in love with the dance, and would become a lifelong devotee who eventually made his own tango film.

[Cross-Reference → Key Events: Tango Argentino Broadway opening]

The show received **three Tony Award nominations** for the 1985–86 season: Best Musical, Best Choreography (for Copes and the entire dance ensemble), and Best Direction of a Musical (Segovia and Orezzoli). It won none, losing to *The Mystery of Edwin Drood*. But its cultural impact dwarfed any award. Vanity Fair discoursed on tango history. Women's Wear Daily devoted a centerfold to tango fashion. Vogue declared "Tango mania." Bill Blass promoted black velvet as "the tango look." Luchow's restaurant sold 2,000 dinner-and-dancing packages in two weeks. The Limelight nightclub hosted an "Homage to Tango Argentino" that drew 500 people. Roseland Dance Hall staged "the world's largest tango class" with three couples from the show. And in Greenwich Village, Sandra Cameron's dance school saw tango enrollment **triple overnight** — from roughly 40 students to 124.

What specifically shocked American audiences was the gulf between what they saw on stage and everything they thought they knew about tango. **Ballroom tango**, the standardized competition form taught in American dance studios since the 1920s, had stripped the Argentine original of its improvisation, its intimate embrace, and its emotional vocabulary. Americans knew a stiff-framed, head-snapping caricature. What they saw at the Mark Hellinger was the opposite: bodies pressed chest-to-chest in the *abrazo*, moving with fierce improvisational spontaneity, each couple dancing in a completely different style. The Mayorals used tiny steps suited to crowded milonga floors. Virulazo, overweight and magnetic, embodied a working-class authenticity that made audience members think, "I can do that." And the range of ages and bodies — the explicit rejection of ballroom's polished youth — was itself a revelation. As co-creator Orezzoli told the Times: "I have people come up to me and say, 'I have dreamed all my life of the tango.' It is like they have seen God."

## Broadway's aftershock and the first teachers

The immediate aftermath of the Broadway opening created a demand for Argentine tango instruction that could not be met, because almost no one in the United States knew how to teach it. The few who did — and the cast members who stepped into the vacuum — became the founding generation of American tango.

**Paul Pellicoro**, who had founded his DanceSport studio in New York in 1985, provided a critical nexus. An original Latin-Hustle street dancer from the late 1970s who had crossed into ballroom, Pellicoro made his space available for the Broadway cast to teach students directly. This was where many of the first American tango lessons occurred — stage performers from Buenos Aires transmitting their art to New Yorkers hungry for the real thing. Fran Chesleigh, who would teach tango in New York for more than 30 years, later recalled: "In 1986, when I was teaching social dance at a New York studio, we hired members of the cast of the seminal Broadway show, Tango Argentino, to introduce us to this magnificent dance." He studied with Copes, Rodolfo Dinzel, Virulazo, and the Arquimbaus, among others. Pellicoro himself would rise to national fame in 1992 when he choreographed **Al Pacino's iconic tango scene in *Scent of a Woman*** — a moment that, alongside the Broadway show, did more than perhaps anything else to embed Argentine tango in American popular consciousness.

Simultaneously, **Danel and Maria Bastone** were teaching tango in New York, running events under the name "Bailemos Tango" and publishing a newsletter called "Tango Times." They hosted events at the 92nd Street Y and organized milongas that became essential parts of the emerging New York scene. Their influence on American tango pedagogy was significant — several early West Coast teachers, including Michael Walker and Luren Bellucci in Los Angeles, credited exposure to Danel and Maria with shifting their understanding from show tango toward social tango.

The Broadway show toured extensively after closing in March 1986. A **second cast** was recruited in Buenos Aires for a road show planned across twelve US cities. The production played the **Pantages Theater in Hollywood** in summer 1986, creating what one chronicler called "the LA Tango Explosion." Eduardo and Gloria Arquimbau appeared on *The Merv Griffin Show*. The Dinzels, the Mayorals, Eduardo and Gloria, and others taught enormous classes at venues including the Hollywood Roosevelt Hotel. Linda Valentino's account of the era describes hundreds of people attending. But the mania was fragile. When the show left Los Angeles, interest collapsed with shocking speed. By the end of 1986, as early community member Paul Palmintere recalled, "there were only about 6 or 7 of us left."

The show continued internationally — Japan in 1987, Europe in 1988, the Aldwych Theatre in London in 1991 (earning an Olivier Award nomination), and finally Buenos Aires in 1992, where it played the Teatro Gran Rex and Teatro Lola Membrives. **The show did not debut in its own country until seven years after Broadway**, a fact that reflected deep Argentine ambivalence about tango's cultural status. Orezzoli had refused to stage it at the Teatro Colón, saying: "Their values really haven't changed toward the tango or anything that is authentic or identifies us."

## New York plants the first roots

New York's tango community grew slowly but steadily through the late 1980s and into the 1990s, anchored by multiple overlapping scenes. Pellicoro's **DanceSport** — a 15,000-square-foot studio that eventually settled in Midtown Manhattan near the Empire State Building — hosted regular Sunday milongas and attracted celebrity students including Robert De Niro, Mira Sorvino, Richard Gere, and Jennifer Aniston. The **Sandra Cameron Dance Center** at 440 Lafayette Street in Greenwich Village became one of the first studios to develop a dedicated Argentine tango curriculum; Jeni Breen and Sandra Cameron built what became "New York City's first program dedicated to teaching classes in Argentine tango," roughly by the mid-1990s.

But the venue that became iconic was the **Lafayette Grill**, a Greek restaurant in Manhattan that transformed into an Argentine tango cultural center on Saturday nights. Dr. Susan Kavaler-Adler's book *Saturday Nights at Lafayette Grill* documents this milonga and its community in rich detail. The Lafayette Grill was part of what became a tango marathon circuit: Friday milonga at La Belle Epoque, Saturday afternoon classes and practice, tango in Central Park, Saturday night at Lafayette Grill, Danel and Maria's milonga running until early morning, Sunday classes, and Sunday milonga at DanceSport. As organizer Jack Hanley described it, these were "strange, magical places that only happen in New York City."

**Daniel Trenner** arrived in New York in the late 1980s carrying something none of the Broadway teachers could offer — direct experience with the social tango of Buenos Aires milongas, the improvisational tradition that existed in a completely different universe from stage performance. Together with **Rebecca Shulman**, whom he began working with in 1991, Trenner co-created **Dance Manhattan's tango program** and became the city's most important bridge between Buenos Aires and the American tango movement. Shulman, a classically trained ballet dancer who had studied contact improvisation, made her first trip to Buenos Aires in 1992 and studied with the Argentine masters: Juan Bruno, Mingo and Esther Pugliese, Gustavo Naveira, Tete Rusconi, Antonio Todaro, and Graciela Gonzalez. She would teach every level of tango at Dance Manhattan from 1994 to 2014, and more than a dozen of New York's subsequent tango teachers would be her former students.

## San Francisco becomes the proving ground

If New York was where American tango began, the San Francisco Bay Area was where it first achieved critical mass. The story starts with a cruise ship. In 1986, Argentine dancers **Nora and Raul Dinzelbacher** visited San Francisco while performing tango professionally aboard a cruise liner. They taught classes and attracted students, including **Al and Barbara Garvey** of Fairfax, California. The Garveys also studied with **Jorge and Rosa Ledesma** from Quilmes — all in the choreographed show-tango style that was the only Argentine tango available in the United States at the time.

Then, in **1987, the Garveys traveled to Buenos Aires** — one of the first American couples to make the pilgrimage. What they found at **Centro Akarense**, a large milonga filled with older dancers in the neighborhood of Villa Urquiza, transformed their understanding. The tango they witnessed was nothing like the show choreography they had learned. It was danced in close embrace, improvised, subtle, and deeply social. Barbara Garvey later noted that "a very simple form of close embrace was prevalent in the center" of Buenos Aires. They returned to California and began organizing milongas around the Bay Area, eventually **co-founding the Bay Area Argentine Tango Association (BAATA)** and publishing a journal that became one of the first regular tango publications in the country.

The Dinzelbachers **settled permanently in San Francisco in 1988**, responding to demand for Argentine tango teachers in the wake of "Tango Argentino's" West Coast tour. They taught a core group of students who would themselves become teachers: **Polo Talnir, Jorge Allende**, and others. A critical detail about Nora Dinzelbacher: she held a Master of Dance degree from the National School of Dance in Buenos Aires, had performed with the Argentina Folk Ballet, and her husband Raul shared the surname "Dinzelbacher" with Rodolfo Dinzel from the Broadway cast — strongly suggesting a family connection, though the exact relationship has not been documented. In 1989, **Richard Powers**, a dance instructor at Stanford University, invited the Dinzelbachers to teach Argentine tango at a weeklong dance festival in Cincinnati. The following year, Powers moved the festival to Stanford. Tragedy struck: **Raul Dinzelbacher collapsed and died at age 40** at the end of the third day. She asked student George Guim to become her assistant and, in 1991, helped Powers transform the event into the **Stanford Tango Weeks** — the first major annual tango festival in the United States.

The Stanford Tango Weeks ran for nine years (1991–1997) at Roble Dance Hall, featuring **32 instructors** over their history, including Juan Carlos Copes, Pablo Veron, Nito García and Elba Sottile, Graciela Gonzalez, Esther and Pablo Pugliese, Daniel Trenner and Rebecca Shulman, and Michael Walker and Luren Bellucci. **"The Stanford Tango Weeks have been credited by tango dancers and teachers for being an essential catalyst for the growth of Argentine Tango in the United States."** Many of the people who later founded tango communities and festivals across the country — Janis Kenyon in Chicago, Clay Nelson in Portland, Tom Stermitz in Denver — first encountered serious tango instruction at Stanford.

The Bay Area's tango explosion arrived in 1994. **Forever Tango**, the show created by Luis Bravo in 1990 using three couples from "Tango Argentino" with Gloria and Eduardo Arquimbau as artistic directors, reopened after a hiatus and played an astonishing **92-week run** at San Francisco's Theatre on the Square. The effect on the local community was seismic. Barbara Garvey's BAATA mailing list grew from **400 to 1,400 dancers** in the first half of 1994 alone. Regional milongas multiplied from **three per month to thirty**. Early venues included the **Verdi Club**, **Ruvano's**, and **Broadway Studios**; in 1996, Julian Ramil started the **El Valenciano** milonga on Tuesday nights, initially as an after-party for the Verdi Club, which became one of San Francisco's most famous tango venues. By the late 1990s, a milonga could be found on any night of the week.

## Los Angeles: boom, bust, and slow rebuild

Los Angeles followed a more volatile trajectory. **Orlando Paiva Sr.**, born December 1, 1935, in Rosario, Argentina, was arguably the most important early figure. A notable cabaret performer who had danced with the orchestras of Osvaldo Pugliese and Miguel Caló and was friends with Virulazo, Paiva immigrated to the United States in the early 1970s, settling in El Monte, California, where he worked as a machinist. He was already offering tango classes in Los Angeles when "Tango Argentino" hit Broadway. When the show toured to the **Pantages Theater** in summer 1986, the resulting "Tangomania" briefly revived interest — but when the show departed, the community nearly evaporated.

Paiva delayed his return to Argentina by two years to sustain what remained. He taught Friday nights at **Dance Center West** in West LA (owned by Carol Montez) and Sunday afternoons at **Nora's Place** in North Hollywood. Key allies emerged: **Paul Palmintere** became his assistant and translator; actress **Miranda Garrison** (later of *Dirty Dancing*) partnered with him for demonstrations; and **Loreen Arbus**, a Hollywood television producer, used her entertainment-industry connections to promote tango, arranging performances on the Wil Shriner show and inviting Hollywood luminaries to tango events. Robert Duvall befriended Paiva and would later cast him in the 2003 film *Assassination Tango*.

In 1987, studio owner **Peri Rogovin** gave tango a professional platform at her Studio of the Performing Arts (later Third Street Dance), hosting Paiva and promoting him to the entertainment community. That summer, Paiva traveled to Japan, teaching classes of 300 people in Tokyo, Osaka, and Kobe. But at the end of 1988, he returned to his family in Rosario. He was described as "arguably the best-loved and best-remembered of all tango instructors who taught in Los Angeles" — characterized by impeccable footwork, sweetness, and serene elegance. When he left, **Michael Walker and Luren Bellucci**, professional dancers arranged by Rogovin, took over his classes and became the leading tango teaching couple in LA through the early-to-mid 1990s. **Loreen Arbus and Alberto Toledano**, who had trained with Paiva and later with masters in Buenos Aires (Antonio Todaro, Pepito Avellaneda, Gustavo Naveira, Susana Miller), won the Feather Award "for the most authentic Argentine tango couple in America" and performed at the Hollywood Bowl and the 1994 World Cup Soccer events.

Los Angeles received its second jolt when **Forever Tango** arrived in Beverly Hills in late 1994, but the community remained smaller and more diffuse than New York or San Francisco throughout this period.

## Seeds in Seattle, Portland, Denver, and beyond

The spread of tango beyond the three major coastal cities followed a pattern: a single motivated individual would attend a workshop or Stanford Tango Week, return home, and begin building a community from scratch.

**Seattle** was an early West Coast outpost. **Sonny Newman**, a Juilliard-trained dancer, saw "Tango Argentino" in Vancouver, B.C., traveled to Argentina to study, and "brought a tango to Seattle that sizzled." He co-taught with **Patty Leverett**, and by the early 1990s Seattle was hosting workshops that attracted dancers from across the Pacific Northwest.

**Portland** emerged through a chain of connections. In 1991, dancer Polly McBride attended a Seattle workshop by Newman and Leverett. In 1992, she met **Clay Nelson and Mary Gilbert** at workshops in Corvallis taught by Michael Walker and Luren Bellucci. Nelson attended Stanford Tango Week, began teaching beginner classes and hosting Sunday "Tango Teas" in 1994, and in November of that year invited Nora Dinzelbacher to Portland for workshops. By 1996, Nelson was bringing tango to the historic Crystal Ballroom in downtown Portland. In February 1997, he organized the first **ValenTango** festival, followed by the **Portland October TangoFest** — making Portland one of the most significant tango festival cities in the country.

**Denver** was seeded directly by Daniel Trenner. In 1995, Trenner taught for ten weeks in Colorado. Approximately 15 of his students accompanied him to Buenos Aires, and out of this experience **Tom Stermitz** and other enthusiasts from Denver, Boulder, and Fort Collins formed **Tango Colorado**, which organized twice-yearly festivals including the Denver Memorial Day festival.

**Chicago** entered the picture in June 1995, when **Janis Kenyon** held a tango festival at **Northwestern University** — one of the earliest Midwest tango events. Kenyon had attended Stanford Tango Week in 1993, where she met Juan Carlos Copes and María Nieves, whom she invited to teach at her event. The Argentine milonguero **Pepito Avellaneda** also traveled to Evanston for this congress — one of only a handful of Guardian Generation dancers who made the journey to the United States. **Miami** saw its first tango festival in February 1997 — "Tango Fantasy on Miami Beach," organized by Jorge Nel, Martha Mandel, Lydia Henson, and Randy Pittman. **Austin**, by contrast, was a later arrival; community co-founder Monica of Esquina Tango began teaching around 1997, initially practicing in a living room that slowly outgrew itself. **Boston's** Tango Society was not established until approximately 1999–2000.

The data point that captures the era most precisely comes from tango historian Tom Stermitz: **"In the US only a few cities had tango before 1995, which was the year the present wave exploded across the country."**

## The pilgrimage to Buenos Aires

The most transformative dimension of the American tango revival was not what happened in US cities but what happened when Americans traveled to Argentina and discovered the living source of the dance. This pilgrimage phenomenon connected two simultaneous revivals — America's discovery of tango and Argentina's own tango renaissance after decades of political repression.

Tango's decline in Argentina began long before the Dirty War. After the military overthrew Juan Perón in 1955, successive regimes imposed curfews and suppressed public gatherings. Since tango was traditionally taught man-to-man — young men practiced together for up to three years before dancing publicly with women — these all-male practice sessions fell under the prohibition. Rock and roll, which required no such gatherings, gained ground among Argentine youth. During the military dictatorship of 1976–1983, cultural repression intensified further. Many tango artists went underground or into exile; Astor Piazzolla spent decades abroad. By the early 1980s, social tango was populated almost exclusively by elderly dancers. The milongueros believed the form would die with their generation.

Democracy returned in 1983 under President Raúl Alfonsín, and tango began a tentative revival — but the catalyst was international. The Paris premiere of "Tango Argentino" in November 1983, followed by its triumph on Broadway, validated tango in the eyes of young Argentines who had dismissed it as their grandparents' relic. As the tango scholar Ana C. Cara explains: "The international success of the spectacle rekindled among Argentines the power of tango as dance." Government cultural programs in Buenos Aires offered free classes. Young dancers like **Gustavo Naveira**, who ran a practica at Cochabamba 444 from 1987 to 1997, reported crowds of 200 or more. The irony was devastating: **tango was being rescued partly because foreigners cared about it**.

The first Americans to make the journey did so independently and found a scene that was itself in the early stages of rebirth. **Al and Barbara Garvey** traveled to Buenos Aires in 1987 and witnessed traditional improvisational social tango at Centro Akarense in Villa Urquiza. The same year, **Daniel Trenner** arrived on a different mission — guest-teaching jazz and improvisation at the State School of Contemporary Dance in the San Martín Theatre — and discovered tango at a milonga in the Palermo neighborhood. He was introduced to **Miguel and Nelly Balmaceda**, who became his first tango teachers and who Trenner later described, perhaps with some overstatement, as the couple who "kept tango alive in the 1980s." Being fluent in Spanish and English, Trenner was uniquely positioned to serve as a bridge. He studied with masters including **Gustavo Naveira, Mingo Pugliese, Tete Rusconi and María Villalobos, Juan Bruno, and Antonio Todaro** — names that would become legendary in American tango education.

### The Guardian Generation

The Argentine maestros who taught these early American visitors belonged to what is sometimes called the "Guardian Generation" — dancers who had learned tango during its Golden Age (1930s–1950s) and preserved it through decades of neglect.

**Antonio Todaro** (born 1929, Chacarita neighborhood) was perhaps the most influential teacher of the revival era. He had operated a tango school in Flores for sixteen years with Raúl Bravo. After the "Tango Argentino" boom, he toured Europe, receiving his first contingent of 22 Dutch students from Amsterdam in 1986. "Half the people you meet from the generation that followed the milongueros were taught by Todaro." His students include Miguel Ángel Zotto, Milena Plebs, Roberto Herrera, and Alejandra Mantiñán. Todaro codified the eight-step basic in parallel feet that became the standard teaching tool worldwide.

**Pepito Avellaneda** (José Domingo Monteleone, 1930–1996) was a self-taught milonguero beloved for his cheeky, endearing style, especially in vals and milonga. He ran the milonga **La Calesita**, one of the most popular in Buenos Aires history. In June 1995, he traveled to Evanston, Illinois, to teach at Janis Kenyon's Northwestern congress — a rare American appearance for a Guardian Generation dancer. He died barely a year later, in April 1996.

**The Dinzels** (Gloria Inés Varo and Rodolfo Dinzelbacher) brought a more systematic approach. After their Broadway triumph, they developed the "Dinzel System" — a formalized methodology cataloging over 3,600 tango figures, born from 40 years of research. They founded the Centro Educativo del Tango in Buenos Aires, and Gustavo Naveira credits them as his first tango teachers. Their studio at Jufre 160 in Buenos Aires became a landmark for visiting Americans and Europeans.

**Susana Miller** occupied a uniquely important position. In the mid-1990s — apparently first in 1995 — she introduced North American dancers to what she called **"milonguero style tango"** (*tango estilo del centro*), characterized by maintained chest-to-chest contact and compact movements. She coined the English term to contrast with the "salon style" that had developed in North America (which was actually a modified version of stage tango). Miller was credited by the newspaper *Clarín* with having "probably put more people on the dance floor than any other single teacher."

The younger revival generation also emerged during this period. **Gustavo Naveira** (born 1960) and **Fabián Salas** founded the Tango Investigation Group, which applied principles of dance kinesiology to analyze the physics of tango movement — shifting instruction "from teaching what to dance toward teaching how to dance." **Miguel Ángel Zotto** (born 1958) studied with the Dinzels, Todaro, Pepito Avellaneda, Copes, and Virulazo before forming **Tango x 2** with Milena Plebs in 1988 and joining the "Tango Argentino" touring company.

### Trenner's Bridge to the Tango

Daniel Trenner formalized the pilgrimage. Between **1993 and 2001**, his **"Bridge to the Tango" tours** brought **more than 1,500 people** to Buenos Aires, dancing across seven countries. The tours were explicitly experiential — Trenner believed "learning tango is best done experientially, by visiting with dancers in their natural environment." He did not focus on one teacher or style but offered "an opportunity for the visiting dancer to experience the breadth and variety of the tango's genetics." A typical pattern: in 1995, Trenner taught for ten weeks in Colorado, after which approximately 15 students accompanied him to Buenos Aires to continue their education firsthand.

What Americans found when they arrived was disorienting. Tom Stermitz described his 1997 visit: "When I first went to Buenos Aires, I was confused because none of the steps I learned in class functioned on the dance floors, which were crowded with superb dancers." Americans had been taught stage tango steps at home, but actual milongas operated under entirely different principles — close embrace, navigation of a crowded floor, musicality, and improvisation. The cultural context — the ritual, the codes of the *cabeceo*, the ronda — could not be learned from steps alone.

## Daniel Trenner: planting tango across America

No single individual did more to physically carry Argentine tango across the United States than Daniel Trenner. His profile deserves particular attention because his method — itinerant teaching, video documentation, organized pilgrimages — was the primary mechanism through which tango reached most American communities before the internet era.

Trenner was a professional **tap dancer for more than ten years**, trained by Brenda Bufalino and Leon Collins — notable figures in the American tap revival. He held a Master of Education in Dance (1984). When he arrived in Buenos Aires in 1987, he was among "the first dozen young people involved in tango's revival" there. His fluency in Spanish gave him access to a world closed to most foreigners.

Upon returning to the United States, Trenner began what would become a **twenty-year itinerant teaching career**, visiting more than 100 cities on four continents. His approach was revolutionary for the era: he was **"one of the early proponents of teaching tango by its elements"** — emphasizing improvisation and lead-and-follow skills as the foundation of social tango, rather than memorized step sequences. His beginner class, "Tango from Zero," was specifically designed to make the dance accessible to absolute beginners, and this is the class that earned him the nickname "the Johnny Appleseed of tango." He would arrive in a city, plant the seed of a community, teach intensively, and move on.

His **Bridge to the Tango Television (BttTV)** video series, produced between roughly 1996 and 2001, comprised **more than 80 volumes featuring more than 40 artists**. The series covered leaders' technique, followers' technique, close embrace, salon tango, milonga, sacadas, crossed systems, and work with numerous Argentine masters including Gustavo Naveira and Olga Besio, Tete Rusconi and Silvia Ceriani, Mingo and Esther and Pablo Pugliese, Osvaldo Zotto, Chicho Frumboli, and Fernanda Ghi and Guillermo Merlo. In an era before YouTube, these videos were the primary visual resource for tango communities in cities that had no resident teachers. The analytical tango blog Tango Voice noted that while "the influence of instructional videos easily can be overrated," in the pre-YouTube era "the videos in this series had a significant impact on tango dancing in North America."

In 1996, Trenner created **The Tango Catalogue**, described as "the first tango store in North America" — offering CDs of traditional dance music, instructional VHS tapes, tango movies, shoes, and contacts. This became the commercial arm of **International Tango Productions (ITP)**, which he operated from 1996 to 2002 before selling it to the owners of Solo Tango, the Buenos Aires tango television station. Trenner settled in Northampton, Massachusetts, in 2002, founded "Casa 62" (the Tango House), and taught at the Five Colleges (Smith, Mt. Holyoke, Hampshire, Amherst, and UMass Amherst) from 2004 to 2020.

## The open embrace wars and other growing pains

The development of American tango teaching in this decade was marked by a fundamental pedagogical confusion that would take years to resolve. Because the first Argentine teachers available in the United States were **stage performers** teaching between shows, American tango instruction was built on a modified version of stage tango — retaining exhibitionist elements like ganchos and high boleos while sacrificing the close embrace of authentic social tango. The typical curriculum included walking, the salida, cruzada, resolution, forward and back ochos, the sandwich, giros, sacadas, barridas, ganchos, boleos, and adornments — all danced in an **open frame without the embrace** of genuine tango de salon.

This modified stage tango was paradoxically labeled **"salon tango"** in North America — a term that in Buenos Aires meant something entirely different (social tango danced at milongas in close embrace). The confusion was more than semantic; it represented a fundamental gap between what Americans were learning and the living tradition in Buenos Aires. Tom Stermitz captured this vividly: **"In 1994 in the US, 90% of tango was done in a wide-open embrace full of ganchos and big boleos. This was not at all typical or authentic for Buenos Aires milongas."**

The correction came in the mid-1990s when Susana Miller introduced "milonguero style" to North American dancers, accompanied by milongueros Pedro "Tete" Rusconi and Oscar "Cacho" Dante. North American instructors began adopting close embrace, including Christopher Nassopoulos in San Francisco, Robert Hauk in Portland, and Barbara Durr in Atlanta. The San Francisco Bay Area shifted toward milonguero-style emphasis after 1994. But the debate between open and close embrace — which reflected deeper questions about authenticity, cultural adaptation, and what tango "really" was — would persist for years and continues in modified form today. The two principal stylistic variants in Buenos Aires itself were *tango estilo del centro* (maintained embrace throughout, associated with downtown milongas) and *tango estilo del barrio* (the Villa Urquiza style, where the embrace opens temporarily for ochos and giros). American tango was developing its own hybrid, and it bore full resemblance to neither.

### Music: the Piazzolla problem

Access to appropriate tango music presented another challenge. Traditional Golden Age dance recordings — by the "big four" orchestras of **Juan D'Arienzo, Carlos Di Sarli, Aníbal Troilo, and Osvaldo Pugliese** — were extremely difficult to find in American record stores. What was widely available was the music of **Astor Piazzolla**, whose "nuevo tango" was brilliant concert music but unsuited to social dancing — its tempos, arrangements, and structures were designed for listening, not for navigating a milonga floor. As the comprehensive *tejastango.com* guide warned: "Although recordings by these artists are widely available and highly regarded for listening, they did not produce music intended for social dance."

This created a paradox in which Americans often associated tango music with Piazzolla while the actual music used in Buenos Aires milongas was essentially unobtainable. Distribution was limited to a few labels (EMI Reliquias, Blue Moon, El Bandoneon, Harlequin); mail order was often the only option. Trenner's Tango Catalogue and later TangoCD.com addressed this gap. Learning which orchestras to play, how to organize *tandas* (sets of three or four songs by one orchestra), and the role of the *cortina* (musical break between tandas) became part of the education that American DJs and community organizers had to acquire — often through direct experience in Buenos Aires.

## American tango at the threshold of 1995

By the end of 1995, the American tango landscape had been transformed from zero to a nascent national movement — small, passionate, concentrated in a handful of cities, but poised for explosive growth.

The numbers tell part of the story. San Francisco's BAATA mailing list had reached **1,400 dancers** by mid-1994, with monthly milongas multiplying tenfold. New York had milongas multiple nights per week, anchored by DanceSport, the Lafayette Grill, La Belle Epoque, Danel and Maria's events, and Dance Manhattan. Los Angeles had rebuilt from its post-1986 collapse, though it remained smaller. Portland, Seattle, Denver, and Chicago had emerging scenes, typically organized by a single motivated couple or individual who had attended Stanford Tango Week or studied with Daniel Trenner. Nationally, the total number of active Argentine tango dancers was likely in the **low thousands** — perhaps 3,000 to 5,000 — though no systematic count exists. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: No reliable national census of US tango dancers exists for 1995; this estimate is an inference from available community data.]

The **Stanford Tango Weeks** had established the annual festival as the organizing institution of American tango — a model that would proliferate rapidly after 1995. Trenner's videos were reaching communities in cities without resident teachers. The first wave of American pilgrims had returned from Buenos Aires with a fundamentally different understanding of what tango was and could be. The open-embrace/close-embrace debate was forcing a reckoning with questions of authenticity and cultural adaptation.

What lay immediately ahead would amplify everything. **Forever Tango** would reach Broadway in June 1997, running 322 performances and featuring Carlos Gavito and Marcela Durán in the iconic "A Evaristo Carriego" number — creating a second national wave of interest. Nora Dinzelbacher would launch **Nora's Tango Week** in Emeryville in 1998, carrying forward the Stanford tradition. Tango festivals would proliferate — Portland's ValenTango, Denver's Memorial Day, Miami's Tango Fantasy — creating a national circuit. Naveira and Salas's analytical "tango nuevo" approach would reshape pedagogy. The internet would connect communities that had been isolated. And Argentina itself would formally recognize what the rest of the world had already acknowledged: in 1996, the **Ley Nacional del Tango** declared tango part of the national cultural patrimony, and in 2009, UNESCO would add it to the Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

But all of this grew from those extraordinary ten years between 1985 and 1995 — from a Broadway show that wasn't supposed to last five weeks, from a butcher in a blood-covered apron who was persuaded to dance one more time, from a tap dancer who walked into a milonga in Palermo and saw something that changed his life, from a handful of Americans who traveled to Buenos Aires and found a dying tradition that turned out to be capable of resurrection. The American tango revival was never a top-down cultural import. It was built, city by city and milonga by milonga, by people who had been awakened to something they didn't know they'd been missing — and who then, in one of the great ironies of cultural history, helped save it in the country where it was born.

## Conclusion: what the revival reveals

Three insights emerge from this decade that reframe how we understand cultural transmission. First, **the American tango revival was catalyzed by stage tango but sustained by social tango** — two fundamentally different art forms sharing a name. The Broadway show created desire; the milonga tradition created community. Every major figure in this history — Trenner, the Garveys, Nora Dinzelbacher, Susana Miller — eventually gravitated from performance spectacle toward intimate social practice. The show opened the door, but what lay behind it was a participatory culture that could not be learned from watching.

Second, **the revival depended on physical human transmission** in ways that seem almost impossibly analog by today's standards. Trenner visiting 100 cities. VHS tapes mailed across the country. Stanford Tango Week graduates returning to Portland and Denver to start communities. Americans flying to Buenos Aires to sit in milongas with septuagenarian milongueros who spoke no English. There was no YouTube, no Instagram, no online tutorial. Every step of transmission required someone to be physically present in a room with someone else — which, when you think about it, is precisely what tango itself requires.

Third, and most remarkably, **the American discovery of tango helped rescue it in Argentina**. The Guardian Generation milongueros were correct that tango was dying. Young Argentines were not interested. What changed was not some internal cultural renewal but the validation that came from foreigners — especially Americans — treating the dance as something precious, worth traveling thousands of miles to learn. The economic support of tango tourism, the professional opportunities created by international shows, and the simple fact of foreign admiration combined to make tango culturally viable again in its homeland. Alberto Paz, the Argentine tango historian, put it plainly: "There has never been a time in history when so many social dancers are dancing Argentine tango." That extraordinary fact traces directly to an October evening in 1985 when a cast of middle-aged Argentines took the stage at the Mark Hellinger Theatre and showed America what it had been missing.
