## 1. STRUCTURE REVIEW

### What's Good
The 6-category structure (Argentina, Dancers, Europe, USA, Orchestras, Events) is solid and reflects how tango people actually think about the history. The separation of dance history from music history is smart — these are parallel but distinct worlds, and conflating them is a common mistake in tango writing. The Dancers category as its own pillar is a strong choice; most tango histories are music-first and treat the dance as an afterthought.

### What's Missing

**Singers deserve their own category** — or at minimum a dedicated sub-category under Orchestras. The current framework has 11 orchestras indexed but only 1 singer (Gardel). This is a significant gap. The cantor de orquesta was central to Golden Age tango — the relationship between singer and orchestra defined how people experienced tango in the 1940s. The framework already has "cantor de orquesta" as an indexed term, which shows awareness of this, but the actual singer profiles are absent. At minimum, a "Singers" era-timeline within the Orchestras category would cover: Gardel (pre-orchestra era), the great cantores (Fiorentino, Vargas, Castillo, Podestá, Rivero, Goyeneche), and the post-orchestra solo singers.

**Lyricists** are a secondary concern but worth noting. Enrique Santos Discépolo, Homero Manzi, Enrique Cadícamo, and Celedonio Flores essentially wrote the emotional vocabulary of tango. For a dance-focused timeline they can stay as indexed entities rather than getting their own category, but their absence from the key figures lists is noticeable. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Should lyricists get more than index entries? Tango poetry is integral to how porteños relate to the music.]

**Venues could be promoted.** The 8 indexed venues are a good start but the framework doesn't tell the story of how venue culture shaped tango. The confiterías, the clubes de barrio, the cabarets — these weren't just places where tango happened, they defined how tango was danced. Sin Rumbo and Sunderland created Villa Urquiza style because of how those floors worked and what those communities expected. Consider a sub-timeline within Argentina tracking venue evolution.

### Recommendations
- Add a Singers sub-category to Orchestras (or make it Category 7)
- Promote key lyricists to indexed figures with brief profiles
- Consider a Venues sub-timeline within Argentina showing how dance spaces shaped styles

## 2. ERA COVERAGE

### Category 1: Tango Argentina

**La Guardia Vieja (1880–1920):** Dates are right by consensus. The summary is solid but needs one critical correction: the framework attributes tango solely to Buenos Aires. The 2009 UNESCO inscription formally recognizes tango as originating in both Buenos Aires and Montevideo. Uruguay's contribution — Afro-Uruguayan candombe, the port culture of Montevideo's Barrio Sur, and the composition of La Cumparsita (Gerardo Matos Rodríguez, 1917) — is well-documented and broadly accepted. Omitting Montevideo is a significant gap that knowledgeable tango people will notice immediately.

Also: the claim that canyengue was "danced in brothels" is contested. Canyengue was danced in conventillos, patios, academias de baile, and some brothels — but framing brothels as the primary venue is an older narrative that many contemporary historians push back against. The academias (dance halls with hired women, requiring municipal permits, located in outer barrios) were likely more important than brothels as incubators. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: The brothel origin narrative — how much weight to give it vs. conventillo/academia narrative?]

**La Guardia Nueva (1920–1935):** Good. The Gardel death date as era boundary works. Consider adding: Julio De Caro's 1924 revolution (the Sexteto De Caro essentially invented modern tango orchestration), and the transition from sextet to orquesta típica format.

**La Época de Oro (1935–1955):** The strongest section. The existing white paper sounds comprehensive. One note: the "~50,000 recordings" figure is widely cited but may be approximate — it's a reasonable estimate but not a verified count. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Verify the 50,000 recordings figure — is this a scholarly estimate or folk wisdom?]

**La Decadencia (1955–1983):** Dates are correct. The summary should note that it wasn't just political suppression — the cultural shift was generational. Rock nacional, the nueva canción movement, and the simple passage of time all contributed. The Dirty War (1976–1983) was the worst period, but tango was already in decline by 1960. Consider splitting: "Cultural Decline" (1955–1976) and "Dirty War Suppression" (1976–1983) — these are qualitatively different kinds of decline.

**El Renacimiento (1983–1995):** Good boundaries. The 1983 start (democracy + Tango Argentino show) is the universally agreed inflection point.

**La Investigación (1995–2005):** Good. The Cochabamba sessions at Club Belgrano (Cochabamba 444, San Telmo) are accurately described. Note: Naveira himself resists the label "nuevo" as a style — he sees it as investigation, not genre. This tension is worth noting in the content.

**Tango Nuevo Peak (2005–2012):** Reasonable boundaries. The 2005 start might be debated — some would say 2003 (Mundial) or 2007 (peak festival era). [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Is 2005 the right start for "Nuevo Peak" or should it be earlier/later?]

**Neo-Traditional (2012–2030):** The start date is reasonable. The encuentro milonguero movement and the passing of the last milongueros both contribute to this shift. Death dates listed (Tete 2010, Cartery 2015, Segarra 2018) are good markers but note that Tete's death (2010) is before the era start. Consider moving the start to 2010 to capture this.

### Category 2: Dancers & Couples

**Issue:** Stage Pioneers era (1983–2000) overlaps with Bridge Generation (1980–2000) and Nuevo Innovators (1995–2010). Three concurrent eras in a 4-era category creates timeline confusion. Consider whether Stage Pioneers should be folded into the Argentina timeline (since "Tango Argentino" is already an Event) rather than living in the Dancers category.

**Carlos Gavito is misclassified** under Nuevo Innovators. Gavito (1942–2005) was not a nuevo dancer. He was a milonguero-style dancer who achieved fame through theatrical productions (Tango Argentino, Forever Tango). His signature tent embrace, minimalist movement philosophy, and emotional intensity are the antithesis of nuevo investigation. He belongs in Stage Pioneers or as a Bridge Generation figure. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Gavito classification — stage dancer? salon? milonguero adapted for stage?]

**Pablo Verón** is similarly questionable under Nuevo Innovators. Verón is primarily a stage and film dancer (The Tango Lesson, 1997). His work is spectacular but not "investigation" in the Naveira/Salas sense.

### Category 3: Tango Europe

The 10-era structure is ambitious and mostly well-conceived. Two concerns:

The "Piazzolla's Europe" era (1970–1985) is important but risks overstating Piazzolla's connection to dance tango in Europe. Piazzolla kept tango music alive in European concert halls, but most European dancers who started in the 1980s-90s discovered tango through the Tango Argentino show, not through Piazzolla concerts. The framework's own summary acknowledges this ("musical and dance worlds almost completely separated") which is accurate.

The "German & Nordic Wave" era correctly identifies Germany as crucial but the Pina Bausch connection deserves careful framing. Bausch invited Tete to Tanztheater Wuppertal, yes, but this was contemporary dance/theater context, not social tango. It mattered for cultural legitimacy but didn't directly build the German social tango scene. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: How much did Bausch actually influence German social tango vs. tango's cultural cachet in Germany?]

### Category 4: Tango USA

This is the weakest section — all placeholders. The 5-era structure looks right, but the framework needs to acknowledge regional specificity. US tango isn't one story — it's New York, San Francisco, Portland, Austin, Denver, etc., each with distinct character. The Broadway impact era should note that Daniel Trenner and Rebecca Shulman were among the first Americans to go to Buenos Aires and bring back authentic social tango in the late 1980s/early 1990s. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Who were the key Americans who traveled to BA in the late 1980s and brought tango back?]

### Category 5: Orchestras

The 4-era structure has a notable gap: there's no era for the Guardia Nueva orchestras (1920–1935). The timeline jumps from "Early Orchestras (Guitar & Flute Era) 1880–1920" to "Golden Age Orchestras 1935–1955," missing the entire Guardia Nueva period where the orquesta típica format was invented. Julio De Caro, Roberto Firpo, Francisco Canaro's early work, Eduardo Arolas — this is when tango became tango as we know it musically. Add an era: "Guardia Nueva Orchestras (Birth of the Orquesta Típica) — 1920–1935."

**Missing from Golden Age orchestra list:** D'Agostino (with Ángel Vargas — 93 recordings for RCA Victor, essential dance music), Laurenz, D'Arienzo's various singer partnerships, and Osvaldo Fresedo (who bridges guardia nueva to golden age).

## 4. GEOGRAPHIC GAPS

### The Uruguay Problem

This is the single biggest factual gap in the framework. Tango is officially, historically, and culturally a product of both Buenos Aires and Montevideo. The 2009 UNESCO inscription names both countries. Omitting Uruguay from the framework will undermine credibility with any serious tango person. At minimum:

- Revise Category 1 to acknowledge the Río de la Plata origins (not just "Buenos Aires")
- Add Montevideo's Barrio Sur, the candombe connection, and La Cumparsita
- Consider whether Uruguay needs its own sub-section or can be woven into the Argentina timeline

### Japan — Major Omission

Japan has one of the world's oldest and most dedicated tango communities outside the Río de la Plata:

- Tango arrived 1913–1914; Baron Megata opened a tango academy in Tokyo in the 1920s
- By the 1950s–60s, over 20 Japanese orchestras were playing tango
- Japan preserved tango during Argentina's Decadencia period
- Japanese tango culture is distinct: emotional resonance with themes of loss and longing connects to Japanese cultural sensibility
- Today Japan has a thriving milonga scene

Japan deserves at minimum a dedicated era within a broader "Tango Global" category, or an expansion of the framework beyond Europe/USA.

### Colombia — Significant Omission

Medellín has the oldest continuous tango tradition outside Argentina and Uruguay:

- Tango arrived at the turn of the 20th century
- Gardel's death there in 1935 cemented the connection
- Tango events occur daily in Medellín today
- The Gardel neighborhood (Barrio Manrique) is a cultural landmark

### Other Countries Worth Noting

- **Turkey/Istanbul:** Became a major tango city in the 2000s; the framework's Festival Era mentions this but it could be expanded
- **South Korea:** Growing scene since the 2010s
- **Russia:** Significant scene especially in Moscow

### Recommendation

Consider renaming Categories 3 and 4 to a unified "Tango Global" category with sub-sections: Europe, North America, Asia (Japan, Korea), Latin America (Uruguay, Colombia), Middle East/Turkey. Or keep the current structure but add at minimum a Category 7: "Tango Global" covering Japan, Colombia, and other significant scenes.

## 5. MUSIC VS. DANCE BALANCE

### Current Balance

The framework is dance-forward, which is appropriate for a tango dancer audience. But the music side has gaps:

### What's undercovered on the music side:

1. **The singer-orchestra relationship** — this was the defining feature of Golden Age tango for listeners and dancers. Which singer sang with which orchestra changed everything about how a tanda feels. D'Arienzo with Echagüe vs. D'Arienzo with Mauré are different musical worlds. The framework treats orchestras as monolithic entities when in reality they evolved through singer partnerships.

2. **Piazzolla** — completely absent from the framework except as a passing mention in the Europe section. Love him or hate him (and the tango dance community is famously divided), Piazzolla is the single most globally recognized tango musician. His absence from a tango history timeline would be conspicuous. He doesn't need to be celebrated, but he needs to be placed in context: what he did, why the dance community resisted it, and how his music eventually found limited use in milongas (particularly through neo-tango DJs). [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: How to handle Piazzolla — does the dance community accept him now more than before?]

3. **The DJ culture** — post-2000, the tango DJ became a key figure in how dancers experience music. The shift from live orchestras to recorded music, the role of the musicalizador, the politics of tanda construction, the digital transfer and mastering of 78 RPM recordings (CTA, TangoTunes) — this is a significant contemporary development.

4. **Electronic/alternative tango music** — Gotan Project (2001), Bajofondo, Otros Aires, Tanghetto. These brought tango to new audiences and created the "neo-tango" DJ phenomenon. Controversial in the traditional community but historically significant.

### Recommendation

- Add singer profiles to the Orchestras category (or create a Singers sub-category)
- Add a Piazzolla section — even a brief, contextualized one
- Consider adding a "Tango Music in the Digital Age" era (2000–present) covering DJs, digital transfers, electronic tango

## 7. AREAS OF COMMUNITY DISAGREEMENT

These are the topics where tango people will argue with you at the bar after the milonga:

### Origin Narratives

**Contested:** The relative weight of African, European, and criollo contributions to tango's birth. The African-Argentine candombe influence is increasingly recognized by scholars but some in the community still emphasize European immigrant contributions more heavily. The framework currently says "African-Argentine candombe, habanera, milonga criolla fusion" which is a good balanced statement, but be prepared for debate.

**Contested:** Whether tango originated in brothels. The older narrative says yes; contemporary scholarship emphasizes conventillos, academias, and patios. The truth is probably "all of the above" but the brothel narrative has been overemphasized in popular tellings. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED]

### Style Classification

**Contested:** Whether "milonguero style" is a historical style or a modern invention. Susana Miller codified it in the 1990s, but did the old milongueros actually dance in a unified "style" or is this a retroactive category? Many dancers who knew Finito, Tete, and Pepito say they all danced differently. The framework's style definitions (Estilo del Centro, Estilo del Barrio) are broadly accepted labels but their boundaries are fuzzy.

**Contested:** Whether Naveira's "investigation" created a new style or revealed existing possibilities. Naveira himself insists it's investigation, not a style. The community generally calls it "nuevo" anyway. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED]

### The Piazzolla Question

**Strongly contested:** Whether Piazzolla's music is "real" tango. The dance community is split. Traditionalists say it's concert music that abandoned tango's rhythmic foundation. Others say he evolved tango the way tango always evolved. The UNESCO inscription includes both traditional and Piazzolla-era tango. For a timeline aimed at dancers, this tension needs to be acknowledged, not resolved.

### Golden Age Canonization

**Contested:** The "Big Four" designation (D'Arienzo, Di Sarli, Troilo, Pugliese). This is widely accepted but some argue for a Big Five or Big Six (adding Biagi, Caló, Tanturi, or De Angelis). Others argue the "Big Four" is a modern construction that wouldn't have been recognized in the 1940s. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Did the "Big Four" concept exist during the Golden Age or is it a modern DJ/teacher construct?]

### Paris Validation

**Debated:** The degree to which Paris "validated" tango for Buenos Aires. The framework states this as fact ("Paris validation brings respectability back to Buenos Aires"). Some historians argue this is an oversimplification — tango was already moving toward respectability through its own internal dynamics, and the Paris story has been mythologized. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED]

### The Role of Perón

**Contested:** Whether Perón was good or bad for tango. The framework implies Perón's overthrow caused the Decadencia, but the relationship is more complex. Perón promoted popular culture including tango, but he also used cultural institutions for political control. Some argue tango was already declining under late Perón due to changing tastes. [HITM REVIEW NEEDED: Was the Decadencia political or cultural? Both?]

## 8. PRIORITIES

### If You Can Only Write 5 More White Papers

1. **La Guardia Vieja (1880–1920) with Uruguay** — This is your origin story. Get it right, include Montevideo, handle the contested narratives with nuance. Everything else builds on this foundation.

2. **Singers of the Golden Age** — The biggest content gap in an otherwise strong music section. Profile the major cantores, their orchestra partnerships, and their contribution to how we experience tango today. This fills a hole that knowledgeable dancers will notice.

3. **La Decadencia (1955–1983)** — Already has a prompt ready. This is the dark period that makes the Renacimiento meaningful. Without understanding what was lost, the revival story lacks weight.

4. **Piazzolla and Post-Golden Age Music (1955–1990)** — Controversial but essential. Handle it as history, not advocacy. What happened to tango music after the Golden Age? Where does Piazzolla fit? What about the musicians who kept playing traditional tango through the dark years?

5. **Tango Global: Japan, Uruguay, Colombia** — Geographic expansion that demonstrates this isn't a parochial project. Japan alone is a story that will surprise and engage your audience.

### The Single Biggest Gap

**Uruguay.** Full stop. You cannot have a credible tango history timeline that doesn't acknowledge Montevideo as a co-birthplace. This isn't a minor omission — it's a factual error that UNESCO has explicitly corrected. Fix this first.
