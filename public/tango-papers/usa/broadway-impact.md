# The Night America Discovered the Real Tango

**The American Tango Revival, 1985–1995**

On the evening of October 9, 1985, a show opened at the Mark Hellinger Theatre on Broadway that would, within a decade, seed a new dance culture across the United States. **"Tango Argentino" was not supposed to be a hit.** It had one set, four bandoneóns, no English dialogue, and a cast whose average age was 42 — including a 58-year-old former butcher with a 38-inch waistline. The producers booked it for five weeks. It ran for 198 performances, earned three Tony nominations, and grossed $6.2 million.

More importantly, it shattered a seventy-year misconception. For the first time, Americans saw what Argentine tango actually looked like — and it bore almost no resemblance to the stiff, rose-in-teeth parody they knew from ballroom studios and Hollywood films.

---

## A Show That Broke Every Broadway Rule

"Tango Argentino" was conceived in 1974 by two Argentine set designers, **Claudio Segovia** and **Héctor Orezzoli**, who wanted to stage an authentic portrait of tango's world. The show took nearly a decade to assemble. Juan Carlos Copes, the most famous tango dancer alive, initially thought the idea was "crazy."

The cast represented the full spectrum of Argentine tango:
- **Copes and María Nieves** — elegant stage virtuosity, partners since adolescence in the 1940s
- **Nelson Avila and Nélida Rodriguez de Aure** — acrobatic speed
- **Gloria and Rodolfo Dinzel** — scholarly precision
- **Héctor and Elsa María Mayoral** — salon authenticity with small, playful steps
- **Virulazo and Elvira Santamaría** — raw milonguero truth, oldest couple in the cast

Music was provided by **Sexteto Mayor**, with arrangements by Horacio Salgán. Every performance opened and closed with "Quejas de bandoneón" — the signature piece of Aníbal Troilo.

The show premiered at the **Théâtre du Châtelet** in Paris on November 11, 1983. The entire cast had flown from Buenos Aires on an Argentine Air Force cargo plane — literally as cargo, with no passenger services. Every show sold out.

### Broadway Success

The New York Times called it "the unlikeliest hit of the season," noting that the show "violated the Broadway dictum that musicals be elaborate, expensive, and stocked with supple young bodies."

The celebrity audience was staggering: **Frank Sinatra, Madonna, Jackie Onassis, Mikhail Baryshnikov, Oscar de la Renta**. Baryshnikov called tango "a dance of indescribable beauty, with the finesse of ballet and the fire of flamenco."

The show received **three Tony Award nominations** for the 1985–86 season: Best Musical, Best Choreography, and Best Direction of a Musical.

## The Founding Generation of American Tango

### Paul Pellicoro and DanceSport

**Paul Pellicoro**, who had founded his DanceSport studio in New York in 1985, provided a critical nexus. He made his space available for the Broadway cast to teach students directly. This was where many of the first American tango lessons occurred.

Pellicoro rose to national fame in 1992 when he choreographed **Al Pacino's iconic tango scene in *Scent of a Woman*** — a moment that did more than perhaps anything else to embed Argentine tango in American popular consciousness.

### Daniel Trenner — Johnny Appleseed of Tango

**Daniel Trenner** arrived in Buenos Aires in 1987, one of "the first dozen young people involved in tango's revival" there. His fluency in Spanish gave him access to a world closed to most foreigners.

He studied with masters including **Gustavo Naveira, Mingo Pugliese, Tete Rusconi, Juan Bruno, and Antonio Todaro**. Upon returning to the United States, Trenner began what would become a **twenty-year itinerant teaching career**, visiting more than 100 cities on four continents.

Between **1993 and 2001**, his **"Bridge to the Tango" tours** brought **more than 1,500 people** to Buenos Aires. His **Bridge to the Tango Television** video series comprised **more than 80 volumes featuring more than 40 artists** — in an era before YouTube, these videos were the primary visual resource for tango communities in cities without resident teachers.

### Stanford Tango Week

In 1991, **Richard Powers** transformed a Cincinnati dance festival into the **Stanford Tango Weeks** — the first major annual tango festival in the United States. The Stanford Tango Weeks ran for nine years (1991–1997), featuring **32 instructors** including Juan Carlos Copes, Pablo Verón, Graciela González, and Daniel Trenner.

**"The Stanford Tango Weeks have been credited by tango dancers and teachers for being an essential catalyst for the growth of Argentine Tango in the United States."** Many who later founded tango communities across the country first encountered serious tango instruction at Stanford.

## San Francisco Becomes the Proving Ground

In **1987, Al and Barbara Garvey traveled to Buenos Aires** — one of the first American couples to make the pilgrimage. What they found at **Centro Akarense** transformed their understanding. They returned to California and eventually **co-founded the Bay Area Argentine Tango Association (BAATA)**.

**Nora and Raul Dinzelbacher settled permanently in San Francisco in 1988**, responding to demand for Argentine tango teachers. **Raul Dinzelbacher died at age 40** during Stanford Tango Week — but Nora continued.

The Bay Area's tango explosion arrived in 1994 when **Forever Tango** played an astonishing **92-week run** at San Francisco's Theatre on the Square. BAATA's mailing list grew from **400 to 1,400 dancers** in the first half of 1994 alone. Regional milongas multiplied from **three per month to thirty**.

---

## Seeds Across America

The spread followed a pattern: a single motivated individual would attend a workshop or Stanford Tango Week, return home, and begin building a community from scratch.

- **Seattle**: Sonny Newman saw "Tango Argentino" in Vancouver, traveled to Argentina, and "brought a tango to Seattle that sizzled"
- **Portland**: Clay Nelson attended Stanford Tango Week, began teaching in 1994, organized first ValenTango festival in February 1997
- **Denver**: Seeded directly by Daniel Trenner in 1995; **Tom Stermitz** founded Tango Colorado
- **Chicago**: **Janis Kenyon** held tango festival at Northwestern University in June 1995

Tom Stermitz captured the era most precisely: **"In the US only a few cities had tango before 1995, which was the year the present wave exploded across the country."**

## The Pilgrimage to Buenos Aires

The most transformative dimension was what happened when Americans traveled to Argentina and discovered the living source of the dance.

### The Guardian Generation

The Argentine maestros who taught these early American visitors belonged to the "Guardian Generation" — dancers who had learned tango during its Golden Age and preserved it through decades of neglect:

- **Antonio Todaro** (1929–1994) — "Half the people you meet from the generation that followed the milongueros were taught by Todaro"
- **Pepito Avellaneda** (1930–1996) — traveled to Evanston, Illinois in June 1995
- **The Dinzels** — developed systematic methodology cataloging 3,600+ figures
- **Susana Miller** — introduced "milonguero style" to North Americans, mid-1990s

### What Americans Found

Tom Stermitz described his 1997 visit: "When I first went to Buenos Aires, I was confused because none of the steps I learned in class functioned on the dance floors, which were crowded with superb dancers."

Americans had been taught stage tango steps at home, but actual milongas operated under entirely different principles — close embrace, navigation of a crowded floor, musicality, and improvisation.

---

## The Open Embrace Wars

The development of American tango teaching was marked by fundamental pedagogical confusion. Because the first Argentine teachers available were **stage performers**, American instruction was built on a modified version of stage tango.

Tom Stermitz captured this vividly: **"In 1994 in the US, 90% of tango was done in a wide-open embrace full of ganchos and big boleos. This was not at all typical or authentic for Buenos Aires milongas."**

The correction came in the mid-1990s when Susana Miller introduced "milonguero style" to North American dancers.

---

## American Tango at the Threshold of 1995

By the end of 1995, the American tango landscape had been transformed from zero to a nascent national movement:

- San Francisco's BAATA mailing list: **1,400 dancers**
- New York had milongas multiple nights per week
- Portland, Seattle, Denver, Chicago had emerging scenes
- National estimate: **3,000–5,000 active dancers**

What lay ahead would amplify everything. **Forever Tango** would reach Broadway in June 1997. Tango festivals would proliferate. The internet would connect communities. And in 2009, UNESCO would add tango to the Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

---

## Conclusion: What the Revival Reveals

Three insights emerge:

1. **The American tango revival was catalyzed by stage tango but sustained by social tango** — two fundamentally different art forms sharing a name

2. **The revival depended on physical human transmission** in ways that seem impossibly analog by today's standards — Trenner visiting 100 cities, VHS tapes mailed across the country, Stanford graduates returning to Portland and Denver

3. **The American discovery of tango helped rescue it in Argentina** — foreign admiration made tango culturally viable again in its homeland

Alberto Paz put it plainly: "There has never been a time in history when so many social dancers are dancing Argentine tango." That extraordinary fact traces directly to an October evening in 1985 when a cast of middle-aged Argentines took the stage at the Mark Hellinger Theatre and showed America what it had been missing.
