# When the Orchestras Died: Tango's 35-Year Fight for Survival (1955–1990)

**Between 1955 and 1990, Argentine tango suffered a near-extinction event.** A military coup severed the political lifeline sustaining hundreds of orquestas típicas. Rock and roll captured the youth. Radio abandoned live broadcasts. The recording masters burned. One by one, the Big Four orchestra leaders — Di Sarli, Troilo, D'Arienzo, Pugliese — died across a span of 35 years, and only the last of them lived long enough to see tango revive.

This is the story of why milongas worldwide still play 80-year-old recordings: because nothing was made to replace them.

---

## The Coup That Killed 200 Orchestras

### The Political Machine That Built the Golden Age

Tango's Golden Age was underwritten by state policy. Under Juan Perón's government (1946–1955), **Decreto 3371/1949** and Law No. 14,226 required that at least 50% of all music played on radio or performed live be of national origin. At the peak, Buenos Aires had approximately **200 active tango orchestras**.

### September 16, 1955: The Music Stops

The Revolución Libertadora overthrew Perón, and the new military government held reflexive hostility toward everything Peronist — including tango.

- **Bans on public gatherings** made milongas effectively illegal
- **Curfews** destroyed the nighttime tango economy
- **Artists were blacklisted, imprisoned, or forced underground**
- The 50% national music quota was no longer enforced

Tango historian Christine Denniston documents a crucial asymmetry: laws banning minors in nightclubs were **rigidly enforced at tango venues but ignored at rock and roll clubs**. "The generation that were 18 years old in 1955 learned to dance the tango well. The generation that were 13 didn't learn it at all."

By the early 1980s, the traditional orquesta típica had effectively disappeared as a functioning institution.

### The RCA Masters Catastrophe

Sometime around 1962–1964, Ricardo Mejía, head of production at RCA Victor Argentina, reportedly ordered the destruction of the label's warehouse of original metal master recordings. The masters were allegedly **burned** — 40 years of recordings encompassing Di Sarli, D'Arienzo, Tanturi, De Caro, Firpo, Troilo, Gardel, and every other RCA tango artist.

**[HITM]** The RCA masters destruction story should be treated as partially verified. It circulates widely, is consistent with poor audio quality of known RCA reissues, but no definitive academic account exists.

## The Big Four: A Countdown

### Di Sarli Falls First (1960)

Carlos Di Sarli — "El Señor del Tango" — died on **January 12, 1960**, of a cerebrovascular event. He was 57 years old, just five days past his birthday. He was the first of the Big Four to fall.

His final recordings were made in **1958 for Philips**. His last performance took place on **March 8, 1959, at Club Podestá de Lanús**, finishing with his own composition "Bahía Blanca."

### Pugliese: The Orchestra That Refused to Die

Osvaldo Pugliese founded his orchestra in **1939** and organized it as a **cooperative** from the very first day — the first tango director to do so. This structure created extraordinary loyalty: his core musicians stayed for **nearly 30 years**.

Pugliese joined the **Argentine Communist Party in 1936** (membership number 108). This made him a target for every government that held power between 1939 and 1983.

- **First imprisoned in 1939**
- **Jailed January to July 1955 at Villa Devoto prison**
- **Imprisoned again in 1956 during "Operation Cardenal"** aboard the ship *París*, reportedly to be sunk with them inside
- **Banned from radio under General Onganía (1966–1970)**, restricted to ten recordings per year
- **Threatened during the Dirty War (1976–1983)** but "simply too popular" to disappear

### The Red Carnation

When Pugliese was imprisoned in 1939, his orchestra performed without a pianist. A **red carnation was placed on the piano** as a symbol of his absence. This became one of tango's most potent symbols, repeated dramatically during the 1956 Radio Splendid broadcast while Pugliese was captive on the ship *París*.

### San Pugliese: The Folk Saint

The "San Pugliese" superstition — invoking the maestro's name to ward off bad luck — emerged in the **early 1990s**. Practices include saying his name three times before performances, displaying his photograph in dressing rooms, playing a Pugliese record to fix technical problems.

Pugliese died on **July 25, 1995**, at age 89. An impressive funeral caravan marched in reverse along Corrientes Avenue while "La Yumba" played.

**Pugliese outlived the other three Big Four members by at least 19 years.** He was the only one to witness the tango revival.

## Piazzolla: The Genius Who Split Tango in Two

Astor Piazzolla joined Aníbal Troilo's orchestra in **1939** as a fourth bandoneonist. The decisive break came in **Paris in 1954** with Nadia Boulanger. After nearly five hours reading through his concert works, she found them derivative. She asked him to play one of his tangos. He played "Triunfal." She took his hands and said: **"You idiot, that's Piazzolla!"**

Returning to Buenos Aires in 1955, he founded the **Octeto Buenos Aires** — the first nuevo tango ensemble.

The reaction was extreme:
- Street fights
- Death threats
- His band was allegedly doused with gasoline
- A taxi driver screamed at him for "desecrating" tango

In **May 1974**, he recorded **"Libertango"** at Mondial Sound Studio in Milan — which became an international sensation.

**Why dancers cannot use his music:** Piazzolla's compositions changed tango's rhythmic structure from the traditional 2×4 to **3+3+2 groupings**, making them unintuitive for social dance. At traditional milongas in Buenos Aires, Piazzolla is essentially never played.

He suffered a brain hemorrhage in Paris on **August 5, 1990**, and died in Buenos Aires on **July 4, 1992**, at age 71.

## Troilo and D'Arienzo: Eight Months That Ended an Era

**Aníbal Troilo** — "Pichuco" — died on **May 18, 1975**, from a stroke followed by cardiac arrest. He was 60. Troilo once said of D'Arienzo: *"Laugh if you will, but without him, we would all be out of work."*

**Juan D'Arienzo** — "El Rey del Compás" — died on **January 14, 1976**, just eight months after Troilo. He was 75. D'Arienzo had maintained a **full-sized orchestra** even in the 1970s.

The loss of Troilo and D'Arienzo within eight months left **Pugliese's as the only remaining active full orquesta típica**.

## The Survivors

### Quinteto Real and Horacio Salgán

**Quinteto Real** was founded in **1960** by pianist **Horacio Salgán** and guitarist **Ubaldo de Lío**. Salgán lived to be **100 years old** (1916–2016), making him perhaps the longest-lived bridge between the Golden Age and the modern era.

### Sexteto Mayor: The Show That Saved Tango

**Sexteto Mayor** was founded on **April 29, 1973**, by bandoneonists **José Libertella** and **Luis Stazo**. Their role became pivotal when director **Claudio Segovia** recruited them for **"Tango Argentino,"** which premiered in Paris on **November 11, 1983**, and transferred to Broadway in **October 1985**.

They won a **Latin Grammy Award** in 2003 — the first Argentine tango ensemble to do so.

## Why We Dance to Ghosts

At traditional Buenos Aires milongas, **virtually all music played dates from the 1930s through the 1950s**. After the mid-1950s, surviving orchestras shifted from dance music to concert music. The singer-as-soloist replaced the singer-as-instrument-of-the-orchestra, disrupting dance rhythm.

The Golden Age orchestras evolved *with* the dancers in a nightly feedback loop — music and movement developing together across two decades, captured in three-minute packages of disciplined perfection. That ecosystem was destroyed in 1955 and has never been rebuilt.
