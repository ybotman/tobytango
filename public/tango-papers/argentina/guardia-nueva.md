# La Guardia Nueva: How Tango Became Art (1920–1935)

**Between 1920 and 1935, tango underwent the most important transformation in its history — evolving from rough, improvised street music into the sophisticated orchestral art form that would power the Golden Age.** Three forces drove this metamorphosis: Julio De Caro's 1924 orchestral revolution, which invented modern tango arrangement; Carlos Gardel's creation of the tango canción, which gave tango a human voice; and the explosive growth of recording and radio technology, which carried both innovations into every Argentine home.

Every element that defines Golden Age tango — the orquesta típica format, the bandoneon's lyrical dominance, complex written arrangements, the integration of singers into orchestras — was invented or codified during La Guardia Nueva. The era began with tango still played largely by ear in smoky waterfront dives and ended with it broadcast on 25 radio stations, pressed onto four million records per year, and projected onto movie screens from Buenos Aires to New York.

---

## Julio De Caro and the Invention of Modern Tango Orchestration

The single most consequential moment in tango's musical evolution occurred at the end of 1923, when pianist Juan Carlos Cobián dissolved his successful sextet to follow a lover to New York. Cobián's ensemble had included two brothers — violinist Julio De Caro and pianist Francisco De Caro — alongside bandoneonist Pedro Maffia. When an entrepreneur offered Francisco De Caro a contract for New Year's Eve performances at society parties, paying the remarkable sum of **800 pesos per dance**, the brothers reassembled their colleagues into a new formation. The **Sexteto De Caro** was born.

**Julio De Caro** (December 11, 1899 – March 11, 1980) came from a musical household of Italian origin. His father José, who had directed the Conservatory at Milan's Teatro della Scala, opened his own conservatory in Buenos Aires's San Telmo neighborhood in 1913. Julio's classical training became the foundation for everything that followed.

The original 1924 lineup consisted of Julio De Caro on violin and direction, his brother **Emilio De Caro** on second violin, **Pedro Maffia** on first bandoneon, **Luis Petrucelli** on second bandoneon, **Francisco De Caro** on piano, and **Leopoldo Thompson** on double bass. Their first recordings for Victor in 1924 — including De Caro's own compositions "Todo corazón" and "Pobre Margot" — marked what scholars call **"the inaugural moment in the history of modern instrumental tango."**

### What the Revolution Sounded Like

The De Caro sextet broke from Guardia Vieja practice on virtually every front:

- Where earlier groups improvised, playing "by ear" (*al oído*), De Caro wrote out complete arrangements with counterpoint and counter-melodic lines
- Where Guardia Vieja groups favored short, choppy, staccato phrases, De Caro **emphasized the longer phrase**, shifting toward legato, sustained melodic lines
- Where all instruments had pounded out rhythm together, De Caro gave each instrument an independent voice — bandoneons in expressive dialogue, piano elevated to "a permanent element of circulation and relay"

Francisco De Caro's piano work was particularly transformative. The left hand doubled the bass line while the right performed melodic, ornamental, and linking functions. The piano became the "heart of the orchestra" — a role it would retain through the entire Golden Age.

Landmark recordings: **"Boedo"**, **"Tierra querida"**, **"Mala junta"** (co-composed with Pedro Laurenz), and **"Recuerdo"** (1926) — a composition by the young Osvaldo Pugliese that De Caro's sextet first recorded.

De Caro's career was prolific: **over 420 works** recorded, performed for the Prince of Wales (April 1925), toured Rio de Janeiro commanding 2,000 pesos per night (1927), and in 1935 debuted at Argentina's Teatro Colón. On December 11, 1977 — his birthday, shared with Carlos Gardel — the Argentine government declared **National Tango Day**.

### The De Caro School and Its Legacy

The musicians who passed through De Caro's ranks formed a direct bridge to the Golden Age:

- **Aníbal Troilo** (1914–1975) played in De Caro's later formations
- **Osvaldo Pugliese** (1905–1995), whose first hit "Recuerdo" was recorded by De Caro's ensemble
- The direct line runs from De Caro through Troilo, Pugliese, Salgán, and ultimately to Piazzolla

Harvard's *ReVista* states plainly: **"The tango decade of the 40s, the gilded age of tango, was mounted on De Caro's legacy."**

## The Bandoneon Finds Its True Voice

By 1920, the bandoneon had displaced the flute as tango's signature instrument. But its artistic potential was unlocked during the Guardia Nueva by three extraordinary players.

**Eduardo Arolas** (February 24, 1892 – September 29, 1924), "El Tigre del Bandoneón," was self-taught. He created **octave phrasing**, passages harmonized in thirds, and "rezongos" (a grumbling bass-note effect). His compositions — over 100, including **"Derecho Viejo"** (1916), **"La Cachila"** (1918), and **"Comme il Faut"** (1920) — displayed astonishing modernity. Arolas was Julio De Caro's artistic godfather, giving the young violinist his first orchestra spot in 1917. But personal tragedy sent Arolas into alcoholic self-exile, and he died of tuberculosis in a Paris hospital on September 29, 1924, alone, aged 32.

**Pedro Maffia** (August 28, 1899 – October 16, 1967) suffered a brutal childhood but made arguably the most important single development in bandoneon history: he **"found in the bandoneon those dark sounds which separated the bandoneon from the flute forever."** Before Maffia, bandoneonists had tried to imitate the bright, staccato flute they replaced. Maffia discovered the instrument's own lyrical, dark, introspective voice. He became the **first professor of the bandoneon chair** at the Conservatorio Superior de Música Manuel de Falla in 1954.

**Pedro Laurenz** (October 10, 1902 – July 7, 1972), "El Cadenero" (the lead horse), brought vibrant rhythmic precision and passionate phrasing. For a legendary period, the duo of **Maffia and Laurenz — "Los dos Pedritos"** — played together and recorded duets for Victor. Laurenz's 1937 recording of "Arrabal" has been called the recording that **"inaugurated the true golden age of tango."**

## From Sextet to Orquesta Típica

The standard sextet (*sexteto típico*) — two bandoneons, two violins, piano, and double bass — was codified in the early 1920s through Vicente Greco, Francisco Canaro, and Roberto Firpo.

The expansion beyond six players happened gradually:
- Larger dance halls required more volume
- Radio broadcasting created demand for richer sound
- The recording industry rewarded sonic variety

By the end of the era, the standard **orquesta típica** had expanded to **3–4 violins, 3–4 bandoneons, piano, and double bass** — the instrumentation that would power the Golden Age.

## Carlos Gardel: The Voice That Defined an Era

If De Caro gave tango its orchestral architecture, **Carlos Gardel** gave it a human soul. No figure in tango history has matched his cultural impact.

### Origins

The **scholarly consensus** holds that he was born **Charles Romuald Gardès on December 11, 1890**, at the Hôpital de La Grave in Toulouse, France, to Berthe Gardès, an unmarried laundress. His birth certificate was located and confirmed in 2012.

Berthe Gardès brought her son to Buenos Aires aboard the SS *Don Pedro*, arriving on **March 11, 1893**. They settled near the Abasto market, where Gardel grew up absorbing opera, zarzuela, and criollo music. Mentored by folk singer **José Betinotti**, who nicknamed him **"El Zorzal Criollo"** (The Creole Thrush), Gardel dropped out of school in 1906 to sing professionally.

### The Invention of Tango Canción

The pivotal moment came on or around **January 3, 1917**, when Gardel performed **"Mi noche triste"** at the Teatro Esmeralda. The song — music by Samuel Castriota, lyrics by **Pascual Contursi** — was revolutionary because before it, tangos had been either purely instrumental or bore only trivial, bawdy lyrics. Contursi wrote the first serious emotional narrative set to tango. Gardel's recording sold **10,000 copies** — a hit across Latin America.

Biographer Simon Collier called it **"a decisive moment in the history of South American popular music, the moment when the tango song as such was born."**

### Peak Years and Global Stardom

His recording output was extraordinary: between **800 and 957 recordings of some 792 different songs**, spanning 29 musical genres, with **514 tangos** specifically. His voice was inscribed on the **UNESCO Memory of the World Register in 2003**.

In **September 1928**, he debuted in Paris and sold **70,000 records in his first three months** there. His **eight Paramount features** between 1931 and 1935 made him the first great mass idol of Latin America.

His creative partnership with **Alfredo Le Pera** (June 7, 1900 – June 24, 1935) produced tango's most famous songs: **"Volver"** (1934), **"Por una cabeza"** (1935), **"Mi Buenos Aires querido"** (1934), **"El día que me quieras"** (1935).

### Medellín, June 24, 1935

At approximately **2:51 PM**, Gardel's Ford Trimotor aircraft collided with a taxiing aircraft at Medellín airport. The explosion killed **17 people**, including Gardel, Le Pera, and guitarists Guillermo Barbieri and Ángel Domingo Riverol.

Gardel's remains traveled by ship through New York (where they lay in state for over a week), Rio de Janeiro, and Montevideo, before arriving in Buenos Aires on **February 5, 1936**. One hundred mounted police accompanied the coffin through streets filled with **tens of thousands of mourners**.

The phrase **"Gardel cada día canta mejor"** (Gardel sings better every day) captures the essential truth: preserved forever at the peak of his powers, he became tango's eternal voice.

## The Orchestra Leaders Who Built Tango's Infrastructure

**Francisco Canaro** (November 26, 1888 – December 14, 1964), "Pirincho," rose from extreme poverty — he built his first violin from an oil can — to become tango's greatest businessman-musician. He recorded an astonishing **approximately 3,800 tracks**, making him the most recorded tango artist in history. Canaro **pioneered the estribillista format** around 1924–1927 and was instrumental in founding **SADAIC** (the Argentine Society of Composers and Songwriters) in 1935.

**Roberto Firpo** (May 10, 1884 – June 14, 1969) definitively established the piano in the tango orchestra and was the **first arranger to write note-for-note parts** for each musician. His recording of **"La Cumparsita"** for Odeon in November 1916 would be adapted approximately **2,700 times** and become the traditional final dance at milongas worldwide.

**Osvaldo Fresedo** (May 5, 1897 – November 18, 1984), "El Pibe de La Paternal," developed the **"aristocratic" style** — refined, elegant, melodic. He introduced the vibraphone, harp, and discreet drums to tango. His recording career spanned an extraordinary **63 years** (1917–1980), producing over **1,250 recordings**.

## The Lyricists Who Gave Tango a Literary Soul

**Pascual Contursi** (1888–1932) opened the door with "Mi noche triste," but the generation that followed turned tango lyrics into genuine literature.

**Enrique Santos Discépolo** (March 27, 1901 – December 23, 1951) brought philosophical depth and nihilistic social commentary. His masterpieces include "Yira, Yira" (1929) and "Cambalache" (1934), a denunciation of corruption still quoted in Argentine political discourse.

**Homero Manzi** (November 1, 1907 – May 3, 1951) revolutionized the milonga genre with "Milonga Sentimental" (1932) and "Milonga del 900" (1933).

**Celedonio Flores** (1896–1947), a former boxer, saturated his lyrics with lunfardo — Gardel recorded 21 of his compositions, including "Mano a Mano" (1923).

**Enrique Cadícamo** (1900–1999), who lived to 99, brought Symbolist poetry influences with "Anclao en París" (1931) and "Nostalgias" (1934).

## The Social Revolution: Tango Enters the Mainstream

### Records and Radio Reshape Tango's Reach

By 1926, Buenos Aires had two record factories pressing **more than four million discs per year**. In 1925, Argentines bought **500,000 records, 90 percent of which were tangos**.

On **August 27, 1920**, Dr. Enrique Telémaco Susini and three medical students broadcast Richard Wagner's *Parsifal* from the Teatro Coliseo — widely regarded as the **world's first entertainment radio broadcast** aimed at a general public audience. By the mid-1930s, Buenos Aires was home to **25 radio stations** — as many as New York, a city three times its size.

### How the Dance Evolved

The transition from **canyengue** — danced with shared axis, closed embrace, relaxed bent knees — to **tango de salón** was driven by social class. By the 1930s, tango de salón had replaced canyengue as the dominant style. Knees straightened, arms rose, new figures emerged: sacadas, barridos, ganchos, boleos, ochos.

A critical observation: **dancing nearly died out during La Guardia Nueva** because De Caro's musicians "began playing with the phrasing, harmony and the melody instead of focusing on a steady beat." The rebirth of dance tango would come with D'Arienzo in 1935.

---

## Argentina's Golden Decade Turns Dark

Argentina entered the 1920s as **one of the wealthiest nations on earth** — in 1913, richer than France or Germany.

The **September 6, 1930 military coup** shattered this trajectory. General Uriburu overthrew President Yrigoyen, inaugurating the **Década Infame** (Infamous Decade, 1930–1943). The Depression hit Argentina's export-dependent economy hard, with GDP falling approximately **25%**.

Tango responded with social protest: Discépolo's "Yira, Yira" and "Cambalache" captured the era's disillusionment.

## The Transition to the Golden Age

On **July 2, 1935** — just eight days after Gardel's death — **Juan D'Arienzo** entered the RCA Victor studio. His approach was the antithesis of De Caro: where De Caro emphasized complex arrangements and legato, D'Arienzo brought back the older 2/4 rhythm, sped it up, and played everything **staccato**. The beat dominated.

The pivotal moment came on **December 31, 1935**, when pianist **Rodolfo Biagi** replaced Lidio Fasoli. Biagi's driving staccato piano intensified D'Arienzo's rhythmic approach into something irresistible.

Between 1935 and 1939, D'Arienzo recorded **116 sides**, selling more than Canaro and Troilo combined.

As one historian observed: "Had Carlos Gardel not died in a plane crash in 1935; had Juan D'Arienzo not stirred up a revival… the Época de Oro, the golden forties, would likely never have taken place."

---

## What La Guardia Nueva Built

The fifteen years between 1920 and 1935 transformed tango more profoundly than any period before or since:

- **De Caro** invented the orchestral language that every Golden Age orchestra would speak
- **Gardel** created the tango canción and proved tango could tell stories with a human voice
- **Canaro, Firpo, and Fresedo** built the commercial infrastructure
- **Discépolo, Manzi, Flores, and Cadícamo** gave tango a literary voice
- The **orquesta típica format** matured to the full bandoneon-and-string sections
- **Technology** — electrical recording, radio, sound film — carried it into every home

The era's paradox: its greatest musical achievement — De Caro's sophisticated tango — nearly killed tango as a dance. It took D'Arienzo's rhythmic counter-revolution in 1935 to restore the beat and ignite the Golden Age.

Everything that makes Golden Age tango what it is was invented between the New Year's Eve party where the De Caro brothers reunited in December 1923 and the runway in Medellín where Gardel's plane caught fire in June 1935.
