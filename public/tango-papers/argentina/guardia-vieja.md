# La Guardia Vieja: The Birth of Tango in the Rio de la Plata, 1880-1920

**Tango was not born in one city but in a cultural estuary.** Between roughly 1880 and 1920—the period Argentine historian Horacio Ferrer named *La Guardia Vieja* (The Old Guard)—a new musical and dance form crystallized from the collision of African drumming traditions, European immigrant melodies, and criollo guitar culture in the working-class neighborhoods of both Buenos Aires and Montevideo. The 2009 UNESCO inscription formally recognizes this dual origin, naming both cities as the cradle of "the Argentinian and Uruguayan tradition of the Tango."

---

## I. The Geography of Birth: A River Wide Enough for Two Cities

### Buenos Aires and Montevideo as Sister Ports

The Rio de la Plata is not a river but an estuary—**the widest in the world**, stretching roughly 220 kilometers across at its mouth. Buenos Aires and Montevideo face each other across this muddy expanse, separated by approximately 200 kilometers of water. Regular steamship service connected the two capitals throughout the nineteenth century, and the movement of people, music, and culture between them was constant and bidirectional.

The demographics tell a parallel story:
- By 1889, **47% of Montevideo's population was foreign-born**; among males over twenty, the figure reached 78%
- By the 1895 census, **52% of Buenos Aires' inhabitants were foreign-born**, holding at roughly 50% through 1914 when the city reached 1.6 million

The 2009 UNESCO inscription was a joint nomination by Argentina and Uruguay. An earlier 2001 nomination by Argentina alone had failed. The successful resubmission employed the concept of tango as a shared "Rio de la Plata expression."

### Where Tango Took Root in Buenos Aires

The southern neighborhoods formed tango's urban cradle:
- **La Boca**: Overwhelmingly Genoese, conventillos built from scrap metal and ship wood
- **San Telmo**: Became a tenement district after wealthy families fled the yellow fever epidemic of 1871
- **Barracas and Constitucion**: Working-class zones near the port and meatpacking plants

Key venues: Cafe La Pandora in La Boca; the Tancredi (c. 1882); Cafe Hansen; Armenonville (opened 1912 as Buenos Aires' first true cabaret).

### Montevideo's Essential Contribution

Montevideo's **Barrio Sur** and **Palermo** were the historic strongholds of Afro-Uruguayan culture. The most famous conventillo was **Conventillo del Medio Mundo** at Cuareim 1080, built in 1885—until the military dictatorship forcibly evacuated it on December 3, 1978, then demolished it. That date was declared **National Day of Candombe, Afro-Uruguayan Culture and Racial Equality** in 2006.

Two distinctive candombe rhythmic styles are identified by streets in these neighborhoods: the **Cuareim** style (slower, melodic) and the **Ansina** style (aggressive, rapid-fire).

## II. The People Who Made Tango

### The Immigrant Tsunami

Between 1870 and 1914, approximately **5.9 million Europeans migrated to Argentina**, with 3.2 million staying permanently.

**Italian immigrants** dominated—approximately two million settled in Argentina between 1880 and 1920. By 1914, Buenos Aires alone had over 300,000 Italian-born inhabitants. The pidgin language *cocoliche* contributed heavily to *lunfardo*, the slang vocabulary of tango lyrics.

**Spanish immigrants** numbered approximately 1.5 million between 1861 and 1920, carrying a crucial musical cargo: the **habanera**, which traveled the route Cuba → Spain → Rio de la Plata.

The immigration was **overwhelmingly male**. By 1914, Argentina had roughly 100,000 more men than women. This produced the practice of men dancing tango with each other to develop skills, and the deep vein of loneliness that defines tango's emotional landscape.

### The African Foundation That Argentina Tried to Forget

In 1778, roughly **one-third of Buenos Aires' population was of African descent**. By 1836, people of African descent still constituted 26% of the city.

**Candombe** was the musical backbone of these communities. Built around three drums—the high-pitched **chico**, the medium **repique**, and the large **piano**—candombe was not merely entertainment but the sonic architecture of African communal identity in exile.

Scholar **George Reid Andrews** argues persuasively that the apparent vanishing of Black Buenos Aires was largely a statistical illusion produced by census manipulation and deliberate state policy.

**Named Afro-Argentine and Afro-Uruguayan pioneers:**
- **Casimiro Alcorta (~1840-1913)**: Afro-Argentine violinist whom Jose Gobello called "the father of tango." Formed **the first documented tango ensemble** with "El Mulato Sinforoso" and a guitarist
- **Rosendo Mendizabal (1868-1913)**: Afro-Argentine pianist who composed **"El Entrerriano" (1897/1898)**—the first tango published in musical notation
- **Gabino Ezeiza (1858-1916)**: Afro-Argentine payador who explicitly linked milonga to candombe: "milonga was the daughter of African Candombe"

### The Compadritos

The **compadrito** was "the young man of modest social condition who inhabited the outskirts of the city... something like a gaucho who had unsaddled." Descended from gauchos who came to the city as Argentina's frontier closed, compadritos brought rural traditions: the milonga campera, the payada, and a swagger inseparable from early tango culture.

## III. How Four Musics Became One

Tango condensed slowly from at least four parent genres:

1. **Habanera**: The foundational rhythmic cell—a dotted 2/4 pattern with syncopated bass
2. **Milonga**: A lively 2/4 tempo and the competitive vocal tradition of the payada
3. **Candombe**: The physical vocabulary—quebradas, cortes—and rhythmic complexity
4. **Payada**: Tango's lyrical dimension and the boastful performance culture

Guardia Vieja tango was played in **2/4 time**, at a brisk tempo faster than most tango heard today. Musicians mostly improvised. The standard structure had **three sections**. The music was predominantly instrumental.

### From Guitar and Flute to the Bandoneon

The earliest tango ensembles of the 1880s-1890s were typically **trios**: flute, guitar, and violin.

The **bandoneon** was developed by **Heinrich Band (1821-1860)** in Krefeld, Germany. It arrived in the Rio de la Plata **around 1865-1870**, brought by German and Italian emigrants. Because early bandoneonists couldn't keep up with the fast milonga tempo, **the music slowed down to accommodate the instrument**—and this deceleration helped birth tango's distinctive feel.

By the 1910s, the bandoneon was becoming central through players like **Juan "Pacho" Maglio** and **Eduardo Arolas** ("El Tigre del Bandoneon").

### La Cumparsita: Uruguay's Anthem for the World

**Gerardo Matos Rodriguez** composed "La Cumparsita" in late 1915/early 1916 as a carnival march for the Federacion de Estudiantes of Uruguay. Roberto Firpo first recorded it in November 1916. In 1997, Uruguay named La Cumparsita its cultural and popular anthem by law.

## IV. Where Tango Was Danced: Contested Narratives

### The Brothel Story, Examined

Contemporary scholarship challenges the conventional brothel narrative substantially. **Ricardo Garcia Blaya** (Todo Tango) rejects it: "Nothing is more absurd and incorrect. There were no musicians working at brothels."

**Christine Denniston** argues that brothels were where upper-class men first *encountered* tango—not where it was born.

### The Real Geography

Tango was danced in a far wider range of venues:
- **Conventillo patios**: Natural gathering spaces where residents from different backgrounds shared music
- **Academias de baile**: Licensed dance venues—many owned and managed by Afro-Argentines
- **Street corners**: Men dancing together to buskers playing *organitos*
- **Carnival**: A major catalyst; orquestas saved their best new pieces for carnival debuts

On September 9, 1862, police arrested people for dancing "tirando cortes y quebradas" at a conventillo—the earliest known police record of tango movements.

## V. Canyengue: The Body Remembers Africa

### Physical Characteristics

Canyengue was the earliest identifiable tango dance style, danced from approximately the late 1890s through the 1920s:
- **Bent knees**, dancing close to the ground
- **Close embrace** in a V-offset position
- Signature **cortes** (sudden stops) and **quebradas** (breaks—jerking movements bending the spine)
- *Corridas* (small quick runs), *sentaditas* (the woman creating illusion of sitting on man's leg)

The word "canyengue" is of **African origin**, from **Kimbundu** (Bantu family), derived from *candombe* and *yongo*.

### The Modern Revival

The modern canyengue revival owes much to **Luis Grondona** (b. 1938), who "rediscovered the beauty of this original style" in the 1990s. In 2002, **Martha Anton**, **Manolo "El Gallego" Salvador**, **Adrian Griffero**, and **Roxina Villegas** founded **Mo.C.C.A.** (Movimiento Cultural Canyengue Argentino).

## VI. Paris Validation and Tango's Return in Evening Dress

### Tangomania, 1912-1914

By 1912, tango was sweeping Europe. **1913 was called "the year of the tango."** Tango transformed women's fashion: looser, shorter dresses; corsets cast off; "tango orange" became a signature color.

The backlash was spectacular:
- **November 1913**: Pope Pius X declared tango immoral
- **November 17, 1913**: Kaiser Wilhelm II banned officers from dancing tango, calling it "Rinnsteinkind" (child of the gutter)
- Queen Mary of England ordered tango kept out of palace functions

Despite all this, tango remained enormously popular.

### The "Return" from Paris

The original canyengue was sanitized: cortes y quebradas were smoothed or eliminated. Vulgar song titles were changed. The embrace opened. The dance that returned from Europe was more refined, more presentable, and further from its African and working-class roots.

## VII. The First Recordings

The **earliest known tango recording** is Angel Villoldo's "El Choclo," captured on disc **no later than 1906 in Philadelphia**—no recording studios existed in Argentina at that time.

In **1910**, Vicente Greco's Orquesta Tipica Criolla made the first recordings of tangos by an ensemble exclusively dedicated to tango.

Between 1910 and 1920, tango featured on **2,500 of the 5,500 records** released in Argentina—approximately 45% of all recorded music.

### Sheet Music

"La Morocha" sold over 280,000 copies. The Argentine naval frigate **Sarmiento** carried 1,000 copies of "La Morocha" sheet music on its European voyage around 1906.

## VIII. When the Old Guard Gave Way to the New

### The Pivot Points of 1917-1924

The single most consequential moment was the premiere of **"Mi Noche Triste"** by **Carlos Gardel** at the Teatro Esmeralda on **January 3, 1917**. This was the birth of **tango-cancion**—the sung tango with serious, emotional lyrics.

The decisive revolution came from **Julio De Caro**, who founded his Sexteto Tipico in late 1923/early 1924. His innovations:
- Written arrangements replaced improvisation
- Shifted tango from **2/4 time to 4/4 time**
- Made all instruments play both rhythm and melody
- Introduced advanced violin and bandoneon techniques

### What the Transition Cost

- **African rhythmic elements diminished**
- **Improvisation** gave way to written arrangements
- The rough humor and raw energy yielded to refined presentation
- Early instrumentation (guitar, flute, clarinet) was largely replaced

The Golden Age that followed was magnificent, but it was built partly on the suppression of tango's most African, most improvisatory dimensions.

---

## Conclusion: What the Old Guard Left Us

The Guardia Vieja was not a prologue. It was the main event—the period when an entirely new form of human expression was synthesized from African drums, European melodies, criollo guitars, and the desperate creativity of people with nowhere else to go.

What the Guardia Vieja demands of us is honesty:
- Honesty about the African foundations deliberately erased
- Honesty about Montevideo's role, systematically diminished
- Honesty about the brothel narrative, which tells us more about elite prejudice than about where tango was created
- Honesty about what was lost when tango put on evening dress to visit Paris

**The Old Guard created tango. Everything after was variation.**
