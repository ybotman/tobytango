# La Decadencia: The Decline of Argentine Tango, 1955–1983

**Tango did not fade away — it was systematically dismantled.** Between September 1955, when a military coup toppled Juan Perón, and November 1983, when a show called *Tango Argentino* stunned Parisian audiences at the Théâtre du Châtelet, the most sophisticated social dance in the Western Hemisphere was driven to the edge of extinction. What had been a citywide culture of hundreds of orchestras, thousands of dancers, and milongas in every neighborhood of Buenos Aires was reduced to a scattered handful of aging men and women dancing in hidden venues, sustained by memory alone. This is the story of those twenty-eight years — of political persecution, cultural upheaval, and the stubborn few who refused to let tango die.

The destruction was not the work of a single villain. It was the cumulative result of six successive military governments, an imported rock and roll revolution, economic devastation, and a generation that decided their parents' music was beneath them. By the late 1970s, tango's most important orchestras had disbanded or buried their leaders. Its dance halls had shuttered. Its young had never learned. And yet — astonishingly — a thread survived. The knowledge persisted in the bodies of old milongueros, in crates of deteriorating 78 RPM records, and in the unbroken stubbornness of an imprisoned Communist named Osvaldo Pugliese, whose orchestra played on without him while a red carnation sat on his empty piano.

## I. The fall of Perón and the night the music stopped

On **September 16, 1955**, a military uprising launched from Córdoba under **General Eduardo Lonardi** began the overthrow of President Juan Domingo Perón. After five days of fighting — at naval bases, garrisons, and airfields across the country — Perón wrote a letter hinting at resignation on September 19 and fled aboard a Paraguayan gunboat on September 22. On **September 23, 1955**, Lonardi was sworn in as de facto president, delivering his famous balcony address: *"Ni vencedores ni vencidos"* — "Neither victors nor vanquished."

The conciliation lasted fifty-one days. Lonardi, a Catholic nationalist who had avoided banning Peronism outright, was deposed in an internal palace coup on **November 13, 1955**, by the hardliner **General Pedro Eugenio Aramburu**. Under Aramburu, what followed was not merely a change of government but a comprehensive cultural purge. The regime called itself the *Revolución Libertadora* ("Liberating Revolution"); Peronists called it the *Revolución Fusiladora* ("Firing Squad Revolution") after the execution of sixteen soldiers and thirteen civilians in the failed June 1956 counter-coup.

The centerpiece of de-Peronization was **Decree Law 4161/56**, enacted on **March 5, 1956**. It banned Peronist symbols, photographs of Perón and Eva Perón, their names in print, the Justicialist Party flag, and specifically prohibited two musical works: the *Marcha de los Muchachos Peronistas* and *Evita Capitana*. Violations were punishable by thirty days to six years in prison. Merely shouting Perón's name became, as anthropologist Antonius Robben observed, "a small act of resistance."

### Why the regime viewed tango with suspicion

Perón had cultivated tango as a national symbol throughout his presidencies (1946–1955). He promoted it as *"an authentic expression of the people"* and milked it, as one historian put it, for its street credibility. Under Perón, tango flourished: approximately **fifty major orchestras** performed nightly across Buenos Aires at salons, cafés, cabarets, and the social-sports clubs (*clubes de barrio*) — River Plate, Huracán, Atlanta, Independiente — where every neighborhood's youth learned to dance. Radio broadcast tango constantly; records were affordable; the culture was universal.

For the post-Perón military, tango was indelibly stained by this association. It was the music of the *descamisados* (the "shirtless ones," Perón's working-class base). It was populist, urban, working-class, and — crucially — it brought large groups of men together in enclosed spaces at night, which to the military mind suggested political conspiracy. The regime did not formally ban tango. It did something more effective: **it eliminated the conditions under which tango could exist.** Curfews killed the nightlife. Bans on public gatherings shuttered the dance halls. Censorship targeted songs using *lunfardo* (Buenos Aires working-class slang), songs sympathizing with workers, and songs that could be read as political commentary. The male-only tango practice sessions, where young men had trained for years before debuting at milongas, were classified as "public disturbances."

As one tango educator later summarized: *"It wasn't that the military banned tango; rather that they eliminated the night life through curfews and harassment."* The effect was the same. A tango historian captured the demographic cliff with stark precision: **"If you were 18 in 1955, you probably learned to dance tango well. If you were 13, not at all."**

### Pugliese's imprisonments — the red carnation on the piano

No figure better embodies tango's persecution than **Osvaldo Pugliese** (December 2, 1905 – July 25, 1995), the great bandleader who was jailed by every faction in Argentine politics for half a century. Pugliese joined the Communist Party in **1936** — party membership number 108 — after reading about the Spanish Civil War in the newspaper *Crítica*. His political convictions made him a target for governments of every stripe.

His first arrest came around **1939**, during the *Década Infame*, while preparing to debut his orchestra at the inauguration of the new Communist Party headquarters. Writer Enrique Medina noted that *"the exact reason Pugliese was imprisoned the first time was never known."* He was treated respectfully, offered mate, and released after a few days — his orchestra debuted shortly after at Café Nacional.

Under Perón's government (1946–1955), the paradoxes multiplied. Pugliese's Communist politics earned him blacklisting and a restriction to approximately **ten recordings per year**, even as several of his orchestra members — singer Alberto Morán, first violinist Enrique Camerano — were avowed Peronists. In 1948, police began canceling his contracts; in 1949, at a Carnival dance at Club Atlanta, the club received a telegram citing the *"official prohibition"* on Pugliese performing. In **January 1955**, he was arrested at the confectionery *La Comedia* and held for **six months at Villa Devoto prison**, until **July 10, 1955**. During his imprisonment, he refused privileges, insisting on cleaning corridors with mop and bucket alongside other inmates. His daughter Beba recalled that he acquired the habit of drinking mate *amargo* (bitter, without sugar) during this period — a small act of solidarity with ordinary prisoners.

Each time Pugliese was jailed, his orchestra played on. A **red carnation (*clavel rojo*)** was placed on the piano — the *símbolo de ausencia* (symbol of absence), an idea attributed to Negro Mella, the orchestra's presenter. People wrote on walls throughout Buenos Aires: **"El tango está preso"** — "Tango is taken captive."

After Perón's fall, the anti-Peronist military proved equally hostile. In **1956**, during *"Operación Cardenal"* (Operation Cardinal), Pugliese was arrested alongside the entire Central Committee of the Communist Party and imprisoned aboard the ship *París*, which served as a floating jail. Rumors circulated that authorities planned to sink the ship with prisoners aboard. His orchestra fulfilled a scheduled broadcast on Radio Splendid that night — without their pianist, with a red carnation in his place.

Under President Frondizi, Pugliese narrowly escaped arrest during the **Plan CONINTES** (c. 1958–1961), which placed leftist protesters under military tribunal jurisdiction. Aided by fellow militants, he evaded police through what comrades called *"la astucia de militante"* — the cunning of a lifelong organizer.

In one celebrated incident, police arrived at a venue where Pugliese was performing. When told the venue would have to close, the club owners protested that they couldn't interrupt while the orchestra was playing. Pugliese, informed of the police presence, began playing *La Cumparsita* — the universal tango closer — and then played it again. And again. The audience kept dancing. The police eventually gave up and left. [HITM REVIEW: The exact date and venue for this anecdote vary across sources and may be apocryphal or a composite of multiple incidents.]

### The economic blow and generational rupture

The coup's impact extended beyond politics. Perón's labor protections — nationalized industries, wage increases, vocational training, the welfare state apparatus — were systematically reversed. The Aramburu regime intervened in and occupied labor unions, dissolved the *Confederación General Económica* (CGE), and pursued economic liberalization. For the working-class families who had constituted tango's core audience, disposable income and leisure time shrank simultaneously.

The clubes de barrio, which had sustained tango as a neighborhood institution, now faced both economic pressure and government harassment. Many closed or converted to other uses. The economics of large tango orchestras — twelve or more musicians requiring steady bookings — became untenable as venues disappeared.

Into this vacuum stepped a foreign sound. **Rock and roll reached Argentina in 1956** via radio. Eddie Pequenino became the first Argentine to record rock and roll that year, with Lalo Schiffrin's orchestra, and his debut single sold over **27,000 copies**. The first rock concert on Argentine soil came in **May 1958**, when **Bill Haley** performed at the Metropolitan Theater in Buenos Aires. Elvis Presley's music followed closely. The *nueva ola* (new wave) of the early 1960s — the TV show *El Club del Clan*, Palito Ortega, Sandro (the "Argentine Elvis") — gave young Argentines pop idols singing in Spanish. A dancer named Vera recalled the mentality of her generation: *"It was something pretty that my parents and grandparents, old people, danced. Me? Tango? Never! I wouldn't have been caught dead."*

## II. The long decline: when tango became a ghost, 1960–1976

### Piazzolla shatters the mold — and splits tango in two

**Astor Piazzolla** (March 11, 1921 – July 4, 1992) had studied with Nadia Boulanger in Paris in 1954–55 on a French government scholarship. Boulanger, hearing his tango *Triunfal*, gave him his artistic directive: *"This is Piazzolla. Don't ever leave him."* He returned to Buenos Aires in 1955 and formed the **Octeto Buenos Aires** — two bandoneons, two violins, cello, double bass, piano, and electric guitar — which broke the mold of the traditional *orquesta típica* entirely. This was chamber music with tango's DNA, incorporating jazz improvisation and classical counterpoint. It was nuevo tango.

The traditional tango community responded with fury. Orthodox *tangueros* called Piazzolla **"el asesino del tango"** — the tango killer. Record companies and radio stations boycotted him. He was physically attacked on more than one occasion. Even his former mentor, Aníbal Troilo, had earlier told the young Piazzolla his arrangements used "too many notes." The community polarized into *piazzolistas* and *antipiazzolistas*, a schism that persists in milder form today.

Piazzolla himself articulated the philosophical break: his music was, he declared, **"a music to listen to and not to dance."** This was not merely aesthetic preference — it was a rejection of the social function that had defined tango for seventy years. For traditional milongueros, this was apostasy. For concert audiences, it was liberation.

Unable to find sustained work after the Octeto dissolved around 1958, Piazzolla moved to **New York (1958–1960)**, where he worked as an arranger. He returned to Buenos Aires in 1960 to form his **First Quintet**, which became the defining ensemble of nuevo tango through the decade. After suffering a heart attack in **1973**, he relocated to **Rome**, where Italian publisher Aldo Pagani offered him a fifteen-year contract. Based in Italy, he recorded **"Libertango" in Milan in May 1974** — the track that would become an international sensation — and *Summit* with Gerry Mulligan that September. He formed his **Second Quintet in 1978** and toured worldwide for eleven years, achieving the recognition abroad that Argentina had long denied him. He did not return permanently to Buenos Aires until approximately **1985**.

### The giants fall silent

The Golden Age orchestras that had defined tango's greatest period did not all disappear at once. Their leaders died, one by one, across two decades — and with each death, irreplaceable knowledge went to the grave.

**Carlos Di Sarli** (January 7, 1903 – January 12, 1960) died first, at only fifty-seven, in Olivos, Buenos Aires. His last recordings — fourteen tracks for Philips with singers Horacio Casares and Jorge Durán — had been made in **1958**. His elegant, lyrical style, built on a flowing piano lead, ended with him. No successor could replicate it.

**Francisco Canaro** (November 26, 1888 – December 14, 1964), the most prolific recording artist in tango history, followed four years later. **Miguel Caló** (October 28, 1907 – May 24, 1972) died suddenly in 1972, having briefly reunited his famous 1940s lineup as the *Orquesta de las Estrellas* in 1961. **Aníbal Troilo** (July 11, 1914 – **May 18, 1975**), the beloved "Pichuco," died of a stroke and cardiac arrest at Buenos Aires's Italian Hospital. He had recorded almost continuously until the end; his final album, *Bandoneón Mayor de Buenos Aires vol. 2*, was released in 1975. In his later years, he had shifted from dance music to a concert sound and formed a quartet with Ubaldo de Lío, Osvaldo Berlingieri, and Rafael del Bagno — smaller ensembles reflecting the new economic reality.

**Juan D'Arienzo** (December 14, 1900 – **January 14, 1976**), "El Rey del Compás" (The King of the Beat), recorded his last sessions in **January 1975** after an exclusive forty-year contract with RCA Victor — producing approximately **one thousand recordings**. Despite the decline around him, D'Arienzo maintained audiences through sheer rhythmic force. He re-recorded *La Cumparsita* seven times across his career, the final version in 1971. He never performed outside Argentina and Uruguay — famously refusing an invitation from the Emperor of Japan because he feared flying. D'Arienzo died exactly one month after his seventy-fifth birthday, **two months before the March 1976 coup**.

The staggering fact bears repeating: **Troilo and D'Arienzo died within eight months of each other**, in 1975 and early 1976, just as Argentina's darkest chapter was beginning. Of the "Big Four" — Di Sarli, D'Arienzo, Troilo, Pugliese — only Pugliese survived into the post-dictatorship era.

Several orchestras persisted in diminished form. **Osvaldo Fresedo** (1897–1984), one of tango's longest-serving musicians, continued recording until **1980** — a sixty-year recording career — before retiring. **Horacio Salgán** (1916–2016), who would live to one hundred, maintained his duo with guitarist Ubaldo de Lío and performed with the **Quinteto Real**, which he had co-founded in 1960. **Enrique Mario Francini** (1916–1978) played in a sextet at Caño 14 in the 1970s and toured Japan with Armando Pontier in 1973, but died in 1978. The **Sexteto Mayor**, founded in the late 1970s by bandoneonists José Libertella and Luis Stazo, would become critically important as a bridge ensemble, eventually providing music for the *Tango Argentino* show.

By **1980**, the number of functioning full *orquestas típicas* in Buenos Aires had dwindled to the single digits. Pugliese's was the most prominent. The economics that had once sustained **hundreds of working orchestras** during the mid-1940s peak had completely collapsed.

### The 78 to LP transition and vanishing record sales

The recording industry's shift from 78 RPM shellac to vinyl LP records happened slowly in Argentina, complicated by Perón-era protectionism and slow economic growth. **Music Hall** was the first label to release tango on vinyl, issuing Di Sarli recordings as 7-inch records in **February 1952**, but consumer resistance forced a partial reversion to shellac. Odeón began issuing vinyl in 1953, and full-size 12-inch LPs emerged by the late 1950s. The transition took nearly a decade.

As formats changed, tango's market share shrank. While precise sales figures are elusive, the qualitative evidence is stark: the "For Export" series that D'Arienzo, Troilo, and others recorded for RCA Victor repositioned tango as a nostalgia product for international markets — primarily Japan, where tango music remained popular — rather than a living domestic genre. Labels did not stop recording tango entirely, but investment shifted decisively toward rock, folklore, and cumbia.

### The dance nearly dies

During tango's peak, Buenos Aires had supported an enormous infrastructure of dance venues. Downtown alone featured approximately **nine major *confiterías bailables*** (dance halls with recorded music) — Montecarlo, Siglo XX, Domino, La Nobel, La Metro, Picadilly, La Cigalle, Sans Souci, Mi Club — open from five in the afternoon until four in the morning. The neighborhoods were even richer: **Sin Rumbo** and **Club Sunderland** in Villa Urquiza, **Club Floreal** in La Paternal, **Club Almagro**, **Glorias Argentinas**, **Club Vélez Sársfield**, **Club Unidos de Pompeya**, and dozens more.

An ethnographic study of Buenos Aires milongas documented the collapse: *"During the '60s and '70s, these clubs gradually stopped holding dances, although in some specific cases dances continued to be organized sporadically."* The downtown confiterías disappeared first. Neighborhood clubs followed, converting to rock nights, tropical music, or simply shutting their doors.

The milongueros — the men and women who had dedicated their lives to social tango — faced an impossible choice. Dance or conform. Nearly all of them stopped.

**Ramón "Finito" Rivera** (March 24, 1929 – May 11, 1987) had been one of the finest social dancers in Buenos Aires, learning at Club Floreal in La Paternal. He worked as a mechanic; tango was his life outside work. Around **1954–55**, as rock and roll invaded and tango's status collapsed, Finito stopped dancing. His wife María Teresa confirmed the duration: **twenty-six years** of silence. He did not return to the milonga floor until **1980**, at Club Vélez Sársfield. María Teresa recalled: *"He believed that with so many years without dancing, new things would have been invented or something would be different. But it was the same. He looked at me, smiled, and said: 'But we're still like the first day.' We went out to the floor and he was inspired. Everything came out."* The legendary Virulazo was present that night and began asking about "that skinny guy who's killing it." From that night until his death — of a heart attack at the Akarense club on May 11, 1987, moments after finishing a tango — Finito did not stop again.

**Ernesto Norberto "Pupi" Castello** (December 18, 1935 – July 23, 2007) stopped dancing for **twenty to twenty-five years** before the early 1980s, giving family as his principal reason. **Carlos Pérez** (b. approximately 1940), who had begun dancing at thirteen in 1953, gave exhibitions at Club Pinocho, Glorias Argentinas, Viento Norte, and Sin Rumbo through the late 1950s. He stopped entirely upon getting married in the early 1960s and did not return for **thirty years** — until **1994**, at the urging of his friend José Vázquez "Lampazo." He would later be called *"el maestro de los maestros"* (the teacher of the teachers), tutoring a new generation at Club Sunderland. [HITM REVIEW: The widely repeated claim that Carlos Pérez worked as a gravedigger during his hiatus could not be verified in any documented source and may derive from oral tradition.]

**Osvaldo Cartery** (1939–2015) reportedly took a **thirty-year break** from tango, consistent with the general pattern, though exact dates of his hiatus could not be independently confirmed. [HITM REVIEW: Specific dates for Cartery's break require verification from primary interview sources.]

The case of **Pedro Alberto "Tete" Rusconi** (January 9, 1936 – January 7, 2010) is more nuanced. In a 2005 interview with the French magazine *Tout Tango*, when asked if he stopped dancing after the 1950s when rock displaced tango, Tete answered directly: *"No, no, I always continued tango and I took up rock, boogie with passion. The milongas, by the way, continued to play tango among the rocks and rumbas."* This suggests that while the milonga circuit collapsed, some venues continued mixing tango with other genres, and Tete found ways to keep dancing — though the nature and frequency of his dancing during the worst years remains unclear.

The reasons for the collective retreat were multiple and reinforcing. No venues. No partners — women stopped coming to milongas. Social stigma — the word *milonguero* carried negative connotations, signaling a womanizer with no job. Family responsibilities, as the generation that learned in the 1940s married and had children. Economic pressure. And the destruction of the transmission mechanism: the male-only *práctica* (practice sessions), where young men had trained for up to three years before dancing at milongas, was eliminated by bans on public gatherings.

As Rosa Forte, Carlos Pérez's wife, put it: *"In that era, tango had a bad reputation in Argentine society. A woman who danced tango was considered by good society to be a woman of the streets."*

### Rock, folk, and cumbia claim the youth

The cultural replacement of tango was swift and total. **Rock Nacional** — Argentine rock in Spanish — emerged as a self-conscious movement in **1967** with **Los Gatos'** single *"La Balsa,"* considered the first true Argentine rock anthem. **Almendra**, founded that same year by Luis Alberto Spinetta, and **Manal** completed what Argentines call the founding trilogy.

**Sui Generis**, formed in **1971** by **Charly García** and Nito Mestre, became the first mass phenomenon of Argentine rock. Their albums — *Vida* (1972), *Confesiones de Invierno* (1973), *Pequeñas Anécdotas sobre las Instituciones* (1974) — each sold over **200,000 copies**. Their farewell concert on **September 5, 1975**, drew 20,000 to Luna Park Stadium, the largest audience in Argentine rock history to that point. **Serú Girán** (García, David Lebón, Pedro Aznar, Oscar Moro), formed in **1978**, encoded critiques of the military dictatorship in metaphor — *"Canción de Alicia en el país"* used *Alice in Wonderland* imagery to describe political terror — and peaked in 1980–82 before Aznar's departure.

The folklore revival ran parallel. **Atahualpa Yupanqui** (Héctor Roberto Chavero, January 31, 1908 – May 23, 1992), who had been censored under Perón for his Communist affiliations, settled in Paris in 1967 and became an international icon. **Mercedes Sosa** (July 9, 1935 – October 4, 2009), la voz de América, broke through nationally at the **1965 Cosquín Folklore Festival** and embodied the *nuevo cancionero* movement. After the 1976 coup, her music was banned from radio. In **1978 or 1979** [HITM REVIEW: exact date uncertain], she was arrested onstage during a concert in La Plata — military men burst into the auditorium, searched her publicly, and detained her and all two hundred audience members. She went into exile in **February 1979**, first to France, then Spain.

**Cumbia** filled the working-class dance void that tango left behind. **Los Wawancó**, the foundational cumbia group in Argentina, formed in La Plata in **1955** — the same year as the coup — founded by Latin American immigrants. Through the 1960s and 1970s, cumbia became the *movida tropical*, offering accessible, democratic rhythms that anyone could dance to, without tango's years-long apprenticeship or its freighted social codes.

By the mid-1970s, tango was irrelevant to nearly everyone under forty. As one tango instructor summarized: *"There was an enormous cultural difference between young people and their parents that was marked very clearly."*

## III. The Dirty War and cultural destruction, 1976–1983

On **March 24, 1976**, a military junta overthrew the chaotic government of Isabel Perón. The junta consisted of **Lieutenant General Jorge Rafael Videla** (Army), **Admiral Emilio Eduardo Massera** (Navy), and **Brigadier General Orlando Ramón Agosti** (Air Force). They named their regime the *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional* — the "Process of National Reorganization" — and launched what became known as the Dirty War.

The regime's targets extended far beyond leftist guerrillas. Students, professors, journalists, artists, trade unionists, lawyers, and anyone perceived as ideologically suspect were detained, tortured, and killed. An estimated **30,000 people were forcibly disappeared** (the figure used by human rights organizations; the official CONADEP investigation documented 8,961 cases and acknowledged the true number was higher). At least **340 secret detention centers** operated across the country. Victims were subjected to torture, and thousands were drugged and thrown from aircraft into the South Atlantic or the Río de la Plata in *vuelos de la muerte* — death flights. As many as **500 children** born to imprisoned mothers were taken and given to military families.

### Nightlife under terror

For tango, the dictatorship delivered the final blow to an already weakened culture. **Gatherings of three or more people in the street were prohibited.** A **curfew** was imposed — reportedly at **10:00 PM** — though enforcement varied. [HITM REVIEW: The 10 PM curfew is cited by tango dancers Rodolfo and Gloria Dinzel in a 1997 Christian Science Monitor article; the exact hours may have varied over time and by location.] Police document-checking at any surviving nightlife venue was routine. As dancer Adriana Vera recalled: *"Persecution, mandatory curfew, a lot of caution, a lot of trembling, a lot of fear."*

Crucially, the enforcement was **selective**. Historian Christine Denniston documented that laws banning minors from nightclubs were rigidly enforced at tango venues but routinely ignored at rock clubs. Tango teacher Marcelo Solis confirmed that rock venues, which generated higher profits, *"could afford to bribe government officials"* — with the bribe sometimes formally included in the event budget. The military appeared to tolerate rock, with its individualistic, bedroom-listening culture, while viewing tango's collective, late-night, working-class gatherings as inherently suspect. Some Argentines have argued that the promotion of American rock on radio was a deliberate strategy to suppress nationalist populism, though this remains an interpretation rather than a documented policy.

### What remained of tango

During the junta's years, tango was neither formally banned nor actively cultivated — it was rendered nearly impossible. The question of where someone could dance tango in Buenos Aires in 1978 yields an unsettling answer: **almost nowhere**, in any organized public sense.

A few neighborhood clubs apparently held sporadic dances mixing tango with rock, cumbia, and tropical music. Tete Rusconi's testimony that *"the milongas continued to play tango among the rocks and rumbas"* suggests that some venues preserved tango as one option among several, rather than as a dedicated event. **Sin Rumbo** in Villa Urquiza maintained some activity and would re-emerge as an important milonga space in the 1980s. Professional tango survived in **show venues** catering to tourists — **Caño 14**, **Casa Blanca**, **Michelangelo**, **Viejo Almacén** — where musicians like Roberto Goyeneche and Oscar Alemán still performed. But these were performances, not social dances.

Otherwise, tango retreated into **private homes and clandestine gatherings** — small, hidden, and scared.

Pugliese, by now a living symbol of resistance, continued to perform under severe constraints. His Communist politics made him a prime target, yet he survived the Dirty War unscathed physically — *"He was simply too popular"* to disappear, as dancer Juan Carlos Copes observed. The honorific **"San Pugliese"** (Saint Pugliese), a superstitious good-luck invocation among musicians of all genres, testified to his totemic status. His orchestra, organized as a cooperative with equitable income distribution, maintained extraordinary loyalty: core members including Osvaldo Ruggiero, Enrique Camerano, and Alcides Rossi stayed for approximately thirty years.

Carlos Rivarola, who performed in tango shows during this period, recalled the atmosphere: *"Of those around me, they took the son of our neighbor, and a guy from my primary school disappeared without trace… I don't know of tango dancers who suffered that destiny, but many actors were never seen again."* [HITM REVIEW: Whether any tango dancers or musicians were among the desaparecidos has not been documented in accessible sources, though the general targeting of artists and cultural figures makes it plausible.]

Among the very few professional dancers who kept working through the entire dark period were **Juan Carlos Copes** (May 31, 1931 – January 16, 2021) and his partner **María Nieves**. Copes, a pioneer of *tango escenario* (stage tango), had debuted on Broadway at the Alvin Theatre in 1962 and appeared on The Ed Sullivan Show. Through the 1960s and 1970s, he performed in tourist-oriented cabarets and worked with Piazzolla on the choreography for *María de Buenos Aires* (1968). **Jorge Martín Orcaizaguirre, "Virulazo"** (October 10, 1926 – August 2, 1990), and his partner **Elvira Santamaría** also persisted, though Virulazo later described the hardship bluntly: *"The hard times came in the '60s… we danced for pennies. We alone endured, Juan Carlos Copes and I. Bohemianism is fun, but you starve."*

## IV. The survivors who kept tango alive

### The few who never completely stopped

Identifying who danced continuously through the entire twenty-eight-year decline is extremely difficult. Most well-known milongueros from the Golden Age generation have testified to hiatuses of twenty to thirty years. **Tete Rusconi** is the strongest candidate for continuity among social dancers, having explicitly stated he "always continued." Among professional performers, **Juan Carlos Copes** and **Virulazo** maintained some level of activity, though both experienced periods of severe economic hardship.

Rick McGarrey, the American tango researcher who spent years filming over a hundred milongueros at tangoandchaos.org, wrote that *"some of them stubbornly kept dancing tango while everyone was home watching television and hiding from the proceso militar."* These anonymous holdouts — unnamed, undocumented — are the truest survivors.

### Preservation through music and memory

Pugliese's orchestra remained the most significant musical continuity. The **Sexteto Mayor**, formed in the late 1970s, provided another living thread. Smaller ensembles like **Buenos Aires 8** (a vocal octet recording jazz-influenced tango arrangements), **Los Tubatango** (an eccentric quartet using tuba, bandoneon, flute, and guitar, founded 1967), and fusion artists like **Rodolfo Mederos** (whose 1976 album *Fuera de Broma* blended tango with rock) maintained creative output during the years of silence.

Bandoneón player **Néstor Marconi** later described the musicians' reality: *"We reached a very low point in the 1980s when to provide food on the table, we had to play broken-down holes we never would have set foot in before."*

### Institutional preservation

**Horacio Ferrer** (June 2, 1933 – December 21, 2014), the Uruguayan-born poet, broadcaster, and tango historian, published the first edition of ***El Libro del Tango: Arte Popular de Buenos Aires*** in **1970**, followed by an expanded edition exceeding **2,000 pages** in 1980. These encyclopedic reference works — compiled during the very years of tango's collapse — preserved an enormous body of historical knowledge. Ferrer had co-founded **El Club de la Guardia Nueva** in 1954 to organize concerts for avant-garde tango musicians and first met Piazzolla in Montevideo in 1955. His collaboration with Piazzolla produced *Balada para un Loco* (1969) and the operita *María de Buenos Aires* (1968). Ferrer would go on to found the **Academia Nacional del Tango** by presidential decree on **June 28, 1990** — the culmination of decades of preservation work, installed at the Palacio Carlos Gardel on Avenida de Mayo 833, above the historic Café Tortoni.

Private record collectors played an unsung role. Thousands of 78 RPM recordings from the Golden Age survived in private hands, maintained by dedicated archivists who recognized their historical value. Discographers compiled meticulous catalogs of every session, every take, every pressing. The website **TodoTango.com** eventually digitized much of this heritage, but its roots lie in the analog dedication of collectors who preserved recordings during the decades when no one else cared.

The **Academia Porteña del Lunfardo** preserved tango's linguistic heritage. Historians **Luis Adolfo Sierra** and **José Gobello** produced reference works on orchestral history and *lunfardo* dictionaries. These institutional efforts ensured that even when tango could not be danced or heard live, it could be studied, documented, and remembered.

### Estimating tango's nadir

**How many active social tango dancers existed at the absolute lowest point?** No census exists, and no reliable numerical estimate has been published. The qualitative evidence — nearly all major milongueros reporting twenty-to-thirty-year hiatuses, the closure of virtually all dedicated milongas, the prohibition on gatherings, the absence of any new generation of learners — suggests the number was **extraordinarily small, likely in the low hundreds at most across all of Buenos Aires**. [HITM REVIEW: This estimate is an inference from qualitative sources; no published quantitative figure has been located.] Poet Enrique Cadícamo captured the feeling: *"El tango en 1983 era un recuerdo"* — "Tango in 1983 was a memory."

## V. Parallel developments elsewhere

### Europe: Piazzolla concertizes while tango dance barely exists

Organized Argentine tango dance communities in Europe during this period were effectively **nonexistent**. Europeans knew tango only in its ballroom form — the International Style codified by the English in 1922, a rigid, competitive dance bearing minimal resemblance to its Argentine ancestor. Piazzolla's European concerts — at the Istituto Italo-Latino-Americano in Rome (April 1972), the Olympia in Paris (1977), and hundreds of venues across Italy, France, and Germany through the 1970s and 1980s — introduced Europeans to tango as *music* but not as social *dance*.

**Finnish tango** developed in complete isolation as an independent tradition. Tango first reached Finland in **November 1913** via performers Toivo Niskanen and Elsa Nyström. The first Finnish tango was composed by Emil Kauppi in 1914. By the 1930s, Finnish songwriters were setting original lyrics against snowy landscapes in minor keys drawn from Finnish and Russian waltz traditions. Composer **Unto Mononen's** *"Satumaa"* (Fairytale Land, 1955) became arguably Finland's most-performed song. In 1964, a Mononen tango competed with The Beatles' *"All My Loving"* for the number-one chart position. Finnish tango used accordion rather than bandoneon, maintained close body contact (pelvis, thighs, upper body), and featured no kicks or aerials — sharing tango's embrace while diverging entirely in music, mood, and cultural context.

### The United States: ballroom tango and nothing else

American tango descended from **Rudolph Valentino's** wildly stylized portrayal in *The Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse* (1921) — a version codified by the Arthur Murray dance studio chain into fixed step patterns (slow-slow-quick-quick-slow), stiff ballroom frames, dramatic head snaps, and open-position turns. It bore essentially no relationship to Argentine social tango. **There was no Argentine tango dance culture in the United States before the mid-1980s.** As one historian noted, *"In the US only a few cities had tango before 1995."*

### Japan: music collectors, not dancers

Japanese interest in tango music dates to approximately **1914**, when North American tango dancers performed in Yokohama. By the 1920s, Japanese writers and performers were traveling to Argentina in search of tango's origins, and records were being imported avidly. **Abo Ikuo** (1937–2021), a legendary Japanese tango singer who sang phonetically in Spanish with remarkable fidelity, performed with the Orquesta Típica Masaichi Sakamoto in the 1960s and eventually recorded **120 albums**. The **Orquesta Típica Tokio**, led by Hayakawa Shinpei, gave concerts in Argentina as early as the 1950s. Yet Japanese tango enthusiasts primarily engaged with the music as listeners and collectors, not dancers. Argentine tango dance did not establish a presence in Japan until after the worldwide revival. [HITM REVIEW: According to academic research by Dr. Yuiko Asaba (SOAS), Japanese dancers initially legitimized British-style tango and rejected Argentine-style dance; this ethos persisted until the late twentieth century.]

## VI. Seeds of revival, 1980–1983

### The junta collapses

On **April 2, 1982**, the military junta — now led by **General Leopoldo Galtieri** — invaded the Falkland Islands (*Islas Malvinas*) in a desperate bid to rally nationalist support amid hyperinflation exceeding **200% annually** and growing awareness of human rights atrocities. The British dispatched a task force. Seventy-four days later, on **June 14, 1982**, Argentine forces surrendered at Port Stanley. The defeat was catastrophic: **746 Argentine soldiers killed**, 1,336 wounded, 11,400 captured. Galtieri was forced to resign on June 17.

The cultural consequences were immediate and paradoxical. The junta's ban on English-language music from radio — intended as wartime nationalism — **gave Rock Nacional unprecedented airplay**, accelerating the genre's dominance. Mercedes Sosa returned from exile in **1982**, performing a legendary series of concerts at the Teatro Colón that were recorded as *Mercedes Sosa en Argentina* and became an instant bestseller. The military's credibility was shattered. Cultural self-censorship began to lift.

### Finito returns — before democracy

In **1980** — three years before democracy was restored, in the depths of the dictatorship — Ramón "Finito" Rivera walked into **Club Vélez Sársfield** with his wife María Teresa and danced tango for the first time in twenty-six years. *"He did everything,"* María Teresa recalled. *"Virulazo was there and began asking about 'that skinny guy who's killing it.'"* Finito's return was not prompted by political change. It was, apparently, simply an irresistible pull — the body remembering what the years could not erase. Rick McGarrey of tangoandchaos.org would later call Finito *"absolutely the best of all."*

Other milongueros began to re-emerge in the early 1980s. Pupi Castello returned to the milongas. The precise timing of each dancer's return is poorly documented, but the pattern is consistent: a trickle before 1983, accelerating after democracy was restored.

### *Tango Argentino* — the show that saved a dance

In **1974**, Argentine theater director **Claudio Segovia** (August 31, 1933 – December 21, 2025) and set designer **Héctor Orezzoli** first conceived a stage production that would present authentic tango — not the ballroom caricature, but the real thing, performed by the people who had lived it. The project gestated for nearly a decade. In 1980, they tested the concept with *Flamenco Puro* in Seville, after two years of research in Andalucía. When the director of Paris's Théâtre du Châtelet requested an original production, Segovia returned to Buenos Aires to find the survivors.

The casting was an archaeological excavation. Juan Carlos Copes and María Nieves — both in their early fifties, having danced together for over thirty years — served as leads and choreographic advisors. **Nelson Avila and Nélida Rodriguez de Aure** were recruited from the Michelangelo tango show in San Telmo. **Mayoral and Elsa María** represented the elegant salon style of the milongas. And Virulazo — who by 1983, at age fifty-seven and weighing **126 kilograms**, had abandoned dance entirely and was working in *quiniela* (illegal gambling) — was persuaded by Copes to audition. Segovia was incredulous when Virulazo arrived. Copes told him: *"Just watch them dance."* When Virulazo and Elvira Santamaría moved, Segovia could not believe *"that such a man seemed to float, he didn't touch the ground."* The **Sexteto Mayor** provided the music.

The company flew to Paris on **an Argentine military transport cargo plane** — sitting on the floor like freight, with no services, on a minimal budget.

On **November 11, 1983** [HITM REVIEW: the premiere date is cited as November 10 in some Spanish-language sources and November 11 in the Bibliothèque nationale de France catalog; the discrepancy is one day], *Tango Argentino* opened at the **Théâtre du Châtelet** as part of the twelfth Festival d'Automne à Paris. It ran approximately six performances. Every show sold out. At the final performance, organizers opened the theater's doors so people standing outside could listen. One Argentine exile told Segovia, in tears: *"I spent thirty years hating the tango, and in reality life is a tango."*

Exactly twenty-nine days later — on **December 10, 1983** — **Raúl Alfonsín** was inaugurated as president of Argentina, ending seven years of military dictatorship. Democracy returned. Cultural programs began. On **December 15, 1983**, Alfonsín created **CONADEP**, the National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons, which would produce the devastating *Nunca Más* report.

The show transferred to **Broadway on October 9, 1985**, at the Mark Hellinger Theatre, running 199 performances and earning Tony nominations including Best Musical and Best Choreography. It won Best Musical Show of the Year from the Japanese Association of Music Writers, beating Michael Jackson and Madonna.

The irony was inescapable. Tango's revival was sparked not in Buenos Aires but in Paris. Validated in Europe and America, young Argentines finally began studying the dance of their grandparents. As Orezzoli commented bitterly when invited to bring the show to the Teatro Colón: *"Their values really haven't changed toward the tango or anything that is authentic or identifies us."*

On **December 26, 1985**, Osvaldo Pugliese — age eighty, survivor of every government that Argentina had produced in half a century — finally performed at the **Teatro Colón**, fulfilling his mother's lifelong dream. He received **five standing ovations**. He told the audience: *"The truth is… it's a night of the people, of the masses, lovers of our genre, our beloved genre, tango."*

## VII. What was lost and what survived

### The losses were staggering

A **full generation** — everyone who turned thirteen after 1955 — never learned to dance tango in the organic, immersive way their parents had. The male-only *práctica* system, where apprentices spent years mastering the lead before ever dancing with a woman at a milonga, was extinguished. The *orquesta típica* format — twelve or more musicians playing to a room of dancers, adjusting in real-time to the energy of the floor — became economically impossible and then functionally extinct. Specific arrangement styles died with their creators: Di Sarli's flowing piano lead, Troilo's breathing *fraseo*, Canaro's rhythmic architecture existed on recordings but not in living ensembles. The dance style called **canyengue**, an older, lower-center-of-gravity form, was nearly forgotten. The art of managing a milonga — proper *tandas* and *cortinas*, the *cabeceo* (eye-contact invitation), seating protocols, the entire architecture of *códigos* (social codes) — survived only in the memories of aging practitioners.

The professional losses were equally severe. Bandoneón player Néstor Marconi described playing "broken-down holes" to feed his family. Virulazo went from international stages to illegal gambling. Carlos Pérez left dance entirely for thirty years. Countless anonymous musicians retired, changed professions, or died without passing on the specific techniques — the way a bandoneon section breathes together, the particular attack of a violin section, the feel of the *compás* — that had been transmitted orally and physically for generations.

### What survived against all odds

**Recordings survived** — thousands of 78 RPM discs, later supplemented by LP reissues, preserved in private collections by dedicated archivists. These recordings became the DNA from which tango would be reconstructed, providing the musical foundation for the revival. The quality and completeness of these collections varied enormously; some recordings of major orchestras exist only in single surviving copies.

**Surviving milongueros preserved the dance in their bodies.** Though few in number, the men and women who had learned during the Golden Age retained the *feeling* — the connection, the musicality, the embrace — even after decades of inactivity. When Finito returned after twenty-six years, it was, as his wife said, "the same." The knowledge was physical, stored in muscle memory and instinct, and it proved remarkably durable. A partial list of the Golden Age–era dancers who survived into the revival includes: **Ricardo Vidort** (1929–2006), **Pupi Castello** (1935–2007), **Tete Rusconi** (1936–2010), **Alberto Dassieu** (1936–2013), **Osvaldo Centeno** (1937–2017), **Osvaldo Cartery** (1939–2015), **Ruben Harymbat** (1939–2015), **Oscar "Cacho" Dante** (b. 1939), and many others. These men and women — born in the 1930s, trained in the 1940s and 1950s — constituted the living bridge between the Golden Age and the modern era.

**Pugliese's orchestra** survived as a functioning cooperative for over fifty years, touring internationally (Japan, China, the Soviet Union, France, Finland, and throughout Latin America) even as the domestic scene collapsed. The **Sexteto Mayor** provided musical continuity in the late 1970s and 1980s. Salgán and De Lío's decades-long partnership maintained the sophisticated end of tango musicianship.

**Institutional knowledge survived** through Ferrer's encyclopedic publications, the writings of Sierra and Gobello, and the archival work of collectors and discographers. The Academia Porteña del Lunfardo preserved tango's linguistic heritage. These efforts meant that even if the living tradition had been completely severed — which it very nearly was — the documentary record existed for reconstruction.

### The twenty-eight-year gap and its implications

The break in continuous tradition — roughly **1955 to 1983** — remains the central fact of modern tango. It means that today's tango, however authentically it is practiced, is a **reconstructed art form**, reassembled from recordings, from the testimony of elderly survivors, and from the bodily knowledge of milongueros who had not danced for decades. The organic chain of transmission — master to student, *práctica* to milonga, neighborhood to neighborhood — was broken.

This gap animates virtually every contemporary debate about tango authenticity. When dancers argue about what constitutes "real" tango, they are arguing about whether a tradition can survive a twenty-eight-year interruption and remain the same tradition. The answer, as Finito's return suggests, is both yes and no. The feeling survived. The institutional infrastructure, the social fabric, and the intergenerational transmission did not. What was rebuilt after 1983 is both continuous with the Golden Age and irrevocably different from it.

As Tete Rusconi said: *"Tango is not a business even though many ones think it is. Tango is a part of our life, a part of our grandparents, fathers, mothers, brothers, sisters and friends. It is our life."* He died on January 7, 2010 — two days before his seventy-fourth birthday — having danced at the El Beso milonga on Corrientes the evening before.

**What role did economics vs. politics vs. generational change play?**
All three were decisive and mutually reinforcing. Politics (curfews, gathering bans, censorship) eliminated the infrastructure. Economics (loss of labor protections, declining working-class leisure time, unsustainable orchestra costs) destroyed the financial base. Generational change (rock and roll, cultural rejection of tango as old-fashioned) eliminated demand. No single factor alone would have produced the catastrophe; together, they were devastating.

Pugliese, through sheer stubbornness and Communist discipline. Juan Carlos Copes and María Nieves, through professional stage performances. Virulazo and Elvira, until poverty forced them out. ## Conclusion: the near-death that defines modern tango

The story of La Decadencia is not a story of gradual decline but of systematic destruction followed by improbable survival. Six military governments in twenty-eight years — each hostile to the conditions tango required — reduced a universal urban culture to a clandestine remnant. Rock and roll, folklore, and cumbia offered young Argentines alternatives that did not require late-night gatherings, years of practice, or association with a discredited political movement.

Yet tango's near-death gave rise to two developments that define its modern form. First, the revival was ignited **from abroad** — the Paris premiere of *Tango Argentino* in November 1983 — creating the internationalized tango culture that now spans sixty countries. Second, the revival depended on **a handful of survivors** whose authority derived not from formal credentials but from lived experience, making the modern tango world permanently preoccupied with questions of authenticity, lineage, and the relationship between old and new.

The last twenty-eight years of tango's first century were the years it almost died. The next forty were the years it came back — changed, globalized, debated, but alive. The red carnation is no longer needed on the piano. But it should never be forgotten.
