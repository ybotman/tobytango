# Rodolfo Cieri

The keeper of canyengue. Rodolfo Cieri preserved and revived tango's oldest surviving dance style — the playful, low-slung canyengue his father taught him before age ten. After a two-decade hiatus for family life, he returned to the milongas in the 1980s with his wife María Topa. Together they became icons of historical tango styles, demonstrating orillero based on childhood memories, touring Europe, Canada, and the United States, and inspiring "several generations" of dancers to rediscover tango's roots.

---

## Biography

### Early Life in La Paternal

Born October 30, 1932 in Buenos Aires, Cieri grew up in the La Paternal neighborhood. His father introduced him to tango before age ten, teaching him the canyengue style — the low, playful, syncopated dance that predated modern salon tango.

### Learning Canyengue

Cieri danced at milongas in La Paternal with family members during his youth. The canyengue his father taught him was already considered old-fashioned, but Cieri absorbed the style completely, preserving technique that would otherwise have been lost.

### The Decisive Statement

After secondary school, Cieri "sealed his fate with a sentence: 'I want to be a dancer.'" Despite this declaration, family responsibilities would pull him away from tango for two decades.

### Twenty-Year Hiatus

Marriage and raising a family interrupted his tango involvement. During these years, tango itself went through its darkest period — the 1960s and 70s when the dance nearly disappeared.

### Return to the Milongas (1980s)

In the 1980s, Cieri resumed dancing with his wife María Topa. They appeared at venues including Volver on Corrientes and Suipacha, bringing their distinctive canyengue style to the revival era.

### Revival of Orillero (~1990)

Around 1990, Cieri revived the orillero style with demonstrations based on childhood memories of dance steps he learned from his father. Together with María, he gave this forgotten dance style a fresh impetus. Although their repertoire was limited, they became icons of the orillero style — restoring the historical value of this expressive and unique form.

### International Teaching

The couple performed professionally throughout Europe, Canada, and the United States. They recorded instructional video lessons, ensuring their knowledge could reach dancers worldwide.

## Dance Styles

### Canyengue

The oldest surviving tango style, characterized by:
- Low, bent-knee posture
- Close embrace with offset position
- Playful, syncopated rhythms
- Cortes and quebradas (cuts and breaks)

Cieri learned this directly from his father, making him one of the last authentic sources.

### Orillero

Tango danced in the outskirts (*orillas* or *arrabales*) of Buenos Aires, developed during the 1920s-1930s. Orillero is playful and expressive, often danced on the edges of the floor. By the end of the 1940s, it had largely ceased to be practiced — until Cieri revived it.

---

## Legacy

Cieri and María became "great maestros" and models of the canyengue style. He possessed "the skill, the wit and the capacity to surprise several generations."

Their work gave a new dimension to modern tango while restoring the historical value of these expressive and unique styles. Without dancers like Cieri who preserved living memory of early tango, these styles might have been lost entirely.
