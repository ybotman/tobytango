# Pepito Avellaneda

The neighborhood legend. José Domingo Monteleone, known as Pepito Avellaneda, embodied his barrio. He began dancing at twelve during Avellaneda Carnival and made his debut at Teatro Roma at fifteen. He was a true milonguero — distinguishing improvised social dancing from choreographed performance. In his 50s, he became one of the first Argentine dancers to teach in Europe, establishing schools in Switzerland, Germany, and Holland. A pizza seller by day, a philosopher of tango by night.

---

## Biography

### Avellaneda Roots (1930)

Born José Domingo Monteleone on November 30, 1930, in Avellaneda — the industrial neighborhood across the Riachuelo from Buenos Aires proper.

### Dancing from Childhood (Age 12)

He began dancing at age twelve during Avellaneda Carnival celebrations, learning in the streets and social clubs of his neighborhood.

### Teatro Roma Debut (1945)

At fifteen, he made his first public performance at Teatro Roma, launching a career that would span half a century.

### The Milongas of Buenos Aires

In his youth, he danced at the main tango bars of Buenos Aires:
- Picadilly
- Sans Souci
- Montecarlo
- And many more

### Taking His Stage Name (~1957-58)

Around 1957-58, he adopted the name "Pepito Avellaneda" to better represent his neighborhood identity while gaining broader recognition.

### Teaching Internationally (Age 56+)

At 56, Pepito became one of only a handful of Argentines going to Europe to teach in the 1990s. He established schools in:
- **Switzerland** — Basel
- **Germany** — Hamburg, Stuttgart
- **Holland**
- **Canada** — Toronto, Montreal

He noted that "European people like to have lessons in the morning" and maintained a rigorous daily teaching schedule.

### International Argentine Tango Congress (1995)

In June 1995, he traveled to Evanston, Illinois, to teach with Suzuki at the International Argentine Tango Congress at Northwestern University.

### Death (1996)

Pepito died on April 29, 1996, just months after his teaching trip to the United States.

## Philosophy

### Milonguero vs. Choreographer

Pepito distinguished between choreographed dancers and true *milongueros* (social dancers). He emphasized improvisation and authentic leadership — the essence of social tango.

### Musical Preferences

| Orchestra | His View |
|-----------|----------|
| Juan D'Arienzo | Rhythm-driven, for energetic dancing |
| Carlos Di Sarli | Poetic, slower interpretations |
| Osvaldo Pugliese | Poetic, slower interpretations |
| Aníbal Troilo | Appreciated |
| Astor Piazzolla | Genius, preferred earlier recordings |

## Style

Pepito was known for:
- **Vals** — Cheeky but endearing style
- **Milonga** — Similarly playful approach
- **Improvisation** — True milonguero dancing
- **Authentic leadership** — Traditional follower-leader dynamic

---

## Other Work

Alongside his dancing career, Pepito worked as a pizza seller. He also sang informally with his father, particularly enjoying the compositions of Enrique Santos Discépolo.

---

## Legacy

His legacy continues through his students and the steps he taught. His approach to tango is still preserved and studied by dancers worldwide.

Graciela González, Giselle Anne, and many other Bridge generation dancers learned from Pepito — absorbing the Guardian generation's embodied knowledge before it was lost.
