# Aníbal Carmelo Troilo — "Pichuco"

The "Supreme Bandoneón of Buenos Aires," Aníbal Troilo was the soul of Golden Age tango. His orchestra became the definitive dance band of the 1940s, and his compositions — "Sur," "La última curda," "Barrio de tango" — remain essential to every milonga playlist. July 11, his birthday, is now National Bandoneón Day in Argentina. He discovered and nurtured talent, gave Astor Piazzolla his first arranging job, and created a sound so distinctive that dancers worldwide still recognize "Troilo" within the first few bars.

> "It is said that I am very often moved and that I cry. Yes, it is true. But I never do these things for trivial reasons."

---

## Biography

### Childhood in Abasto

Aníbal Carmelo Troilo was born on July 11, 1914, in the Abasto neighborhood of Buenos Aires — the same barrio that had been home to Carlos Gardel. As a young boy, he heard the sound of the bandoneón coming through the doors of numerous cafes and taverns.

### The First Bandoneón

At age 10, Troilo's mother Felisa went to a local shop and bought him a bandoneón for 140 pesos, to be paid off in monthly installments. This investment changed the history of tango.

### Child Prodigy (1925–1930)

At age 11, in 1925, Pichuco played his first public performance in a bar near El Abasto market. At 14, he formed his first quintet. His talent was unmistakable from the start.

### Apprenticeship with the Masters (1930–1937)

- **December 1930**: Joined the Elvino Vardaro-Osvaldo Pugliese sextet
- **1931 and beyond**: Played with multiple orchestras including Juan Maglio, Juan D'Arienzo, and Ángel D'Agostino

This period exposed Troilo to different styles and approaches, shaping his future sound.

### His Own Orchestra (1937)

On July 1, 1937, Troilo debuted his own orchestra at the Marabú nightclub. The orchestra would become one of the most popular with social dancers during the Golden Age (1940–1955).

### Recording Career (1938–1971)

- **March 7, 1938**: First recording as leader for Odeon label
- **March 4, 1941**: Resumed recordings with Victor label featuring Francisco Fiorentino
- **1939–1944**: Astor Piazzolla played in and arranged for Troilo's orquesta típica
- **1955–1956**: Memorable quartet recordings with guitarist Roberto Grela
- **1968**: Formed Cuarteto Aníbal Troilo
- **1970**: Recorded historic duets with Astor Piazzolla
- **June 24, 1971**: Final recording — 449 total renditions over his career

### Leadership Philosophy

Troilo's long-lasting success was based not only on his immense talent, but on his skill for discovering talent, forging it, and giving talented artists sufficient room to experiment and find their unique artistic expression.

> "He dug an undoubtedly tango style, balanced, without histrionics and of undeniable taste. He knew how to choose the best players according to his musical ideas."

### Evolution to Concert Sound

While his orchestra was among the most popular with social dancers during the Golden Age, Troilo changed to a concert sound by the late 1950s, adapting to the changing times while maintaining his essential artistic vision.

### Death and Legacy

The "Supreme Bandoneón of Buenos Aires" died on May 18, 1975, at the Italian Hospital of Buenos Aires, from a stroke and subsequent cardiac arrest. He is buried in the "Rincón de los Notables" (Famous Corner) of La Chacarita cemetery, next to Agustín Magaldi and Roberto Goyeneche.

In 2005, the National Congress of Argentina declared July 11 (Troilo's birthday) **National Bandoneón Day**, commemorating one of the nation's most celebrated musicians.

## The Orchestra

### Notable Singers

Troilo's orchestra featured some of the greatest voices in tango history:

- **Francisco Fiorentino** — defining voice of early Troilo recordings
- **Alberto Marino** — powerful interpretation
- **Floreal Ruiz** — lyrical style
- **Edmundo Rivero** — dramatic baritone
- **Jorge Casal** — distinctive phrasing
- **Raúl Berón** — emotional depth
- **Roberto Goyeneche** — "El Polaco," later became legendary soloist

### Notable Musicians

- **Astor Piazzolla** — bandoneonist and arranger (1939–1944)
- **Roberto Grela** — guitarist for quartet recordings

## Style & Philosophy

### The Troilo Sound

Troilo created a balanced, unhistrionic style of undeniable taste. His orchestra was distinctively for dancing during the Golden Age — rhythmic, melodic, and emotionally expressive without excess.

### On Emotion

> "It is said that I am very often moved and that I cry. Yes, it is true. But I never do these things for trivial reasons."

### On His Life's Purpose

When asked about himself, Troilo responded that he is "a good person, well acquainted with sadness, and a person with a great ambition: to be counted as having made something important of his life."

### Nurturing Talent

Troilo gave talented artists room to experiment and find their unique expression. This philosophy allowed him to attract the most talented singers and musicians to his ensemble — including the young Astor Piazzolla, who later revolutionized tango.
