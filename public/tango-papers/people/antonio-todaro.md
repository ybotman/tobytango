# Antonio Todaro

The tailor of tango. Antonio Todaro shaped an entire generation of professional dancers during tango's darkest years and into its renaissance. Working as a bricklayer by day, he never stopped teaching — running academies through the 1960s and 70s when tango had "practically shrunk to a fringe cult." His students read like a who's who of stage tango: Miguel Ángel Zotto, Milena Plebs, Roberto Herrera, Gustavo Russo. His influence runs through almost everyone who performs or teaches in the world today.

---

## Biography

### Early Life in Buenos Aires

Born September 12, 1929 in the Chacarita neighborhood, Todaro grew up moving through Buenos Aires barrios — Parque Chacabuco, then Mataderos from age 13. He worked as a bricklayer and briefly assisted "Virulazo" selling vegetables in San Juan.

### First Competition Victory (1949)

In 1949, Todaro won a dance competition at Club Marplatense in Lanús. This victory launched his professional career — he began performing in confectioneries and variety shows while continuing his day job as a mason.

### Teaching Through the Dark Age

Unlike many who abandoned tango during the 1960s-70s decline, Todaro never stopped organizing training sessions in different salons. He taught at the school of Lita and José Méndez at a young age, developing his methodology.

### Partnership with Raúl Bravo (1964–1993)

In 1964, Todaro partnered with Raúl Bravo. Together they established academies throughout Buenos Aires:

- Misiones and Rivadavia
- Rojas Street
- Nazca Street
- **Jujuy and Carlos Calvo** (primary location)
- Belgrano Street (2259 and Potosí)
- Sarmiento 2950

In 1993, Bravo honored Todaro with a commemorative plaque celebrating 15 years of collaboration.

### The 1980s: Training a Generation

In the 1980s, Todaro became the essential teacher for young professional dancers. It appears almost no exhibition dancer trained in the eighties who did not study with him. His assistant Martha Antón joined in the early 1980s.

### European Influence (1983–1994)

In 1983, Todaro began touring Europe — Germany, Holland, Switzerland, Belgium, Paris. In 1986, he received the first Dutch contingent of 22 students in Buenos Aires. He continued teaching three times weekly in Buenos Aires while making extended European workshop tours until shortly before his death.

## Teaching Style

### "The Tailor of Tango"

Todaro was known as "the tailor of tango" for his ability to customize choreographies to each dancer's abilities and style. He did not teach a rigid system — he fitted tango to the individual.

### Precise and Complex

His dancing was based around precise and complex figures and embellishments — dips, kicks, boleos, ganchos, side leans, reverse sacadas, and jumps. These movements were designed for theatrical presentation rather than crowded social dance floors.

### Bridging Eras

Todaro combined salon and fantasy tango choreography, connecting the traditions of the 1940s with the emerging 1980s performance style. He revolutionized stage dance beyond what was seen in milongas.

### Equal Partnership

Unlike some predecessors, Todaro elevated both partners' roles equally — a progressive approach that influenced modern tango pedagogy.

## Philosophy

### On Tango as Art

> "Tango as a dance is the most beautiful thing that exists. It must be approached with force, with a lot of affection..."

### Dedication Through Decline

Todaro's greatest contribution may have been his persistence. When others abandoned tango during its cultural nadir, he continued teaching, preserving technique and vocabulary that might otherwise have been lost.

---

## Legacy

Half the people you meet from the generation that followed the milongueros — the ones who dance well but learned tango in classes — were taught by Todaro. His influence runs through people like Celia Blanco and Miguel Zotto to almost everyone who performs or teaches in the world today.

He created "a brilliant, beautiful and entertaining tango" by synthesizing movements observed in clubs into a cohesive system. His approach defines much of what modern audiences envision when imagining tango.
