# José "El Turco" Brahemcha

Co-creator of the Villa Urquiza style, José "El Turco" Brahemcha was one of the most intellectually rigorous dancers in tango history. Between 1945 and 1956, working alongside Luis "Milonguita" Lemos, he developed a style characterized by elegant rotation, connection to the ground, and refined musicality. His two principles — "Never dance for the audience but for your dancing partner" and "Don't dance to everything, but only to what you feel" — became foundational teachings for generations of dancers.

---

## Biography

### Early Life

José Brahemcha was born on July 17, 1931, in Buenos Aires. He earned the nickname "El Turco" (The Turk) likely due to his family's Middle Eastern heritage, a common pattern among Argentine-Lebanese and Armenian immigrants.

### Learning to Dance (mid-1940s)

Brahemcha began dancing in the mid-1940s at Asociación de Fomento y Cultura Villa Cerini in the Saavedra neighborhood. He practiced with his uncle at Club Huracán in Villa Martelli, developing his foundational technique before finding his true artistic direction in Villa Urquiza.

### The Villa Urquiza Revolution (1945–1956)

In Villa Urquiza, El Turco encountered Luis "Milonguita" Lemos, a prodigious dancer whose lively, powerful, and sleek elegance was reshaping tango in the neighborhood. Milonguita's unique approach came from attending various milongas and seeing new things in a tango scene experiencing strong development, yet doing everything he saw differently.

El Turco joined a group of enthusiasts including Eduardo "Perita" Clemente, Miguel Mancini, Reinaldo "El Negrito" Davila, and Juan Luna, all inspired by Milonguita's dancing. Together, they codified and refined what would become the Villa Urquiza style.

### "Opening the Dance"

El Turco was known as someone who "opened the dance" — a strong intellectual of dancing and movement in space. He used existing rotating structures to move rotations in space and create figures with enormous dynamics. He had an amazing ability to synthesize, giving the style a polished and simple functional elegance in line with modernity.

### Musical Preferences

El Turco favored melodic tangos, particularly those by Osvaldo Pugliese. His energetic dance style mixed suspension (flowing, held movements) with rhythmic footwork (percussive, grounded steps), making him especially skilled at interpreting dramatic bandoneon arrangements.

### Partnership with Nelly Acosta

El Turco performed and taught with his dance partner Nelly Acosta, becoming a respected instructor in Villa Urquiza's tango community. He left dancers with "the beautiful and versatile entry to the ocho" — a technical contribution still taught today.

### Teaching Career

As an instructor and mentor, El Turco trained subsequent generations of tango dancers. Many contemporary tango stars view him as one of the most important sources of the dance. He advocated for dancing with intention and selection:

> "Don't dance to everything. But only to what you feel, to what touches you."

### The Right Moment

According to El Chino Perico, El Turco always looked for the right moment to take to the dance floor — it was important to him to dance comfortably. In this philosophy, the Urquiza style avoids stepping onto a too-full dance floor, and an unkempt ronda with disregarded dance direction can be a reason not to dance, also out of respect for the partner.

### Death

José "El Turco" Brahemcha died on April 15, 2010, in Buenos Aires, leaving behind a style that continues to be danced in milongas worldwide, especially in the neighborhood of its origin.

## The Villa Urquiza Style

### Characteristics

El Turco and Milonguita developed a style distinguished by:

- **Elegant walking**: Precision, sobriety, and finesse, always gliding close to the floor
- **Musical sensitivity**: Much finer listening to the music than other styles
- **Upright posture**: Slightly more upright than milonguero style, though exchange still occurs through torsos
- **Closed embrace**: During walking, with flexibility for ochos and turns
- **Figures**: Sacadas, arrastres, ganchos, boleos — but not volcadas or colgadas
- **Rotation in space**: Using rotating structures to create dynamic figures

### Philosophy

The Urquiza style embodies selectivity and respect:
- Don't dance on overcrowded floors
- Respect the ronda and dance direction
- Dance for your partner, not the audience
- Only dance to music that moves you
