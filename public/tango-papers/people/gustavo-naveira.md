# Gustavo Naveira

Argentine tango dancer, teacher, and theorist, widely regarded as the "Godfather of Argentine Tango" and co-founder of tango nuevo. In the 1990s, Naveira organized the legendary Cochabamba Investigation Group that deconstructed tango to its fundamental elements, transforming how the dance is taught worldwide. With his partner Giselle Anne since 1995, he continues to teach and perform internationally from their base in Boulder, Colorado.

---

## Biography

### Early Life

Gustavo Naveira was born on August 12, 1960, in Comodoro Rivadavia, in the Patagonian province of Chubut, Argentina. His family moved to Buenos Aires when he was one year old. His father was a professional tango dancer who immersed young Gustavo in tango culture from an early age.

A formative moment came at age 12, when Naveira witnessed his father dance with a stranger at a wedding reception. This experience planted the seed for his later dedication to understanding the dance at its deepest level.

### Education and First Teachers (1981)

Naveira began formal tango training around 1981, studying with Rodolfo Dinzel and his wife Gloria — his first teachers. He distinguished himself as an exceptional student, and Dinzel worked him particularly hard. During this period, Naveira met Olga Besio at the University of Buenos Aires's folklórico ballet program. She became his practice partner and later his wife.

### First Teaching and Argentine Democracy (1983)

Naveira began teaching tango in 1983 with Olga Besio. He credits Argentina's return to democracy that year — and President Raúl Alfonsín's cultural center initiative — as pivotal to tango's resurgence. The new government offered free classes in cultural centers across Buenos Aires. Naveira's tango classes at these centers attracted unprecedented enrollment, sometimes exceeding 500 students, far surpassing offerings like photography or guitar.

### The Cochabamba Investigation Group (1990s)

In the early 1990s, Naveira organized what would become known as the Cochabamba Investigation Group (later Tango Investigation Group). Working with Fabián Salas and inviting dancers trained in other techniques — classical, modern, contemporary — they applied principles of dance kinesiology to analyze the physics of tango movement.

Their revolutionary approach: instead of teaching sequences, they deconstructed tango to its minimum units — its building blocks. From these fundamental elements, dancers could compose their own movement rather than memorizing choreography.

As Naveira described his method:

> "In a way, that is what I have been doing ever since — sitting there with that paper and pencil, analyzing and synthesizing, looking for the structure underneath the social dance that surrounded me."

The Investigation Group's work generated what contemporaries began calling "tango nuevo" — though Naveira himself has always been critical of the term.

### Senior Ballet and Family

With Olga Besio, Naveira founded and directed the "Senior Ballet," a tango group that performed throughout Buenos Aires. They had two children together: Ariadna and Federico Naveira, both of whom became gifted tango dancers in their own right.

### Partnership with Giselle Anne (1995–present)

Naveira met Giselle Anne in 1995 at a tango festival in Sitges, Spain, where they performed together in a show. They formally established their professional partnership in 1999 and began developing their joint methodology — what would later be documented in the 2021 film *El Método Naveira*.

The couple moved to Boulder, Colorado, where they established the Boulder Tango Studio. They have two children together.

## Style & Philosophy

### The Analytical Approach

Naveira's signature contribution is his analytical methodology. Rather than teaching steps or sequences, he breaks tango down to fundamental units of movement — weight transfers, pivots, dissociation — from which dancers can construct any combination.

> "His colleagues credit him with being the first to understand the dance, analyzing it and stripping it down to its minimum units. Through this path of research, Gustavo Naveira developed his own style and an original pedagogy."
> — Wikipedia

### On Technique

> "Not only about learning to put the foot or to learn a certain step. The technique is also learning how to follow the music's rhythm. You have to know every orchestra's style in order to react — that is to say, all this is a learning process which takes time."

### On Tango as Life Philosophy

In a 2004 interview with *Página 12*, when asked why so many people outside Argentina want to study tango:

> "It's a dance that offers you a number of answers to the problems of modern life... and it's a guide to communication that goes far beyond language and culture."

### On "Tango Nuevo"

Naveira has consistently rejected the use of "tango nuevo" as a style label. In his influential 2009 essay:

> "There is great confusion on the question of the way of dancing the tango: call it technique, form, or style. The term tango nuevo is used to refer to a style of dancing, which is an error... In reality, tango nuevo is everything that has happened with the tango since the 1980s."

> "The words tango nuevo express what is happening with tango dancing in general; namely that it is evolving."

### Recognition in Buenos Aires

Critics in Buenos Aires recognized his impact early:

> "You could say that three stylistic tendencies contend for supremacy: Urquiza's style, Almagro's style and Naveira's style."
> — *Clarín*, August 8, 1999

## Notable Quotes

### On the Investigation Group

> "We started working with Chicho — with Fabián and me — and we said, 'Well, we have to get him to really join us in this work, or else we have to break his legs!' He did crazy things no one else could do!"

### On Chicho Frúmboli

> "With only six months of study, he was amazing as a tango dancer."

> "Chicho was absolutely the best dancer I ever saw."

### On Tango's Revival

Naveira emphasizes that tango's 1980s revival in Argentina stemmed from grassroots interest within Argentina — the democratic opening and cultural centers — rather than being triggered by international shows like *Tango Argentino*.
