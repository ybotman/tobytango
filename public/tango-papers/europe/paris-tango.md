# Paris as Tango's Recurring Savior: Two Waves That Changed Everything

Twice in the twentieth century, tango crossed the Atlantic to Paris and returned home transformed. The first time, between 1907 and 1914, a raw, scandalous dance from the docks and tenement houses of Buenos Aires conquered Europe's cultural capital and gained the respectability that Argentina's own elite had denied it. The second time, on November 11, 1983, a group of aging milongueros — some rescued from butcher shops and gambling dens — took the stage at the Théâtre du Châtelet and saved their art form from extinction.

Both times, Parisian validation triggered a global chain reaction. Both times, the dance that returned to Buenos Aires was different from the one that had left.

---

## Part I: The First Tangomania (1907–1914)

### How Tango Crossed the Atlantic

Tango did not arrive in Paris on a single ship or in a single year. It seeped across the ocean through multiple channels — phonograph recordings, sheet music, traveling musicians, and above all, the sons of Argentina's cattle barons making their obligatory European tours.

In **1906**, a thousand copies of "La Morocha" sheet music were distributed at ports worldwide during the ARA Presidente Sarmiento's circumnavigation. In **1907**, **Ángel Villoldo** traveled to Paris alongside guitarist **Alfredo Gobbi** to make phonograph recordings. **Flora Rodríguez** recorded "La Morocha" in Paris — likely the first tango vocal captured on European soil.

The crucial intermediaries were wealthy young Argentine men — the *niños bien* — who encountered tango in Buenos Aires and carried it abroad. As dancer **Maurice Mouvet** recalled: "It was a group of young South American boys who really introduced the Tango. Unable, very often, to find a girl who knew the Tango, I would dance with one of the men."

### 1913: The Year the World Went Tango-Mad

By 1913 — the year H.G. Wells called **"the year of the tango"** — the dance had achieved critical mass. **Magic-City** became the epicenter, offering dance lessons, *thés dansants* three days per week, evening dancing, and holiday balls.

The *thé dansant* — the afternoon tea dance — became tango's primary vehicle for social spread. These events combined tea, sandwiches, and dancing in a setting where smoking and alcohol were prohibited, creating a veneer of respectability that allowed bourgeois women to participate without scandal.

### The Scandal That Made It Famous

The moral panic was swift and international — and only fueled the craze.

- **November 1913**: Pope Pius X declared tango dancing "immoral and off-limits to Catholics"
- **November 17, 1913**: Kaiser Wilhelm II forbade German officers from dancing tango in uniform
- Boston Mayor John "Honey Fitz" Fitzgerald banned tango from city dance halls

On **October 25, 1913**, poet **Jean Richepin** delivered a speech arguing that tango's "impropriety" was attributable to the dancers, not the dance itself — a distinction that gave French society intellectual permission to keep dancing.

### Fashion Revolution

The dance demanded new clothing:
- **Corsets were cast off** — incompatible with tango's close embrace
- **Slit skirts** appeared
- **Tango Orange** — a vivid yellow-orange — became the signature color
- Special **tango shoes** appeared

The convergence with **Paul Poiret's** fashion revolution was striking: Poiret had eliminated the corset starting in 1906, and his looser silhouettes were precisely what tango required.

World War I killed the first tangomania — but tango had already changed the world.

## Part II: The Interwar Thread (1920–1940)

The 1920s and 1930s brought a quieter but deeper Argentine tango presence to Paris.

**Manuel Pizarro**, a bandoneonist, arrived in 1920 and formed the house orchestra at **El Garrón** on rue Fontaine. Together with **Le Palermo**, these venues became "the Mecca of tango in Paris."

**Carlos Gardel's Parisian debut came in September/October 1928**. He **sold 70,000 records in his first three months** in Paris and counted among his friends Charlie Chaplin, Maurice Chevalier, and Mistinguett. Between 1931 and 1933, he made several films at Paramount's Joinville Studios.

### The Ballroom Divergence

The formalization of European ballroom tango occurred in England:
- **1929 "Great Conference"**: Standardized tango at 32–34 bars per minute
- **1934**: Dramatic head snaps introduced under German influence
- The result: two dances sharing only a name

## Part III: The 1983 Revival

### Trottoirs de Buenos Aires Prepares the Ground

On **November 19, 1981**, a tanguería called **Trottoirs de Buenos Aires** opened at 37, rue des Lombards — the first venue in Europe dedicated exclusively to tango performance. Its godfather was **Julio Cortázar**, the great Argentine novelist who had lived in Paris since 1951.

During the Argentine military dictatorship, Trottoirs served as a crucial meeting space for political exiles. Major artists performed: Sexteto Mayor, Roberto Goyeneche, Raúl Lavié.

### Segovia and Orezzoli Imagine the Impossible

**Claudio Gastón Segovia** (1933–2025) and **Héctor Orezzoli** (1953–1991) conceived an authentic tango show — not a sanitized export but the real dance. The obstacles were extraordinary. **No producer would invest**. Segovia's mother financed the initial production using the life insurance payout from his father's death.

### Finding the Cast Among the Forgotten

They wanted milongueros — social dancers who embodied tango's authentic culture:

**Virulazo** — born Jorge Martín Orcaizaguirre — was working at a **butcher shop**, weighing **278 pounds**, age 57. He had quit tango entirely. When he finally danced for Segovia with his partner **Elvira Santamaría**, Segovia "couldn't believe what he was seeing — such a man seemed to float."

**Juan Carlos Copes and María Nieves** were the established headliners — partners since they were teenagers.

The musical ensemble was anchored by **Sexteto Mayor**. **Roberto Goyeneche** sang at the world premiere.

The average age of the cast was **42 years**.

### A Cargo Plane, an Exocet, and a Cold Morning at Orly

The entire cast — approximately **60 people** — flew to Paris on an **Argentine Air Force cargo plane** because they could not afford commercial flights. They landed at the military zone of Orly airport on a cold autumn morning.

### Six Nights That Changed Tango's History

The world premiere occurred on **November 11, 1983**, at the **Théâtre du Châtelet** during the Festival d'Automne. Only **250 tickets** had been sold before opening night. Then it sold out. Then it sold out every night.

**Libération** wrote on November 14, 1983: *"Le tango est entré de nouveau dans Paris"* — "Tango has entered Paris again."

What Parisian audiences saw was unlike anything in their experience. These were middle-aged and older milongueros dancing with an authenticity, intimacy, and improvisational fire that no ballroom competition had ever hinted at. A 278-pound man floated across the stage.

### Broadway and Beyond

After one preview, *Tango Argentino* opened at the **Mark Hellinger Theatre** on **October 9, 1985**. It ran for **198 performances**, grossing **$6.2 million**. Three Tony nominations.

The celebrity audience was staggering: Frank Sinatra, Madonna, Jackie Onassis, Mikhail Baryshnikov.

The show toured for **fifteen years**. It did not arrive in Argentina until **September 16, 1992** — nine years after conquering Paris.

## Why Paris, Both Times

The question demands more than a shrug:

1. **The Buenos Aires–Paris axis was a century old** — Argentina's elite deliberately rejected Iberian influence and turned to France as a model. Buenos Aires earned the nickname *La París de América*.

2. **Paris positioned itself as the arbiter of global culture** — Diaghilev's Ballets Russes (1909), Josephine Baker (1925), tango: Paris served as the stage where non-European cultural forms acquired legitimacy.

3. **French attitudes toward sensuality in art** made Paris more receptive than London or New York.

4. **In 1983, Jack Lang's cultural revolution** — Lang doubled the culture budget and broadened the Ministry's scope to include popular culture.

Segovia himself understood the dynamic: **"Paris has always played a great role in the history of tango. At the beginning of the century, its Parisian success gave tango its letters of nobility in Argentina. Decades later, the history repeated itself with Tango Argentino."**

---

## What Each Wave Meant for Tango's Survival

In 1913, tango arrived as a disreputable import and departed as a worldwide phenomenon that changed fashion, challenged morality, and gave the twentieth century one of its defining dances.

In 1983, tango arrived on a military cargo plane and departed as the spark that ignited a global revival now encompassing **over sixty countries**, a UNESCO heritage designation, and a tango tourism industry generating hundreds of millions annually.

Carlos Gavito offered the simplest summary: "We are painters. We paint the music with our feet." What Paris provided, twice, was the canvas.
