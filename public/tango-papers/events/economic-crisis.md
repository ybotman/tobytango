# Economic Crisis (2001–2002)

On December 1, 2001, the Argentine government froze bank accounts in a measure called the "corralito." Citizens could withdraw only $250 per week.

Within three weeks, President Fernando de la Rúa fled the Casa Rosada by helicopter as riots erupted across Buenos Aires, leaving 39 dead. Argentina defaulted on $95 billion in foreign debt — the largest sovereign default in history at that time. The peso lost three-quarters of its value. Unemployment exceeded 25%. The country burned through five presidents in two weeks.

## Tango in Freefall

At the Torcuato Tasso Tango Dance Hall in San Telmo, which had been packed with tourists every night, the floor was "nearly deserted." The tango industry — worth an estimated $180 million annually — was in freefall. Attendance at milongas dropped 40%.

## The Strange Gift

And then something strange happened. The crisis that devastated Argentina paradoxically spread tango across the world.

Argentine tango professionals, unable to survive at home, migrated to the United States, Europe, and Japan to teach. They brought with them an authenticity and depth that transformed tango communities from Tokyo to Berlin to San Francisco.

## Tango Tourism

Meanwhile, the collapsed peso made Buenos Aires astonishingly cheap for foreigners — tango shoes cost $20, group classes were practically free. As the economy slowly recovered from 2003 onward, a new phenomenon emerged: tango tourism.

Dancers from around the world discovered they could live like royalty in Buenos Aires for what they'd spend on a weekend in New York. The milongas that survived the crisis filled up again, this time with an international clientele.

As one milonga owner put it during the darkest days: **"During the crisis, people need a place to meet, to distract themselves, to get away from the emptiness."** Tango, born in hard times, knew how to survive them.
