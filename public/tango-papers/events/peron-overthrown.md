# Perón Overthrown (September 1955)

On September 16, 1955, the Argentine military rose against Juan Domingo Perón. The Navy threatened to bomb Buenos Aires. Three days later, Perón resigned and fled to Paraguay on a gunboat, beginning an exile that would last 18 years.

## Revolución Libertadora

The *Revolución Libertadora* installed General Eduardo Lonardi, then the harder-line General Pedro Eugenio Aramburu. They banned the Peronist party, blacklisted artists with Peronist connections, and began an aggressive campaign of "de-Peronization" that treated working-class culture as a threat.

## Catastrophe for Tango

For tango, this was a catastrophe. Perón had been a nationalist populist, and tango was both national and popular. Under his government, up to half a million porteños danced in Buenos Aires milongas on any given night. The Golden Age wasn't just musical — it was social, economic, political.

The new regime hated everything Perón had touched. Songs in lunfardo — Buenos Aires slang — were censored. Milongas were restricted. Tango artists were imprisoned or silenced.

## Rock Arrives

Simultaneously, rock and roll arrived from the United States, captivating Argentine youth who wanted nothing to do with anything that seemed old or populist. As tango historian Christine Denniston observed, there had been no tradition of formal tango teaching during the Golden Age — you learned by immersion in the milonga. When that chain of transmission was broken, the knowledge began to evaporate.

## Beginning of the End

The decline that started in 1955 would last nearly 30 years, pushing tango to the edge of extinction. Tango didn't die on September 16 — but the world that sustained it did.
