# Dirty War (1976–1983)

On March 24, 1976, the Argentine military seized power from Isabel Perón and installed a junta led by Lt. General Jorge Rafael Videla. They called it the "Proceso de Reorganización Nacional."

What followed was a reign of terror: an estimated 22,000 to 30,000 people were "disappeared" — kidnapped, tortured, murdered.

## The Quiet Death of Tango

For tango, the junta didn't need an explicit ban. They simply imposed a 10 p.m. curfew in a culture where milongas traditionally started at midnight. They prohibited gatherings of three or more people in the street. They shut down clubs and cafes.

In the mind of the military, as tango teacher Marcelo Solis explained, **"if Argentina was a mess, it was because its culture was a mess."** Rock venues could afford to bribe officials to look the other way. Milonga organizers could not.

## Near-Annihilation

The result was near-annihilation. Milongas closed. The aging milongueros — the living carriers of a dance tradition transmitted only through practice, never through formal instruction — were isolated from each other, afraid.

American rock filled the airwaves, partly as a tool to suppress nationalist populism. Young Argentines embraced rock 'n' roll as rebellion and rejected tango as the music of their grandparents.

## The Mothers

The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo marched in circles for their disappeared children. In the milongas — those few that survived — people danced in circles too, but there were almost no one left to dance with.

By the time the junta collapsed after the Falklands War defeat in 1982, tango had been reduced to what one historian called **"its minimum expression."** It was alive, but barely. The knowledge of how to dance it was now held by a shrinking handful of old men who had learned it in another era entirely.
