# Mundial Begins (2003)

In 2003, the Buenos Aires city government — under Mayor Aníbal Ibarra — added something new to its annual Tango Festival (running since 1998): a competition.

## The First Championship

The first Campeonato Mundial de Baile de Tango drew 218 couples, ages 18 to 85, who competed in two categories:

- **Tango de Pista** (salon tango) — danced in close embrace moving counterclockwise in the ronda, as in a real milonga
- **Tango Escenario** (stage tango) — with lifts, acrobatics, and choreography

The salon finals were held at Estadio Obras Sanitarias — a venue normally associated with rock concerts — before 3,500 spectators. Enrique Usales and Gabriela Sanguinetti won the first Tango de Pista title. Gisela Galeassi and Gastón Godoy took Tango Escenario. The prize was 3,500 pesos.

## Global Growth

What started as a modest civic event became a global phenomenon. By 2013, the festival drew 550,000 attendees, with 556 couples from 37 countries competing. By 2024, it was 750 couples from 53 countries. Pre-qualifying rounds now span more than 30 cities worldwide, from Montevideo to Moscow to Nanjing.

The economic impact is estimated at over $460 million.

## Landmark Decision

In 2013, organizers made the landmark decision to allow same-sex couples.

## Controversy

But the Mundial remains controversial among tango purists, who argue that competition-style "Tango de Pista" — with its exaggerated adornments, tense posture, and dramatic movements — bears little resemblance to actual social dancing at a milonga.

The label "Tango de Pista" ("dance-floor tango"), they say, misleadingly implies that what wins competitions is what people dance on a real dance floor.

## The Numbers

Supporters counter that the Mundial elevates tango's global profile, creates aspirational goals for dancers worldwide, and brings vital tourism revenue to Buenos Aires. Whatever your position, the numbers are hard to argue with: tango is now practiced in over 60 countries, and the Mundial is a significant reason why.
