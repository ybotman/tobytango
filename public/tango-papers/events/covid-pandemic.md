# COVID Pandemic (2020–2022)

On March 8, 2020, the organizers of Buenos Aires milongas met and collectively made a decision that would have been unthinkable a week before: they closed everything. All of them. Every milonga in the city — estimates ranged from 118 to 200 — shut their doors simultaneously.

Argentina declared a strict national quarantine. For 18 months, the government barred tango events entirely.

## Devastation

The iconic La Viruta Tango Club, which had hosted hundreds of dancers Wednesday through Sunday, went dark. Of its 18 employees, only three kept their jobs. Musician Nicolás Ponce started selling plants. Costume designer Daniela Inglese pivoted to making underwear and canvas bags. Some musicians pawned their violins and bandoneóns.

The toll was devastating. More than 50 milongas closed permanently. The Rivadavia Club shuttered in March 2021 after 16 years when its owner could no longer pay rent. The tango industry — employing roughly 10,000 people — collapsed. Borders closed, cutting off the tourist revenue that had become tango's financial lifeline.

## Aid and Adaptation

In August 2021, Buenos Aires distributed 16 million pesos (about $152,000) among roughly 100 tango clubs — barely enough to cover a month of expenses.

Communities adapted as best they could: online classes, outdoor milongas at the Obelisco, couples dancing in their apartments. The annual World Championship went virtual in 2020, with dancers submitting video recordings — the Association of Milongas called it "ridiculous."

## The Cruelest Impact

But the cruelest impact was on the elderly. **"Many older people in the tango milieu have died of sadness more than of COVID, for not being able to dance,"** said dancer María Campos.

Milongas began reopening in September 2021, but as organizer Julio Bassan admitted: **"We're definitely back, but the industry isn't on its feet yet."**

The tango community that re-emerged was smaller, poorer, and forever changed.
