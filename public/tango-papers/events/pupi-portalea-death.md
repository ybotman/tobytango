# Pupi & Portalea Die (2007)

> **HITM FLAG:** Sources on Portalea are limited. **CORRECTION:** The research referenced "Osvaldo Portalea" but the correct name is **Gerardo Portalea** (not Osvaldo). Portalea's exact death date could not be confirmed, only that he died approximately one month before or after Pupi, in 2007.

In 2007, the Villa Urquiza tango tradition lost its two greatest pillars within roughly a month of each other.

## Pupi Castello

Ernesto "Pupi" Castello (born December 18, 1935) died on or around July 20, 2007, at Hospital de las Clínicas in Buenos Aires.

Pupi was the extrovert, the life of the party, cigarette dangling, hands in pockets, overwhelming everyone with his charm. He danced with Graciela González for 19 years and taught internationally. He appeared in the BBC documentary *La Confiteria Ideal* in 2005, where he gave his famous confession: **"It'd be like cutting off one of my legs."**

Even in his final months at the hospital, he was teaching tango to doctors and nurses.

## Gerardo Portalea

Gerardo Portalea (born circa 1928) died the same year, the exact date unrecorded in English-language sources.

Portalea was serious, modest, reserved. He worked his entire life as a gravedigger at the San Martín cemetery — a detail that lent his dancing a metaphysical gravity. By many accounts, he was the single finest dancer the northern barrios had produced — the man people always named when you asked who was the best.

He was Jorge Dispari's first maestro. He danced the musical phrase, not the beat, in a way that baffled newcomers until they understood what they were seeing.

## The Villa Urquiza Style

They were opposites in temperament but shared the same elegant, walked, sober, musical style that defined *tango estilo Villa Urquiza* — the style of the northern Buenos Aires clubs like Sunderland and Sin Rumbo.

Pupi himself had said: **"Most of the old milongueros, other than Portalea, are dead now. That's why we have to encourage the young people. Otherwise, tango will die with us."**

Months later, both were gone. As Buenos Aires newspaper *Clarín* wrote, the milonga was in mourning.
