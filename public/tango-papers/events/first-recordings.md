# First Recordings (1905–1910)

Before the phonograph, tango lived only in the room where it was played. It was born in the port neighborhoods of Buenos Aires and Montevideo in the late 19th century — a music of immigrants, Afro-Argentine communities, and criollos that no respectable venue would touch.

Then the recording machine changed everything.

## The First Recordings

In April 1905, Columbia Records sent engineers to the Río de la Plata, and a self-taught guitarist and former tram driver named Ángel Villoldo stepped up to record some of the earliest tango matrices.

In mid-1906, Alfredo and Flora Gobbi traveled to London and recorded 56 cylinders for Edison-Bell.

In 1907, sponsored by the Buenos Aires department store Gath & Chaves, Villoldo himself traveled to Paris with the Gobbis to make what would become some of the first internationally distributed tango recordings — including his masterpiece, "El Choclo."

## El Choclo

Villoldo had composed "El Choclo" around 1898–1903 (he couldn't read or write music; José Luis Roncallo transcribed the score). It was premiered on November 3, 1903, at Restaurant El Americano in Buenos Aires, smuggled onto the program under the disguise of "Danza Criolla" because the restaurant owner disapproved of tango.

The title — "The Corn Cob" — came from the nickname of a local pimp with corn-colored hair.

## La Morocha

Another Villoldo composition, "La Morocha" (music by Enrique Saborido, lyrics by Villoldo), sold more than 280,000 copies of sheet music and became the first tango score exported to Europe.

## The Spread

Argentina had no recording studios at the time — every early tango recording was made in Philadelphia, Bridgeport, London, or Paris. But by 1910, over 350 tango discs had been produced, and more than a third of all gramophone records released featured tango.

The music of the slums was suddenly in living rooms across the world, and nothing could put it back.
