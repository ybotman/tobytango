# Democracy Returns (1983)

On October 30, 1983, Argentina elected Raúl Alfonsín president, and on December 10, civilian government returned. The dictatorship was over.

Tango lyricist Enrique Cadícamo captured the moment perfectly: **"The tango in 1983 was a memory."**

## Tentative Return

Milongas began to reopen — slowly, tentatively. The old milongueros, now in their sixties and seventies, emerged from years of isolation and fear to find a younger generation that barely knew what Argentine tango was.

There had been no formal teaching tradition during the Golden Age; tango was learned by doing, in the milonga itself, over years. That chain of transmission had been shattered. The old dancers were suspicious of strangers, reluctant to teach, uncertain whether the nightmare was really over.

But they came back. They couldn't help it.

## Two Currents

Remarkably, at almost the exact same moment — November 1983 — Claudio Segovia and Héctor Orezzoli premiered *Tango Argentino* at the Festival d'Automne in Paris. It was as if two currents of revival started simultaneously: one from within Buenos Aires, as aging milongueros reclaimed their dance floors, and one from the outside, as international audiences fell in love with something they'd never seen before.

## Cultural Opening

Alfonsín's government launched the CONADEP truth commission and the Trial of the Juntas in 1985, creating a cultural opening that matched the political one. The average age of the *Tango Argentino* cast was 42 — old enough for audiences to think "I can do that."

It was both a homecoming and a new beginning. The question was whether enough knowledge had survived to rebuild.
