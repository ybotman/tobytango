# D'Arienzo Revolution (1935–1938)

By the mid-1930s, tango had a problem: people had stopped dancing to it. The genius of Carlos Gardel had moved tango "from the feet to the mouth," as Enrique Santos Discépolo put it. The sophisticated arrangements of the Julio De Caro school were beautiful — and undanceable. Orchestras played for theaters, radio, and records, not for dancers.

## The Transformation

Then Juan D'Arienzo changed everything. In 1935, a young pianist named Rodolfo Biagi joined his orchestra and pushed D'Arienzo to abandon the languid 4/8 time and return to the propulsive 2/4 beat of the old Guardia Vieja. The story goes that one night D'Arienzo arrived late to find his orchestra already playing in the new style — and the crowd dancing with an energy no one had seen in years. On July 9, Argentine Independence Day, the audience clapped and shouted for more, and D'Arienzo had no choice but to play the new way all night long.

## Impact

The effect was electric. D'Arienzo's staccato, driving rhythm made dancers' feet move almost involuntarily. Between 1935 and 1939, he recorded 116 sides, outselling Canaro and Troilo combined. Record stores required customers to buy something else before they could purchase a D'Arienzo disc. His broadcasts on El Mundo radio spread the sound nationwide.

## El Rey del Compás

They called him "El Rey del Compás" — the King of the Beat — and he earned it. Virtually every orchestra in Buenos Aires sped up to keep pace. As Aníbal Troilo later admitted: "Laugh if you will, but without D'Arienzo, we'd all be out of work."

The D'Arienzo-Biagi partnership (Biagi left in 1938 to form his own orchestra) launched the Golden Age of Tango, filling dance floors across Buenos Aires for the next twenty years.
