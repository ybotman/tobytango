# Paris Tangomania (1912–1914)

Tango arrived in Paris the way a fire arrives — first a flicker, then an inferno. By 1912, Argentine sons of wealthy families had been showing off this exotic dance from the Río de la Plata in Montmartre cabarets for a few years. But in 1913, the craze exploded.

## The Inferno

Musicians like Casimiro Aín sailed from Buenos Aires aboard the steamship *Sierra Ventana* and set up at clubs like La Princesse (later known as El Garrón), run by fellow Argentine Manuel Pizarro. Within months, tango was everywhere — in ballrooms, in salons, in the fashion pages.

"Tango teas" became the rage. Designers created split skirts so women could move their legs. A new color — tango orange — appeared in dress shops across the city. The Argentine ambassador, mortified, warned the French that tango "is found only in whorehouses and filthy taverns."

The French didn't care.

## The Establishment Panics

The establishment panicked. In November 1913, Pope Pius X declared tango immoral and off-limits to Catholics. In January 1914, Cardinal Basilio Pompili, the Vatican's Vicar General of Rome, denounced it as "the overwhelming immorality of the new paganism." Cardinal Amette, Archbishop of Paris, declared it a sin requiring confession.

Germany's Kaiser Wilhelm, England's Queen Mary, and the Italian War Minister all banned or condemned the dance. A French journalist claimed the Pope had a pair of young Roman aristocrats demonstrate tango for him and that he called it "one of the dullest things imaginable." The Vatican denied the story.

The *Mercure de France* called it "la danse des filles publiques" — the dance of prostitutes.

## None of It Mattered

None of it mattered. By the time World War I interrupted the party, tango had conquered European popular culture and permanently escaped the slums of Buenos Aires. It would never go back.
