# Stage Pioneers (1983–2000): The Generation That Saved Tango

**The stage pioneers rescued tango from near-extinction and brought it to global audiences through theatrical spectacle.** Their story begins in 1983, when democracy returned to Argentina and a low-budget show in Paris ignited a cultural wildfire that would transform Argentine tango from a fading folk tradition into the fastest-growing partner dance in the world.

---

## The Near-Death and Resurrection

Tango's Golden Age (1930s–1950s) ended abruptly with the 1955 coup against Juan Perón. Military regimes associated tango with Peronism and banned public gatherings. By the late 1970s, during the brutal "Dirty War" dictatorship (1976–1983) that disappeared an estimated 9,000–30,000 people, tango retreated to basement venues and was practiced mainly by the elderly. Only a handful of dedicated dancers — **Juan Carlos Copes** and **Virulazo** chief among them — kept the tradition alive through clandestine shows and sheer stubbornness.

On **November 11, 1983**, the show *Tango Argentino* premiered at the Festival d'Automne in Paris. Created by set designers **Claudio Segovia and Héctor Orezzoli**, the production was low-budget, monochromatic (black, white, and silver costumes), and featured an average cast age of **42 years**. When it transferred to Broadway in 1985, it became the unlikeliest hit of the season, earning Tony Award nominations and attracting Princess Diana, Rudolf Nureyev, Mikhail Baryshnikov, and Robert De Niro.

The show's genius was authenticity. Each couple performed their own style — from Copes and Nieves' elegant understatement to Virulazo and Elvira's raw milonguero power. When audiences saw a 126-kilogram milonguero appear to float across the stage, their reaction was: *"I can do that."*

## Juan Carlos Copes (1931–2021) — The Man Who Put Tango on the World Stage

**Born May 31, 1931, in Mataderos, Buenos Aires**, Copes was the grandson of tango pianist Juan Berti. He began dancing with 14-year-old **María Nieves** in the late 1940s; they won top prize at Luna Park Stadium in **1951** among 300 couples. Through the 1950s and 1960s, they performed at Teatro El Nacional, the Waldorf Astoria in New York, and on CBS's *The Arthur Murray Party* (1960) and *The Ed Sullivan Show* (1962–1964).

Copes was **the first person to create choreographed tango stage shows**, transforming the dance from social folk practice to theatrical art form. His choreographic credits include Astor Piazzolla's tango-opera *María de Buenos Aires* (1968), Carlos Saura's film *Tango* (1998), and Wong Kar-wai's *Happy Together* (1997).

His style was characterized by a **soft step with economy of movement**, elegant body lines, and precise synchronization. He and Nieves created the "estilo Copes-Nieves" — treating man and woman as equal partners (50/50), emphasizing mutual respect and understated passion.

He taught **Robert Duvall, Julio Bocca, Mikhail Baryshnikov, and Liza Minnelli**, and lectured at Juilliard, Stanford, the Sorbonne, and the University of Chicago. He was proclaimed **"Best Dancer of the 20th Century"** in Argentina in 2000. He died on **January 16, 2021**, of COVID-19 complications.

## María Nieves (b. 1934) — Born to Dance Tango

**Born September 6, 1934, in Saavedra, Buenos Aires**, to Galician immigrant parents, Nieves left school after fourth grade to work as a maid in La Boca. She never had a single formal dance lesson, famously declaring: *"The first time I danced the tango, it entered my skin through my feet, passed from my skin to my blood and through my blood to my heart."*

Beyond her partnership with Copes, Nieves built a substantial independent career. After their professional split (~1997), she starred in *Tanguera* (2002, playing the brothel keeper) with runs in Buenos Aires, New York, Paris, Berlin, and Tokyo. Her film appearances include *Assassination Tango* (2002) and *Our Last Tango* (2015). She was honored as **Illustrious Citizen of Buenos Aires** in 2008 and received the **María Ruanova Award** (2000) for lifetime achievement. She was a pioneer in **empowering women in tango**, challenging traditional gender dynamics by creating strong, independent female roles.

## Virulazo — Jorge Martín Orcaizaguirre (1926–1990)

**Born October 10, 1926, in Haedo, Greater Buenos Aires**, Virulazo was the embodiment of authentic, unpolished barrio tango. He debuted dancing with his mother at age 12. Tango poet **Celedonio Flores** discovered him: *"Boy, you can't go on dancing for free."* He performed at the great Buenos Aires cabarets — **Chantecler and Tabarís** — and won a major national contest in **1952**.

At 57 years old, weighing **126 kg**, with a scar across his face, he auditioned for *Tango Argentino*. When Segovia saw him arrive, he looked at Copes with incredulity. Copes simply said: *"Míralos bailar"* — and when Virulazo began dancing, "Claudio could not believe what he was seeing — that such a man appeared to float."

He explicitly rejected choreography: *"I am a professional only because I'm paid. Deep in my heart I keep on being an amateur. I don't stick to a choreography — that's what the dancers do and I am a milonguero."*

His wife and dance partner **Elvira Santamaría** (b. 1929) complemented his massive frame with thin, graceful ornaments. He died on **August 2, 1990**, of lung cancer. His dream was: *"I wish I died dancing a tango."*

## Gloria and Rodolfo Dinzel — The Systematizers of Tango Pedagogy

**Gloria Dinzel (1948–2018)** trained in classical ballet at the Teatro Colón; **Rodolfo Dinzel (1950–2015)** came from folkloric dance. They formed their partnership in **1972** and developed the **Dinzel System** — considered the **first systematization of tango dance structure**. Their notation system covered over **3,600 tango figures**.

They joined *Tango Argentino* in **1984** and toured internationally for more than a decade. Their book *Tango: An Anxious Quest for Freedom* was the first literary work on tango dynamics addressing technical and theoretical aspects.

Their teaching legacy is profound: **Miguel Ángel Zotto** credits Rodolfo Dinzel as a formative teacher, and **Gustavo Naveira** — the intellectual father of tango nuevo — lists the Dinzels as his first tango teachers. This shared root connecting stage and nuevo lineages is significant.

## Carlos Gavito (1942–2005) — The Poet of the Pause

**Born April 27, 1942, in Avellaneda**, Gavito learned tango from tango artists, not dance instructors. Poet **Julián Centeya** was, as Gavito said, *"my best teacher — he taught me tango from the inside."* Orchestra leader **Miguel Caló** taught him to *"dance the voice, just the voice, now dance the piano."*

Gavito's late-career renaissance came through *Forever Tango* (1995–2005). His partnership with **Marcela Durán** became the show's emotional centerpiece, performing their signature piece to *"A Evaristo Carriego."*

His style was **minimalist and dramatic** — he created a distinctive close embrace called **"the tent"** and was known for the philosophy of **"less is more."** His most famous teaching: *"I don't see the rush to finish one step and go into the next one. I think it's much more interesting to do one, stop, without really stopping but more like a pause and just do nothing for a while, enjoying the moment."*

Essential Gavito quotes:
- *"Tango is a sad thought that you can dance."*
- *"Tango is between one step and the next. Steps are not tango, steps are memory and choreography."*

He died of lung cancer on **July 1, 2005**, at age 62.

## Pablo Verón (b. 1971) — Tango's Cinematic Ambassador

**Born October 17, 1971, in Buenos Aires**, Verón joined *Tango Argentino* in **1989** and settled in Paris. His 1993 performance in *Mortadella* won the **Prix Molière for Best Musical**.

His defining role was choreographing and starring in **Sally Potter's *The Tango Lesson* (1997)** — the semi-autobiographical film that introduced millions to Argentine tango. The film won the **American Choreography Award** for Outstanding Achievement in Feature Film. His other credits include *The Man Who Cried* (2000, with Johnny Depp), *Assassination Tango* (2002), and *Our Last Tango* (2015, playing Copes).

*Time Out New York* declared: *"Pablo Verón has the feet of God."*

---

## Miguel Ángel Zotto (b. 1958) — Bridging Tradition and Spectacle

**Born August 7, 1958, in Vicente López, Buenos Aires**, Zotto grew up in a tango family. At 17, he began formal study with **Rodolfo Dinzel**, **Antonio Todaro**, **Pepito Avellaneda**, **Copes and Nieves**, and **Virulazo and Elvira**.

In **1985**, he met **Milena Plebs**, who became his student, wife, and legendary partner. They joined *Tango Argentino* in **1986** — the **youngest couple in the cast** — and in **1988** founded the **Tango x 2 Company**.

Zotto's philosophy bridges tradition and spectacle: *"Tango is not masculine, it is a couple: fifty percent man and fifty percent woman, even though the most important step, the 'ocho,' which is like the heart of tango, is done by the woman."*

His awards include the **María Ruanova Award** (1991) — the first time it was awarded to tango — and the **Gino Tani Award** (1998, Italy's most coveted dance prize). He was elected among the **"three greatest tango dancers of the century"** alongside Copes and Virulazo.

Notable dancers who emerged from his company include Sebastián Misse and Andrea Reyero, Osvaldo Zotto and Mora Godoy, and Laila Rezk and Leandro Oliver.

---

## Legacy: The Global Boom

Cast members began teaching in New York, seeding the global tango teaching tradition. Successor shows proliferated — *Forever Tango*, *Tango x 2*, *Tango Pasión*, and dozens more. By 2006, tango generated approximately **$135 million** in direct income in Buenos Aires. In **2009**, UNESCO inscribed tango on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The stage pioneers didn't just save tango from extinction — they transformed it into a global art form while preserving its authentic soul.
