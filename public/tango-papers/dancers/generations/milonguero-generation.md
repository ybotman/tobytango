# The milonguero generation: guardians of tango's soul

**The dancers profiled here preserved Argentine tango through decades of political repression, cultural neglect, and generational change**, carrying the art form from the Golden Age of the 1940s through to its global renaissance in the 1990s. Most were working-class porteños — pizza makers, gravediggers, mechanics, utility workers — who danced not for money or fame but because, as Finito put it, "Tango has to give you goosebumps, brother; otherwise it's no good." They learned in male-only practices, mastered the códigos of the milonga, and kept tango alive in neighborhood clubs even as rock and roll, military dictatorships, and social upheaval nearly extinguished the dance. Their styles — milonguero, Villa Urquiza, salon, canyengue — became the foundation on which all subsequent tango development was built, including the tango nuevo movement whose pioneers explicitly studied these very dancers. This report profiles 26 dancers and couples from this generation, drawing from tango history sites, obituaries, interviews, and published references.

---

## The world these dancers inhabited

### The Golden Age and its collapse

The Golden Age of tango (roughly **1935–1955**) saw over 50 orchestras performing nightly across Buenos Aires in salons, cafés, cabarets, and neighborhood sports clubs. Every barrio had its own club — River Plate, Huracán, Atlanta, Independiente — where young men learned to dance through rigorous male-only practices before ever setting foot in a milonga. The era produced the orchestras that milongueros would dance to for the rest of their lives: D'Arienzo, Di Sarli, Troilo, Pugliese, Canaro, Tanturi, Biagi.

The overthrow of Perón in 1955 began tango's long decline. Military governments imposed curfews, suppressed public gatherings, and viewed tango culture with suspicion. The **1976–1983 military junta** (the "Dirty War") made social dancing nearly impossible. Concurrently, rock and roll, folk music, and cumbia captured younger audiences. Many of the milongueros profiled here — Finito, Pupi Castello, Carlos Perez, Osvaldo Cartery — **stopped dancing entirely for 20 to 30 years** during this period.

### The renaissance

Democracy's return in 1983 under Alfonsín brought free cultural programs, including tango classes in newly created cultural centers. The Broadway show *Tango Argentino* (Paris premiere 1983, Broadway 1985) ignited global interest. By the late 1980s, old milongueros began returning to the dance floor — Finito came back in 1980, Pupi Castello in the mid-1980s, Carlos Perez in 1994. A brief, precious window opened in which the living tradition of Golden Age social tango could be observed, learned from, and documented before its practitioners died.

### Códigos and milonga culture

The milongas operated under elaborate unwritten codes. The **cabeceo** — invitation by eye contact and subtle head nod — ensured no public rejection. Music was organized in **tandas** (sets of 3–4 songs by one orchestra) separated by **cortinas** (musical interludes signaling partner changes). Men and women sat on opposite sides of the floor. Counter-clockwise navigation, small steps, proper hygiene, formal dress, and no stage moves were mandatory. At strict milongas like Cachirulo, violations meant expulsion. These codes reflected a culture where, as Pocho Carreras described it, "tango is the sensuality of two souls in one rhythm."

### Travel patterns

**Most milongueros never left Buenos Aires.** El Chino Perico has famously never left the city in his life. Ismael El Jalil, Roberto Segarra, Elba Biscay, and Adela Galeazzi were purely local social dancers. International teaching by milongueros only began in the mid-1990s, catalyzed by Pina Bausch's invitation to Tete Rusconi (1994–97), the Broadway *Tango Argentino* cast's US teaching, and growing tango tourism. A handful — Tete and Silvia Ceriani, Ricardo Vidort, Pepito Avellaneda, Carlos Perez and Rosa Forte — eventually taught abroad. Argentina's 2001–2002 economic crisis made Buenos Aires extremely affordable for foreigners, accelerating tango tourism and creating demand for milonguero teachers.

## Styles forged by this generation

### Villa Urquiza style (Estilo del Barrio)

Named for the northern Buenos Aires neighborhood but danced throughout many barrios, this style features **upright posture, separate axes, long elegant steps, and complex figures** (sacadas, arrastres, amagues, boleos). The embrace is typically closed but loosened slightly for turns. Co-created by **Luis "Milonguita" Lemos and El Turco José Brahemcha** between roughly 1945 and 1956 at clubs like Sin Rumbo and Sunderland. Key exponents include Gerardo Portalea, Finito (Ramón Rivera), Carlos Perez and Rosa Forte, and El Chino Perico.

### Milonguero style (Estilo del Centro / Apilado)

Developed in the **crowded downtown clubs of the 1950s**, characterized by close embrace with shared axis ("apilado" — stacked/leaning), small steps, and syncopated rhythmic footwork. The **ocho cortado** was invented when a milonguero started an ocho, realized he lacked space, and interrupted the move. Associated with downtown neighborhoods and intimate venues. Key exponents include Ricardo Vidort, Tete Rusconi, and dancers at milongas like Lo de Celia and El Beso. Susana Miller is credited with codifying the term "milonguero style" in the early 1990s.

### Canyengue

The **oldest form of Argentine tango**, dating to the 1920s–1930s, with roots in African-Argentine candombe rhythms. Characterized by bent knees, short frequent steps in ric-tic-tic rhythm, and close embrace in an offset V. **Luis Grondona and Martha Anton** were the foremost researchers and practitioners who revived this style for modern audiences.

### Orillero

From the edges (orillas) of Buenos Aires, a playful, rhythmic style associated with working-class neighborhoods. **Osvaldo and Coca Cartery** explicitly identified their style as orillero, and Pepito Avellaneda's creative milonga dancing drew from this tradition.

## The dancers: individual profiles

### 1. Ricardo Vidort — "The Last Compadrito"

**Full name:** Ricardo Vidort. **Born:** August 30, 1929, Buenos Aires. **Died:** May 21, 2006, Buenos Aires. **Nicknames:** "The Last Compadrito," "The Grand Architect" (both from Rick McGarrey's tangoandchaos.org).

Vidort danced milonguero-style tango with **exquisite musicality and emotional depth** that prioritized connection and improvisation over figures. He used corridas, sacadas, vai-ven (rock steps), and a distinctive technique of planting one foot while making small stabbing movements with the other. His philosophy: "You can copy my steps, but you can't copy what I feel." He held a **degree in parapsychology** and had a daughter named Solange. He taught internationally in Europe, the Americas, and Asia, though he reportedly felt underappreciated in the United States. His regular milongas included El Beso, Confitería Ideal, and Celia's. His final life partner, Polish dancer **Ewa Kiełczewska**, created a comprehensive memorial website.

**Key partnerships:** Danced with Alejandra ("Alej"), taught with multiple partners, influenced Marcelo Solis and Myriam Pincen. **Disciples:** His philosophy shaped the global milonguero-style movement.

**References:** ricardovidort.wordpress.com (16-chapter memorial by Kiełczewska); tangoandchaos.org/chapt_5video/33ricardo.htm; jantango.wordpress.com/2017/08/30/ricardo-vidort-4/; escuelatangoba.com; tango.am/biography-ricardo-vidort/.

### 2. Myriam Pincen — keeper of traditional technique

**Full name:** Myriam Pincen. **Location:** Buenos Aires. Active teacher at the **Escuela de Tango de Buenos Aires** (co-directed by Marcelo Solis and Néstor Pellicciaro).

Pincen teaches Tango Salón Tradicional Argentino, drawing on **over 30 years of study** with teachers including Miguel Gutierrez, Eduardo Arquimbau, Mingo Pugliese, Pepito Avellaneda, and J.C. Copez. Her stepping technique has been analyzed on tangoandchaos.org, where McGarrey noted her steps have "many of the characteristics of the leg of a cat, as it reaches forward, and places its paw firmly on the ground." She danced famously with **Ricardo Vidort** and with Roberto Calaza at the Región Leonesa milonga. In January 2025, she shared a preserved note from **Jorge Daniel Dispari** (1957–2020) on floorcraft and the primacy of walking over figures.

**References:** escuelatangoba.com/about-escuela-tango/about-myriam-pincen/; tangoandchaos.org/chapt_3search/18catshorses; barryhashimototangoarts.com/p/the-dispari-pincen-note.

### 3. Pedro "Tete" Rusconi — the man with an orchestra in his head

**Full name:** Pedro Alberto Rusconi. **Born:** January 9, 1936, Pompeya, Buenos Aires ("a block away from Centenera and Tabaré" — the neighborhood Homero Manzi immortalized). **Died:** January 7, 2010, two days before his 74th birthday, Palermo, Buenos Aires. **Nickname:** "Tete"; associated with "Reyes de la Noche" (Kings of the Night).

Tete was a porteño dancer acclaimed for his personal waltz-dancing style with unique displacements and natural sways. "When I dance to it I feel like flying," he said of vals. His style was colorful and energetic, with shorter steps, quicker movements, and more turns than Villa Urquiza dancers. He learned by observing milongueros in his teens and modeled himself on "Tin," a legendary dancer of the 1950s.

In **1994 (or 1997), German choreographer Pina Bausch invited Tete to Europe** to dance in her piece "Nur Du" (Only You) with Tanztheater Wuppertal for three consecutive years. Bausch told her collaborators that Tete "had an orchestra in his head." He toured internationally with **Silvia Ceriani** (his partner from the mid-1990s), taught across Europe and the Americas, and is considered one of the first to teach close-embrace style in the US. The evening before his death, he went to dance at El Beso — his "last lesson."

**Earlier partners:** Maria Villalobos, Rosanna Remon. **Film/documentary:** Daniel Tonelli directed "A volar señores: Un Vals Para Tete." Paul Pellicoro's tango book lists Tete and Silvia among the most important couples of the last 30 years.

**References:** todotango.com/english/artists/biography/1593/Tete-Rusconi/ (biography by Silvina Damiani); silviaceriani.com.ar/teteysilvia/english/eng_background.htm; jantango.wordpress.com/2010/01/09/pedro-alberto-rusconi/; mytangodiaries.com/2010/01/tete-rusconi-dancing-on-clouds.html; tangotales.com/tete-and-silvia/.

### 4. Silvia Ceriani — musicalizadora and maestra

**Full name:** Silvia Ceriani. **Location:** Buenos Aires. **Active:** Teaching internationally since 1996.

Ceriani was Tete Rusconi's long-time dance and life partner. Together they taught Tango Estilo Milonguero with its maintained enclosed embrace worldwide. Described as "intellectual and artist, dancer, teacher, musicalizadora and cultrice of Argentine Tango," she continues teaching internationally (particularly in Italy and Europe) after Tete's death, focusing on "rhythm, cadence and elegance of Tango Salon" and "being friends with music." She also works as a **DJ (musicalizadora) at milongas**. Their instructional video "Fundamentals of Salon Tango" covers posture, walking, ochos, turns, pauses, and dancing to the rhythm. They also appeared in Daniel Trenner's "Bridge to the Tango" video series.

**References:** silviaceriani.com.ar; teteysilvia.blogspot.com; tejastango.com/bridge_tango.html; tangotales.com/tete-and-silvia/; ballatango.it (Rome workshop listings).

### 5. Pepito Avellaneda — "His Majesty" of milonga

**Full name:** José Domingo Monteleone. **Stage name:** Pepito Avellaneda. **Born:** November 30, 1930 (some sources say October 30), Avellaneda, Buenos Aires Province. **Died:** April 29, 1996. **Ancestry:** Italian and Spanish.

Pepito was a **self-taught genius of milonga** whose creativity and musicality made him legendary. "As for the milonga, I'm the creator of all the steps I play," he declared. He learned through male-only practices: "There were no women at the practices. It was only with men." His first exhibition was at age 15 at Teatro Roma de Avellaneda (1945). By trade, he was a **pizzero** — "My trade is to sell pizza. We had a pizza shop with daddy." He lost his mother at age 5.

Pepito taught extensively in **Europe** ("In Europe, they love me very much") and made his only US appearance in June 1995 at the First International Argentine Tango Congress at Northwestern University, organized by Janis Kenyon. He was issued a five-year US work visa but died before returning. His **last performance** was March 25, 1996, at Teatro Comunale di Casale Monferrato, Italy — his first visit to his ancestral homeland — dancing "La Payanca" to Color Tango. He died one month later.

**Partner:** His wife **Gilda Suzuki de Sousa** (Brazilian; commonly called simply "Suzuki"). They are the couple referred to as "Pepito and Gilda" in tango circles. **Disciples:** Omar Vega, Oscar Mandagaran, Gabriel Angió and Natalia Games, Diego di Falco. His style influenced generations of milonga dancers. He preferred D'Arienzo for orillero tango, Di Sarli and Pugliese for slow dancing.

**References:** todotango.com/english/artists/biography/658/Pepito-Avellaneda/ (biography by María Susana Azzi); jantango.wordpress.com/2008/10/14/pepito-avellaneda/; elizabethwartlufttango.com/blog/2014/8/19/pepito-avellaneda-and-his-dance-an-exploration; tangoe14.co.uk/pepito-avellaneda/; mariachiaramichieli.com (Italian tribute); discepolintango.com.ar (interview); tangofashionnews.com.ar.

### 6. Ismael Heljalil — the minimalist

**Full name:** Ismael Heljalil (confirmed spelling by Janis Kenyon: "I am a stickler for getting complete and correctly spelled names"). **Born:** August 30, 1930. **Status:** Was still dancing as of at least 2014 (age 84).

Heljalil exemplified milonguero minimalism. "He walks and turns, focusing on the essentials — the music and his partner. His tango isn't cluttered. It's simple and elegant," wrote Kenyon. Rick McGarrey's analysis of his dancing noted: "Like most milongueros, he knows dozens of steps and figures, but he doesn't use them. I don't even see a real giro or a corrida." He carried a **handkerchief in his left hand** while dancing, used the cabeceo faithfully, and preferred the music of **Miguel Caló**. He was a regular at **Lo de Celia** and **Maipu 444** until it closed. He **never traveled internationally** — a purely Buenos Aires social dancer whose dancing was preserved through McGarrey's video documentation.

**References:** tangoandchaos.org/chapt_5video/17ismael.htm (detailed video analysis); jantango.wordpress.com/2011/02/17/ismael-heljalil-3/; jantango.wordpress.com/2014/10/20/tango-and-minimalism-los-milongeros-viejos/; tangovoice.wordpress.com/2010/07/06/ (milongueros dancing analysis).

### 7. Luis Grondona and Mirta Sol — canyengue's champions

**Full name:** Luis Grondona. **Born:** October 23, 1938. **Wife/dance partner:** Mirta Sol (not "Nilda" — no "Nilda" was found associated with Grondona). **Earlier partner:** Martha Anton. **Status:** Suffered a stroke around 2006; was in a wheelchair at Instituto Geriatrico Princess in Buenos Aires.

**Note on "Luis and Nilda":** Research found no dancer named Nilda associated with Luis Grondona. His documented partners are Mirta Sol (wife) and Martha Anton (earlier canyengue partner). "Luis and Nilda" may refer to a different, unidentified couple.

Grondona was an **internationally recognized authority on tango canyengue**, the oldest form of Argentine tango. With Martha Anton, he researched and revived canyengue as a teachable style, releasing the VHS instructional series **"Así se baila canyengue" Volumes 1 and 2** (1999). He taught internationally across Europe. His students included Leo of Tango Fandango (UK), Nicolás Bertucci (Vienna), and Alejandro Angelica (Tangopuro founder), who described studying with Grondona as essential for understanding "the origin of the marca, the walk, the embrace, the use of the body center and the interpretation of music." After his stroke, Mirta Sol continued teaching canyengue in Avellaneda.

**References:** jantango.wordpress.com/2008/10/23/luis-grondona/; worldcat.org (VHS catalog records); tango-fandango.co.uk; nicolastango.at/biografie; tangopuro.org/en/alejandro/.

### 9. Osvaldo and Coca Cartery — "Honey Feet"

**Full names:** Osvaldo Ernesto Cartery (born March 10, 1938; died October 9, 2015, age 77) and **Luisa Inés "Coca" Anaclerio de Cartery**. From the southern suburbs of Greater Buenos Aires (Gerli, near Lanús).

Osvaldo learned tango the traditional way — watching for years, then dancing the follower's role, then finally dancing in milongas. After marrying Coca, he **did not dance for nearly 30 years**, working as a **municipal employee**. Upon retirement, at their daughters' urging, they returned to the milonga. In **2004, competing among 665 couples, they won the World Tango Championship (Tango Salón category)**.

Their style was explicitly **orillero** (from the outskirts). Osvaldo was nicknamed **"Pies de Miel" (Honey Feet)** — he explained his soft dancing was out of respect for his female partner. Rick McGarrey described Osvaldo's dancing as "the closest thing to legendary dancer Petroleo's style in modern milongas." Their technique featured sophisticated **asynchronicity**: Coca often followed the melody at the beat or slower, while Osvaldo marked beats and half-beats. "Many too many dancers dance to be seen, when what really matters is the opposite: the attention to the music, to your partner, and to your own pleasure," Osvaldo said. After their 2004 championship, they toured internationally in Slovenia, Italy, Sweden, Austria, Croatia, Chile, and throughout Argentina. Osvaldo died of pulmonary complications in 2015. Coca continued as a revered milonguera.

**References:** cultura.gob.ar/noticias/despedida-a-osvaldo-cartery/ (Argentine Ministry of Culture obituary); issuu.com/lamilongaargentina/docs/117_nov15b (La Milonga Argentina obituary); tanguito.co.uk/blog/we-osvaldo-and-coca/; escuelatangoba.com/marcelosolis/osvaldo-coca-cartery/; tangology101.com/main.cfm/id/100; siempremilonguero.org/interview-with-osvaldo-and-coca-cartery/.

### 11. El Turco José Brahemcha — co-creator of Villa Urquiza style

**Full name:** José Brahemcha. **Nickname:** El Turco José. **Born:** July 17, 1931, barrio de Saavedra, Buenos Aires. **Died:** April 15, 2010.

El Turco José was the **co-creator, alongside Luis "Milonguita" Lemos, of the Villa Urquiza style** of tango between approximately 1945 and 1956. Described as a "strong intellectual of dancing and movement in space," he was famous for "opening the dance" — using existing rotating structures to create figures with enormous dynamics. His style combined "symmetry, contrast and elegance." He "left us with the beautiful and versatile entry to the ocho."

Born in Saavedra, he began dancing as a teenager at the Asociación de Fomento y Cultura Villa Cerini. His mother sang tangos and encouraged him to dance. He preferred melodic tangos, especially Pugliese. His maxims: "Never dance for the audience but for your dancing partner" and "Don't dance to everything — only to what you feel, to what touches you." As a child, he was dedicated to cycling before turning to tango.

He **primarily stayed in Buenos Aires** for most of his career. At age 73 (around 2004), he traveled outside Argentina for the first time to promote the Villa Urquiza style in Europe, including Denmark and Berlin. He danced with **Nelly Acosta** and was part of the "Barra" of Villa Urquiza milongueros. His students included Hernán Alvarez Prieto, and Natalia Games and Gabriel Angió cite him among their inspirations. **Graciela Gonzalez** also lists him among her teachers.

**References:** todotango.com/english/artists/biography/1738/El-Turco-Jose/ (biography by Gustavo Benzecry Sabá); tanguito.co.uk/blog/el-turco-jose-brahemcha/; nataliaygabriel.com (dedicated page); tangourquiza.com/interview-med-jose-el-turco-brahemcha/; urquiza.com. Book: Gustavo Benzecry Sabá, *Nuevo glosario de tango danza* and *La pista del abrazo*.

### 12 & 13. Roberto Segarra (and the question of "Revert Segarra")

**Full name:** Roberto Segarra. **Born:** September 16, 1920. **Died:** November 21, 2018 (age 98).

Segarra was a milonguero who danced for many decades in Buenos Aires. Known for his energetic dancing, particularly in vals — one partner recalled: "He surprised me dancing with so much energy. At the end of the tanda, Roberto asked me: 'te mareaste?' [did I make you dizzy?]" He was described as "a gentleman with a palpable zest for life." His long-time dance partner was **Olga Gomez**, with whom he danced at Club Fulgor de Villa Crespo and Salon El Pial. He **never traveled internationally** — a purely local social dancer. In his final weeks, he was "out of breath and unable to walk." Family members reportedly all lived to 98.

**"Revert Segarra"** returned zero results across all search queries. This is almost certainly a mishearing or alternate rendering of "Roberto Segarra." No separate individual by this name exists in any tango documentation found.

**References:** jantango.wordpress.com/2018/11/23/roberto-segarra-10/ (obituary); jantango.wordpress.com/2023/01/16/like-a-high-school-class-reunion/ (Salon El Pial memories).

### 14. Pocho y Nelly — sweethearts reunited

**Full names:** Roberto "Pocho" Carreras (born ~1931; died September 8, 2012, age 81, of cancer) and **Nélida "Nelly" Fernando**. From barrio Pompeya, Buenos Aires.

Pocho worked for Argentina's most important electricity company, **handling 220,000-volt cables** — a job he said taught him generosity because "nobody could keep a secret because it could mean the death of another." He started dancing at age 16 (~1947) and progressed through Club Patagones, Club Estados Unidos, Franja de Oro, Club ANBA, and Club Oeste, where he said he "really learned this art."

Their love story is tango mythology. Pocho and Nelly were sweethearts when she was 16 and he was 20, then **separated for 30 years** over romantic complications. They reunited at a milonga. They married, separated, continued dancing together, and officially married again **two months before Pocho's death**. His philosophy: "The woman dances, not the man. She caresses the floor with her feet to express the music for both of them." Their signature move was described by Nelly: "My husband puts his foot back and where he takes his foot out, I put mine. A synchronization that nobody can achieve." They performed exhibitions at Salón Canning, El Beso, Glorias Argentinas, Sin Rumbo, and appeared on "Tango de Pura Cepa" on Solo Tango TV. Preferred music: **Osvaldo Pugliese**, recordings from 1946 onward. They taught in Buenos Aires and gave seminars in the US beginning around 2000.

**References:** soymilonguera.com/despedida-para-pocho-bailar-hasta-el-ultimo-dia/ (obituary); escuelatangoba.com/marcelosolis/pocho-y-nelly/; travesiasdigital.com/destinos/milongas-en-buenos-aires/; tangoba.org/actividad/ultima-tanda-milongueras-y-milongueros-de-buenos-aires/ (documentary featuring Nélida Fernando).

### 16. Elba Celia Biscay — "You have to like tango before you learn to dance it"

**Full name:** Elba Celia Biscay. **Born:** February 26, 1941. **Status:** Active as of at least 2023 (age ~82).

One of Buenos Aires's most respected milongueras, Biscay has danced for many decades and is known for her **beautiful footwork**. Janis Kenyon wrote: "New female dancers should study Elba and learn from this milonguera." She was **the only milonguera interviewed** for the TV series "Códigos de Milongas" on Solo Tango channel (broadcast July 8, 2006), where she discussed how much things had changed since her youth — women never wore pants to dance in her early days. Her advice to women: "Wear colored clothing rather than all black to tell everyone 'estoy aquí.'" She **also leads**, though only for fun at parties, not at milongas. In her early days, she went to Confitería Sans Souci and Club Monte Carlo on Avenida Corrientes.

She competed in the **III World Tango Championship** (2005) finals with Osvaldo Centeno, placed 10th in Milonga with Antonio Yuffré at the 2006 Metropolitan Championship, and 5th in Senior Tango at the 2008 Metropolitan. She performed regularly in **Milonguisimo** shows organized by Oscar Hector Malagrino at Confitería Ideal and Salon El Pial. She has **never traveled internationally**.

**References:** jantango.wordpress.com/2023/02/26/elba-celia-biscay-3/; tangoandchaos.org/chapt_6school/4boundaries.htm; dance-forums.com (tandem tango discussion).

### 18 & 19. Carlos Perez and Rosa Forte — "maestros of the maestros"

**Full names:** Carlos Pérez (born ~1939–1940) and **Rosa Forte** (often mistakenly called "Rosa Perez"; she uses her maiden name). **Location:** Buenos Aires.

Carlos began dancing in Buenos Aires milongas in **1952–1953 at age 13**, learning by observing milongueros including Lampazo (José Vázquez), Gerardo Portalea, Alberto Villarrazo, and Mingo Canónico. From 1955, he gave exhibitions at Club Pinocho, Glorias Argentinas, Viento Norte, and Sin Rumbo. After marrying Rosa, they **stopped dancing for approximately 30 years** — it was socially unacceptable for married couples to attend milongas, and tango was in decline. In 1994, their friend Lampazo invited them back. When Lampazo died, they took over his classes at **Club Sunderland in Villa Urquiza**.

Called **"maestros of the maestros,"** their style preserves the tango of the 1950s: smooth, poised elegance with slow walking, many pauses matching the music. "Bailá con la música" (Dance with the music) is Carlos's constant instruction. Their classes are undivided by levels — they teach posture, embrace, musicality, and cadence, not choreographed sequences. They particularly enjoy improvising on D'Agostino's "Café Dominguez."

Their students include multiple **World Champions**: Sebastián Achaval and Ximena Galliccio (2005), María Inés Bogado and Sebastián Jiménez (2010), Noelia Hurtado (Metropolitan champion), and many others. They taught internationally in France, Italy, Spain, Switzerland, Germany, Belgium, and Japan. They appeared in Robert Duvall's **"Assassination Tango"** (2002).

**References:** tanguito.co.uk/blog/rosa-carlos-perez/; soymilonguera.com/carlos-perez-y-rosa-forte-sobre-la-esencia-del-tango/; tangoclasicomoderno.weebly.com/carlos-y-rosa-perez.html; tango.info/wiki/Carlos_Perez_y_Rosa_Forte; tangomeet.com (The Legacy chapter); abrazo-tango.fr.

### 20. El Chino Perico — Villa Urquiza's eternal milonguero

**Full name:** Ricardo Ponce. **Nickname:** El Chino Perico. **Born:** July 10, 1931, Villa Urquiza, Buenos Aires. **Status:** Still alive and dancing in his 90s as of 2022.

A bureaucrat at the **Ministry of Economy** by day, Perico has been dancing since age 13. He idolized **Luis "Milonguita" Lemos**, saying: "His clothes and shoes were made to measure, he looked uniquely elegant and as a dancer he was like an angel. He was the best and has not been surpassed." His style features long steps (paso largo), eternal pauses, and whispered lyrics following the singer. He dances to single and double time rhythms, intersperses pauses matching musical variations.

**He has never left Buenos Aires.** A legendary bohemian, he goes to sleep at 6 AM, wakes at 6 PM, eats steak with fries at 3–4 AM after milongas, washing down whisky with red wine. His favorite toast: "porque estamos" (because we're here). His favorite orchestra is Miguel Caló. He dances at Sin Rumbo, Sunderland, Salon Canning, Lo de Celia, and Club Nuevo Gricel. He danced regularly with **Ada Peloso** and **Paola Tacchetti**.

He was among the maestros at **ACETA** (Academia de Estilos del Tango Argentino) and was named a "living treasure" alongside Parejita, Flaco Dany, and Osvaldo y Coca in a **La Nación** article (2007). He is the subject of the short documentary **"A Tango with Chino Perico"** (2020, dir. Jing Wang).

### 21. Paola Tacchetti

**Full name:** Paola Tacchetti (note double 'c' and 'h'). **Location:** Buenos Aires.

Tacchetti is a Buenos Aires-based tango dancer, trained in classical dance, who partners with **Pancho Martínez Pey** and **Nany Peralta** among others. She was a **regular dance partner of El Chino Perico** at milongas and in demonstrations. She should be distinguished from **Florencia Taccetti**, a more extensively documented tango teacher who was a protégée of Graciela González, appeared in the film "Tango Bar" (1987), created the instructional video "Technique and Adornment for Followers," and taught extensively in the US. The two may be related (similar surname) but are distinct individuals.

### 22. Ernesto "Pupi" Castello — "the most flamboyant of the milongueros"

**Full name:** Ernesto Norberto Castello. **Nicknames:** Pupi, Puppy, Pupy (he said: "It should be 'puppy'"). **Born:** December 18, 1935, Balvanera, Buenos Aires. **Died:** July 20, 2007.

Pupi learned tango in the 1940s during the Golden Age, practicing "the tango danced between men." Like many milongueros, he stopped dancing for 20–25 years, returning during the 1980s revival. He was the **most flamboyant** of the old milongueros — "flashy ganchos, risqué wraps, sacadas, and can even reverse the embrace." His style was the 1940s northern Buenos Aires neighborhood type: long straight lines punctuated with sudden complicated movements. "Times change. Maybe youngsters are dancing the tango of the future. But I like my own style. It's the style of the '40s. It's got rhythm and elegance. Young people move like elephants."

He deeply respected Gerardo Portalea, saying in the BBC documentary "La Confitería Ideal": "Most of the old milongueros, other than Portalea, are dead now." At home in Balvanera, he was "a venerable father and grandfather" in T-shirt, Bermuda shorts, and slippers. Even during his final hospitalization, he **taught tango to the doctors and nurses**.

**Key partnerships:** **Graciela González** (approximately 20-year partnership — she calls him "my mentor, my dance partner and also a father for me"). Also danced with Guillermina Quiroga and Geraldine Rojas. **Appearances:** BBC 4 documentary "La Confitería Ideal: The Tango Salon" (2005); "Bridge to the Tango" instructional videos; stage show "Danza Maligna" (2003, Théâtre de Chaillot, Paris). He taught at **ACETA** (Academia de Estilos de Tango Argentino).

**References:** ultimatetango.com/blog/tango-legends-pupi-castello; todotango.com/english/history/chronicle/508/Confessions-by-Puppy-Castello/; jantango.wordpress.com/2008/12/18/pupy-castello/; clarin.com (obituary); escuelatangoba.com.

### 23. Graciela González — "La Leona del Tango"

**Full name:** Graciela González. **Nickname:** La Leona del Tango (The Lioness of Tango). **Born:** Buenos Aires (began dancing January 9, 1988). **Status:** Actively teaching and touring, celebrating 35+ years in tango.

González is a worldwide-known tango dancer, teacher, and researcher, **highly considered among traditional milongueros in Buenos Aires**. She created the **first Followers' Technique seminar** (January 1994), the first **Leaders' Technique/Marcación seminar** (June 1995), and **AntiTécnica** (June 2016). She co-founded the first **Association of Teachers, Dancers and Choreographers of Argentine Tango (AMBCTA)** in April 2001. She has served as jury for the **Tango World Cup** since its inception.

Her most important mentor and partner was **Pupi Castello** (approximately 20 years together until his death in 2007). She also studied with Pepito Avellaneda, Antonio Todaro, Gustavo Naveira, Tete Rusconi, El Turco José, Gerardo Portalea, and many others — making her a living connective thread among nearly all the milongueros in this report. Her **disciples** include Mora Godoy, Lorena Ermocida, Corina de la Rosa, Fernanda Ghi, Chicho Frumboli, Horacio Godoy, Fernando Galera, and many more. She tours internationally across Europe, and an academic paper, "GRACIELA GONZÁLEZ: Bodily Experience and Generational Change in Traditional Tango," was published on ResearchGate (2021).

### 24. Gerardo Portalea — "dancing the phrase, not the rhythm"

**Full name:** Gerardo Portalea. **Born:** August 27, 1928 (some sources say 1929). **Died:** 2007, Buenos Aires. **Correct spelling:** Portalea (not Portaleo).

Portalea was one of the defining dancers of the **Villa Urquiza style**. When asked who was the best dancer in the north of the city, "the answer always came back as 'Portalea.' I was told it was the way he interpreted the music that made him the best... **He wasn't dancing the rhythm of the music. He was dancing the phrase.**" Alex Krebs noted: "When we first watched Gerardo Portalea, we were struck by the beauty in the simplicity... it's only afterwards that you realize there wasn't anything 'fancy.'" Fellow dancers praised his "impressive cadencia" (cadence) and his distinctive use of the *vaivén* movement.

He worked all his life as a **gravedigger** (sepulturero). He described himself as a "bailarín de patio" (a patio dancer/non-professional). He maintained a **17-year absence from dance** then returned, keeping "unwavering fidelity to the pistas of Villa Urquiza." He danced at Sin Rumbo, Sunderland, and Akarense. He appeared in the films **"Tango, Baile Nuestro"** (1988) — in a famous milonga scene with Finito and Miguel Balmaceda — and **"Assassination Tango"** (2002). Robert Duvall was deeply moved by his dancing.

**Dance partners:** Susanna (see below), Martha Portalea (likely a relative). **Students:** Yanina Quiñones and Neri Piliu, Hernán Alvarez Prieto, Carlos Perez and Rosa Forte. He taught at ACETA. He and Pupi Castello **died within a month of each other** in 2007.

### 26. Finito (Ramón Rivera) — "a monument of ideas made into movement"

**Full name:** Ramón Rivera. **Nicknames:** Finito, Fino. **Born:** March 24, 1929, La Paternal, Buenos Aires. **Died:** May 11, 1987, at the milonga Akarense, Donado 1355, Villa Urquiza — **died of a heart attack while dancing**. **Wife:** María Teresa (married 1954; she died September 19, 1999, age 69). **Children:** Diana, Alicia (both lawyers), and Ramón (merchant).

Despite being synonymous with Villa Urquiza style, Finito was **not from Villa Urquiza** but from La Paternal (confirmed by Lampazo: "Finito era de Boyacá y Juan B. Justo. Tirando más a La Paternal"). His dancing was described by Carlos Estévez "Petróleo" as **"a monument of ideas made into movement"** — "His dancing was a conjunction of forms in search of beauty... Sometimes he glided without touching the floor; other times, he caressed it." Oscar Coria declared: "Finito's style got lost; now there is nobody who dances like him. He used to make a turn so particular that when others tried to reproduce it, it was impossible for them to maintain their balance." Petróleo called him "a magician of balance."

By trade, Finito was a **mechanic**. He started as a semi-professional at Club Floreal in La Paternal, then **stopped dancing for approximately 26 years** during tango's decline. He returned in 1980 at Club Vélez Sársfield with María Teresa. She recalled: "He believed that, with so many years without dancing, new things would have been invented. But it was the same. He looked at me, smiled, and said: 'But we're still like the first day.'"

He **never traveled internationally**. Robert Duvall saw him dance and proposed he participate in his next film, but when Duvall called from the US, Finito had already died. He appeared in **"Tango, Baile Nuestro"** (1988, filmed before his death). María Teresa recalled his end: "We finished dancing a tango and he got dizzy. We moved to the side and there he stayed. His heart failed. **He died as he wanted — dancing.**"

## What this generation gave tango before nuevo

The milonguero generation's most profound contribution was **preservation through embodiment**. During three decades when tango nearly died — suppressed by dictatorships, displaced by rock and cumbia, socially stigmatized — these dancers carried the art form in their bodies. They did not write treatises or create curricula. They simply kept dancing.

When they returned to the milongas in the 1980s, they brought back something that could not have been reconstructed from recordings or photographs: the feel of the embrace, the weight of the walk, the instinct for navigation, the intuitive response to orchestras they had heard since childhood. **Gustavo Naveira**, the intellectual architect of tango nuevo, explicitly studied these milongueros — learning from Pepito Avellaneda, Antonio Todaro, Virulazo, and Gerardo Portalea — before attempting to analyze and systematize what they did. As Fabián Salas said in 2001: "What did we invent? Nothing... Everything was already there."

The clash between old and new was real but productive. Chicho Frumboli, a nuevo pioneer, later reflected: "They are completely lost! I learnt with the last great milongueros, I took the information directly." The nuevo movement's contribution was analytical — explaining "how to dance" rather than "what to dance" — but its raw material came entirely from the generation documented here. By the mid-2000s, the lines blurred: few dancers in Buenos Aires identified as strictly "nuevo," and the traditional codes reasserted themselves.

What distinguishes these 26 dancers is their ordinariness outside the milonga. A pizza maker, a gravedigger, a mechanic, a utility worker, a bureaucrat — people who held down jobs, raised families, and then transformed nightly into artists of extraordinary subtlety. Their legacy is not choreography but a way of being in the music and with another person that continues to define what tango means.

## Conclusion: a vanishing world, imperfectly preserved

Several patterns emerge from this research. First, these dancers existed on a spectrum from the **completely undocumented** (Nelly Acosta, Adela Galeazzi, Santiago Cantenys) to the **extensively chronicled** (Pepito Avellaneda, Tete Rusconi, Finito). The difference often depended on whether a single dedicated observer — Janis Kenyon, Rick McGarrey, Gustavo Benzecry Sabá — happened to interview or film them. Second, nearly all followed the same arc: learning to dance in the 1940s–1950s, stopping for decades during tango's decline, then returning in the 1980s–1990s with their style intact. Third, the overwhelming majority never left Buenos Aires — international teaching was the exception, not the rule, and usually came late in life (El Turco José at 73, Osvaldo and Coca after their 2004 championship win).

What is most striking is how these ordinary people articulated extraordinary philosophies of dance. Pepito the pizza maker saying "You can copy my steps, but I grow with milonga." Portalea the gravedigger dancing phrases rather than rhythms. Finito the mechanic whose turns were physically impossible for others to reproduce. Ismael the minimalist who "knows dozens of steps and figures, but doesn't use them." Their collective wisdom — that tango lives in the connection between two people and the music, not in the complexity of figures — remains the deepest insight the art form has produced.
