# The Masters Before Nuevo: A Research Guide to Pre-Nuevo Argentine Tango Dancers, Teachers & Performers

*A comprehensive research document on the milongueros, maestros, and milongueras who shaped Argentine tango before the nuevo era*

---

## The Era: Understanding Pre-Nuevo Tango

Before tango nuevo emerged in the early 2000s — with its emphasis on open embrace, off-axis movements, and analytical deconstruction of tango mechanics — there existed a rich, deeply rooted world of social tango. The dancers in this document lived through, and in many cases bridged, three critical periods:

**The Golden Age (c. 1935–1955):** Tango's zenith. Over fifty orchestras played nightly across Buenos Aires in salons, cafes, cabarets, and neighborhood sports clubs. Juan D'Arienzo ("El Rey del Compás") returned tango to its rhythmic roots in the mid-1930s, filling dance floors. Aníbal Troilo debuted in 1937 and pushed the music forward. Every barrio had its own club — Sin Rumbo and Sunderland in Villa Urquiza, Club Estados Unidos and Club ANBA in the south. Boys learned by watching, then practicing man-to-man for months or years before being allowed onto the floor with women. Only the best survived socially.

**The Decline (c. 1955–1983):** After the military overthrew Perón, nightlife was crushed through curfews and harassment. Rock-and-roll, folk music, and tropical rhythms swept through youth culture. Tango salons closed, orchestras dissolved. Many of the great dancers stopped for 20–30 years — they married, had families, and moved on. But the dance lived in their bodies.

**The Revival (c. 1983–2000):** The Broadway show *Tango Argentino* (1983 Paris, 1985 New York) reignited worldwide interest. Democracy returned to Argentina. The old milongueros came back to the dance floors, and a new generation sought them out. Susana Miller studied with Tete Rusconi and codified "estilo milonguero." Teachers like Antonio Todaro, Pepito Avellaneda, and Pupi Castello began transmitting what they knew to eager students from around the world. This was a narrow window — many of these masters were already elderly, and this document captures the figures who made that transmission possible.

### How Tango Was Learned

There were no formal classes during the Golden Age. Boys watched older dancers from the balcony or the edge of the floor. They practiced among themselves — dancing both roles — for months before attempting the real thing. Women learned on the floor from experienced partners. The knowledge was oral, physical, and deeply personal. Each dancer developed their own style within the framework of their neighborhood's tradition.

### Two Broad Style Families

**Estilo del Centro / Milonguero Style:** Developed on the packed floors of downtown confiterías. Close, maintained embrace (apilado — slight forward lean). Compact, circular movements. Musical interpretation focused on rhythm and pauses. Representative figures: Ricardo Vidort, Tete Rusconi.

**Estilo del Barrio / Villa Urquiza Style:** Developed in the larger club floors of outer neighborhoods. Upright posture. Embrace that can open for turns and ochos. Elegant walking close to the ground, with sacadas, paradas, and refined footwork. Representative figures: Gerardo Portalea, Carlos Pérez & Rosa Forte, El Turco José, Finito.

### Ricardo Vidort — "The Last Compadrito"

**Full Name:** Ricardo Vidort
**Nickname:** "The Last Compadrito"
**Born:** August 30, 1929, Buenos Aires
**Died:** May 21, 2006, Santa Fe, New Mexico, USA
**Style:** Milonguero / close embrace

**Life & Tango:** Ricardo danced tango for 53 years and was one of fewer than a dozen people who still danced the milonguero style popularized in the 1920s. He was highly educated, holding a degree in parapsychology, and had the mind of a philosopher. But his life was tango — he was described as "almost certainly the last of the old-time milongueros, a compadrito really, whose entire life was dedicated to tango and nothing else."

**Style & Philosophy:** Vidort rejected step-based teaching entirely. He said: "We don't agree with teaching steps. We want you to feel the music. You need to understand your own rhythm." He believed he could transmit everything he had to offer in seven classes. His dancing was characterized by exquisite musicality, emotional depth, and an unmistakable personal style rooted in feeling rather than figures.

**Venues & Travel:** A regular at El Beso and the Confitería Ideal in Buenos Aires. He performed internationally at tango festivals across Europe, North America, and Asia, including events in Paris, Rome, and Japan.

**Partners:** Danced with Myriam Pincen (documented in 2004 footage). His life partner was Ewa Kiełczewska, who created a memorial website documenting his life and philosophy.

**Teaching:** He was generous with his time but expected students to practice on their own. He did not care to make money beyond what was necessary.

**Legacy:** His unselfishness and generosity were legendary. He spent his final days in Santa Fe, New Mexico, surrounded by love, flowers, nature, and his favorite tango music.

### Pedro "Tete" Rusconi — "Reyes de la Noche"

**Full Name:** Pedro Alberto Rusconi
**Nickname:** "Tete"
**Born:** January 9, 1936, Pompeya, Buenos Aires
**Died:** January 7, 2010 (two days before his 74th birthday)
**Style:** Salon tango in close embrace / milonguero style

**Life & Tango:** Tete danced tango for almost 60 years. Born in the working-class barrio of Pompeya, he was a porteño through and through. He possessed what German choreographer Pina Bausch described as "an orchestra in his head" — his natural musicality was so stunning that Bausch asked him to teach her company. He debuted internationally with the tango "Pavadita" in Paris.

**Style & Philosophy:** Tete's style was playful, fluid, and deeply musical — especially in vals, which became his signature. He developed a unique form of close-embrace tango he called "salon tango" (others labeled it milonguero style). His philosophy was clear: "The tango is a feeling. It is not difficult to learn. Nor is it easy. But it is not danced by figures and steps. It is danced to the music."

**Partners:** His earlier partner Maria Villelobos helped develop the foundational style. From 1995 onward, his teaching and performing partner was Silvia Ceriani, with whom he became internationally famous. They were known particularly for their beautiful vals.

**Travel & Teaching:** Tete was one of the first to teach close embrace in the U.S. (around 1994, under the auspices of Susana Miller). Within a few years, he and Silvia were teaching around the world. They produced instructional videos including *Fundamentals of Salon Tango*.

**Legacy:** His passing in 2010 was mourned globally. He influenced generations of dancers worldwide and is considered one of the definitive milongueros of the 20th century.

### Silvia Ceriani — Writer, Artist, Partner of Tete

**Full Name:** Silvia Ceriani
**Role:** Dancer, writer, artist, DJ, teaching partner of Tete Rusconi (from 1995)

**Life & Tango:** Silvia was a writer and artist who became Tete's teaching partner in 1995. Together they became one of the most celebrated partnerships in tango history. While Tete had developed his foundational style with Maria Villelobos, it was with Silvia that he achieved international fame.

**Contributions:** Beyond dancing, Silvia served as a milonga DJ and maintained a web presence documenting their work. They co-produced instructional videos covering posture, lead and follow, walking, changes of direction, ochos, turns, pauses, and musical interpretation in the apilado embrace.

**Legacy:** Silvia was instrumental in helping Tete reach a global audience. Their partnership demonstrated that milonguero tango was not just a historical artifact but a living, evolving art form.

### Pepito Avellaneda — "The King of the Milonga"

**Full Name:** José Domingo Monteleone
**Nickname:** "Pepito Avellaneda" (from the Carnival celebrations in Avellaneda where he began dancing)
**Born:** November 30, 1930, Buenos Aires (Italian and Spanish ancestry)
**Died:** April 29, 1996
**Style:** Orillero / milonguero

**Life & Tango:** Pepito started dancing at twelve at Carnival celebrations. He described learning entirely by trial and self-discovery: "It was a question of trying, to make a step, another step." Like all dancers of his era, he practiced in men-only sessions at clubs, taking turns leading and following.

**Style & Philosophy:** Known as "The King of the Milonga," Pepito was celebrated for his cheeky, endearing style — especially in vals and milonga. His Orillero style came from the poor outer neighborhoods of Buenos Aires and featured creative improvisation, sharp musicality, and an irresistible personality on the floor. He said he "fed on tango" and spent all day long teaching.

**Partners:** His primary performing and teaching partner was Suzuki (Gilda Suzuki de Souza). Together they performed at the Galería del Tango Argentino in Boedo, taught in Café Max in San Cristóbal, and traveled internationally. They lived together in San Cristóbal.

**Travel & Teaching:** Pepito taught at the International Argentine Tango Congress at Northwestern University (Evanston, Illinois, 1995). He and Suzuki taught in Paris and across Europe. A tango school in Amsterdam identifies him as their teacher from 1991–1996. In Europe, he was deeply loved, receiving constant letters and invitations.

**Legacy:** Pepito influenced generations — Graciela González, Myriam Pincen, and many others studied with him. His death in 1996 was a significant loss to the tango community.

### Ernesto Norberto "Pupi" Castello — "El Maestro de los Maestros"

**Full Name:** Ernesto Norberto Castello
**Nickname:** "Pupi" (an Englishwoman friend of his mother called him "puppy" at birth — the family misheard it as "Pupi")
**Born:** c. 1935–1936, Buenos Aires
**Died:** July 21, 2007
**Style:** Tango Salón / Estilo del Barrio

**Life & Tango:** Pupi began dancing at age 15 during the Golden Age and never stopped — except for the 20–25 year hiatus that most milongueros experienced from the late 1950s to the early 1980s. He worked as a regular laborer, and tango was his parallel life. He lived through "the glorious era, the decline, and the phenomenal comeback of tango de pista."

**Style & Philosophy:** His style was elegant, walked, sober yet rich, deeply musical — the essence of tango-salón. His most famous teaching was: "Figures are easy. Walking is hard." He represented the Estilo del Barrio tradition — upright posture, movement into and out of the embrace, with the walk as the foundation of everything.

**Partners & Students:** His most important partnership was with Graciela González — together for nearly 20 years of teaching and performing internationally. Their collaboration helped usher in a tango renaissance. His students read like a who's who of modern tango: Guillermina Quiroga, Geraldine Rojas, Analía Centurión, and countless others. Two of the greatest tango followers ever — Quiroga and Rojas — both name Pupi as their primary influence.

**Film & Stage:** Appeared in *Danza Maligna* (2003, Théâtre de Chaillot, Paris), the BBC 4 documentary *La Confiteria Ideal: The Tango Salon* (2005), and the film *Maestros Milongueros* (2007).

**Legacy:** He warned near the end of his life: "Most of the old milongueros, other than Portalea, are dead now. That's why we have to encourage the young people. Otherwise, tango will die with us." He passed away just months after Gerardo Portalea in 2007.

### Graciela González — "La Leona del Tango"

**Full Name:** Graciela González
**Born:** Buenos Aires
**Style:** Traditional Salon Tango (with pioneering pedagogical approach)

**Life & Tango:** Graciela started dancing Argentine tango in 1988 — relatively late compared to the old milongueros, but she threw herself into studying with the greatest dancers alive. Her teachers included Pupi Castello (her most important teacher and 20-year dance partner), Pepito Avellaneda, Antonio Todaro, Gustavo Naveira, Juan Bruno, Tete Rusconi, Martha Anton, Manolo, El Turco José, Lampazo, Eduardo Pareja, and Nito y Elba.

**Revolutionary Teaching Method:** Graciela was the first teacher to develop seminars using a technical approach to tango. She created the groundbreaking **Followers' Technique** workshop (1994) and **Leaders' Technique** workshop (1995) — programs that were emulated even in Buenos Aires. More recently, she developed an approach called "anti-técnica," contrasting traditional technique with a more organic, intuitive method. Her pedagogical system is now taught worldwide by certified instructors under the "Método Graciela González."

**Students (Partial List):** Mora Godoy, Lorena Ermocida, Corina de la Rosa, Florencia Taccetti, Fernanda Ghi, Karina Romero, Tamara Juhan, Tomás Howlin, Fernando Galera, Horacio Godoy, Ramiro Gigliotti, and many more. She refers to Martha Anton as "mi madre de tango."

**Academic Recognition:** Her work has been the subject of academic research, including studies on the bodily experience and generational change in traditional tango, analyzing how the gestural and intentional characteristics of the old milongueros were transmitted through her partnership with Pupi Castello.

### Ramón "Finito" Rivera — The Finest Social Dancer of the 1980s

**Full Name:** Ramón Rivera
**Nickname:** "Finito" (also sometimes "Fino")
**Born:** c. 1932
**Died:** 1987 (aged approximately 55)
**Style:** Villa Urquiza / Salon

**Life & Tango:** Finito was considered the best social tango dancer of the 1980s Buenos Aires revival. Like many of his generation, he had stopped dancing for 20–25 years before returning in the early 1980s. His comeback coincided with tango's resurgence, and he became the standard-bearer for the elegant Villa Urquiza style.

**Style:** His dancing was smooth, elegant, and precise — the epitome of what became branded as "Villa Urquiza style." He walked close to the ground with an upright posture, opening the embrace for turns and ochos, and maintained a firm, straight delivery of the foot in long, caressing steps.

**Partners:** He danced with Maria Teresa. They appear together in the film *Tango: Baile Nuestro* (Jorge Zanada) and in Miguel Angel Zotto's video *Perfumes de Tango* — the only recording that clearly identifies and credits Finito.

**Influence:** Finito directly taught or influenced Miguel Angel Zotto, who went on to spread Argentine tango worldwide through his production *Tango x 2*. Zotto learned from the best — Todaro, Pepito Avellaneda, Copes, Finito, Petróleo — and Finito's elegant salon style was a cornerstone of that education.

**Tragedy:** Finito died young, at roughly 55, in 1987 — just as tango was fully reviving. His early death meant he never saw the global explosion that his style would inspire.

### Carlos Pérez & Rosa Forte — Living Memory of the 1940s & 50s

**Full Names:** Carlos Pérez and Rosa Forte (sometimes called Rosa Pérez)
**Style:** Villa Urquiza / Tango Salón of the 1950s
**Nickname:** "Maestros de los Maestros" (Masters of the Masters)

**Life & Tango:** Carlos began dancing in the milongas of Buenos Aires in 1952. At that time, no formal classes existed — you learned by watching experienced dancers. Rosa started a bit later, guided by her older brothers. After marrying, they stopped dancing around 1964 — it was frowned upon for young couples to go to milongas, and tango was fading.

**The 30-Year Time Capsule:** By chance, they encountered José "Lampazo" Vázquez, who invited Carlos to help at Club Sin Rumbo. In 1994, they began dancing professionally again — after nearly 30 years away. Having not danced since the early 1960s, they had inadvertently preserved the tango of the 1940s and 50s in their bodies like a time capsule, which created enormous excitement among younger dancers eager to learn the authentic style.

**Style:** They dance together to the melody, with poise, very slowly, taking many pauses as the music pauses — creating a feeling of suspension and elegance. They mark rhythmical sections with sacadas, paradas, and giros. Their style is more balanced and synchronized between partners compared to some other Villa Urquiza dancers.

**Teaching & Legacy:** When Lampazo passed away, Carlos and Rosa naturally took his place at Club Sunderland in Villa Urquiza. They trained World Tango Salon Champions including Sebastián Achaval & Ximena Galliccio (2005 Champions), Francisco Forquera, Natalia Hills, Jesús Velásquez, and María Inés Bogado. They have traveled the world — Paris, Tokyo, Rome — performing and teaching.

### José "El Turco" Brahemcha — Co-Creator of Tango Urquiza

**Full Name:** José Brahemcha
**Nickname:** "El Turco" (The Turk) or "El Turco José"
**Born:** July 17, 1931, Saavedra, Buenos Aires
**Style:** Urquiza / Salon

**Life & Tango:** Born in the barrio of Saavedra, El Turco dedicated himself to cycling as a young man but danced across every neighborhood in Buenos Aires and its surroundings. Together with his friend Luis "Milonguita" Lemos, he co-created what became known as the Urquiza tango style between 1945 and 1956.

**Style & Philosophy:** The Urquiza style is characterized by elegance, sobriety, finesse, and great precision in walking — always gliding close to the ground, with refined musical interpretation. El Turco's contribution was particularly notable in his entry to the ocho, which became a hallmark of the style. His dancing mixed suspension and rhythmic footwork. He always sought the right moment to step onto the floor — it was important to him to dance comfortably, not on a too-crowded floor. He taught: "The dancer dances what he is."

**International Impact:** Although the Urquiza style was revered in Argentina, its masters had never traveled outside the country — until El Turco, at age 73, went to Copenhagen where his student Linus Aabye had established a school dedicated to Tango Urquiza. His influence continues today through schools like Urquiza Tango Berlin and events like Herencia del Tango.

**Legacy:** El Turco and Milonguita together stand as the very symbols of Urquiza tango. Their creation represents one of the most well-developed and consistent cultural traditions within the tango world.

### Gerardo Portalea — The Standard of Villa Urquiza

**Full Name:** Gerardo Portalea
**Born:** August 27, 1928
**Died:** June 2, 2007 (aged 78)
**Style:** Villa Urquiza / Tango Salón
**Day Job:** Gravedigger (sepulturero) at San Martín Cemetery

**Life & Tango:** Portalea's approach to tango began just after childhood, watching practices between men at Club Sin Rumbo. He was a serious, modest, and reserved man who worked his entire life as a gravedigger. After a severe depression, a psychiatrist recommended he return to dancing — his comeback anticipated by a few years the triumphant return of tango itself.

**Style:** In terms of posture, maintaining the line, the chest-to-chest hold and the embrace, Gerardo Portalea was *the standard*. His tango was elegant, walked, sober and rich, deeply musical. He liked to describe himself as a "bailarín de patio" (patio dancer). Observers noted he did not have a great variation of steps, but had "an impressive cadencia."

**Partners:** Danced with "Susanna" (documented in videos from 2007) and also with Martha Portalea (possibly a family member). He only gave classes sporadically and informally. He refused to dance professionally, saying he only went out when an orchestra truly inspired him.

**Travel:** Despite his modesty, he traveled to Chile, France, Germany, and Norway for classes and demonstrations. His style was documented by European filmmakers and in the film *Tango, Bayle Nuestro* by Jorge Zanada.

**Influence:** Claudia Codega, Esteban Moreno, Analía Centurión, and many others studied with him. Geraldine Rojas was performing with Portalea at age 11.

**Legacy:** He maintained unwavering fidelity to the dance floors of Villa Urquiza until his death. He is buried at the same cemetery where he worked his entire life.

### Myriam Pincen — Keeper of the Tradition

**Full Name:** Myriam Pincen
**Role:** Milonguera, maestra, instructor at Escuela de Tango de Buenos Aires
**Experience:** 30+ years of dancing and teaching

**Life & Tango:** Myriam Pincen is an outstanding milonguera based in Buenos Aires who has been dancing for over three decades. She studied with many of the greatest teachers of the Golden Age generation: Miguel Gutiérrez, Eduardo Arquimbau, Mingo Pugliese, Pepito Avellaneda, and J.C. Copes, learning not only to dance but also to teach dance, choreography, and stage presence.

**Teaching:** Her classes cover the full spectrum of tango de salón: posture, musicality, balance, cadence, styles, different orchestras, lead and follow, adornments, milonga codes, and floor craft. She is part of the staff at the Escuela de Tango de Buenos Aires alongside Blas Catrenau and Néstor Pellicciaro.

**Partners:** She danced with the great Ricardo Vidort (documented in 2004 footage). She preserved an important undated note from Jorge Dispari about the importance of the walk, floorcraft, and tradition in social tango, which she shared publicly in 2025.

### Osvaldo & Coca Cartery — 2004 World Champions

**Known As:** Osvaldo y Coca Cartery
**Style:** Tango Salón / Milonguero
**Achievement:** 2004 Tango World Champions

**Life & Tango:** Osvaldo was born in Argentina and has been intimately involved with tango culture his entire life through family and community. He has worked as a tango performer, choreographer, and instructor for over 30 years. Together with Coca, they represent the bridge between authentic milonguero tradition and competitive tango.

**Style:** Their dancing is characterized by asynchronicity — a technique where the partners are not always mirroring each other, creating a rich, conversational quality. They are recognized as "Maestros Milongueros," and their performances are used to illustrate what is often hidden behind the flashy performances of stage stars — the "hidden world of traditional tango dancing."

**Venues:** Regulars at El Beso milonga and PractiMilonguero in Buenos Aires.

### Ricardo "El Chino Perico" Ponce — Living Link to Villa Urquiza

**Full Name:** Ricardo Ponce
**Nickname:** "El Chino Perico"
**Born:** c. July 10, 1941 (though he reportedly won't admit his age)
**Style:** Villa Urquiza

**Life & Tango:** El Chino Perico is one of the last master milongueros directly connected to the Villa Urquiza lineage. His idol and teacher was Luis "Milonguita" Lemos, the co-founder of the Urquiza style. Through this direct transmission, El Chino bridges the 1940s origins of Urquiza tango to the present day.

**Style & Philosophy:** He is known for smoothness and subtlety. He lamented the homogenization of tango, telling the Buenos Aires daily Clarín: "Today, everybody dances the same." Like El Turco José, he values choosing the right moment to step onto the dance floor and avoids overcrowded floors.

**Partnerships:** Documented dancing with partners including Pamela Natalia Mármol, Cinthia Wilson, Aurora Lubiz, Malee Rodriguez, and Lorena Ermocida.

**Legacy:** He has contributed to the edifice of tango alongside figures like Osvaldo y Coca Cartery and singer Alberto Podestá. His style is cited as a clear example of the classic Villa Urquiza aesthetic.

### Ismael El Jalil (Heljalil) — The Minimalist Milonguero

**Full Name:** Ismael El Jalil (also spelled Heljalil)
**Born:** August 30, 1930
**Style:** Milonguero / close embrace

**Life & Tango:** Ismael was a traditional milonguero of Buenos Aires, part of the generation born around 1930 who learned tango during the Golden Age. He was a habitué of milongas including Maipú 444 and Lo de Celia (in Barrio Constitución). When Maipú 444 closed, he shifted to Lo de Celia.

**Style:** The milongueros viejos are minimalists, and Ismael embodied this principle. He walked and turned, focusing on the essentials — the music and his partner. Even with energizing music, he danced calmly. With his maintained embrace, he offered his partner serenity and peaceful movement — no rapid changes of direction, no off-axis disruptions. His tango was simple and elegant.

**Documentation:** Rick McGarrey of the Tango and Chaos website filmed Ismael as part of an important video archive of Buenos Aires milongueros who might otherwise have fallen into obscurity. There are few videos of him online, and most people outside Buenos Aires do not know his name — but those who do consider him a favorite.

### Pocho y Nelly (Nélida Fernando) — Elegance and Cadence

**Known As:** Pocho y Nelly
**Nelly's Full Name:** Nélida Fernando
**Style:** Milonguero / Tango Salón

**Life & Tango:** Back in 1947, when he was 16, Pocho practiced tango with other boys three or four times a week at Club Patagones. He soon graduated to the huge tracks of Club Estados Unidos and the Franja de Oro. Older dancers would arrive and teach them steps, correcting their mistakes. From his neighborhood Pompeya, he especially remembered a dancer named Tin who danced to Troilo with his partner Sarita. He later became a habitué of Club ANBA and Club Oeste, where he truly refined his art. Over time, he stood out in tango and even more in milonga.

**Partnership:** He teamed up with Nelly (Nélida Fernando) and they began a career of classes, exhibitions, and international tours. His name became a synonym of elegance and cadence.

**Venues:** Regular performers at El Beso milonga in Buenos Aires.

**Legacy:** They are recognized as maestros milongueros — masters of the social tango tradition.

**References:**
- [Escuela de Tango — Pocho y Nelly](https://escuelatangoba.com/marcelosolis/pocho-y-nelly/)

### Nito y Elba García — "The Masters of Masters"

**Full Names:** Nito García and Elba Sottile (née)
**Nito Born:** Buenos Aires Province
**Elba Born:** Junín, Buenos Aires Province
**Style:** Tango Salón

**Life & Tango:** Both Nito and Elba experienced devastating personal tragedies before finding each other. Nito's wife and mother died in a traffic accident just fifteen days after his wedding. Elba lost her husband in a separate traffic accident, left alone with two young daughters. They met at a dance — Nito in a light blue suit, Elba with impeccable blonde hair. He tried to invite her to dance all night; she wouldn't give him the time. They lost track of each other for five years, then reconnected and married within five months.

**Career:** Nito had started professionally by winning fifteen consecutive dance contests from 1955–1965 and performed with Osvaldo Pugliese's company in 1966. Elba didn't know tango before meeting Nito — he was her only teacher. Within a year, she was competing. Since 1970, "Nito y Elba" toured milongas across Buenos Aires Province, won the prestigious *Lobo del Mar* award, and received the *Master Artist of Dance* award from Stanford University.

**International Teaching:** From 1991 onward, they traveled to the United States and worldwide — San Francisco, LA, New York, Texas, Hawaii, Mexico, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay, and Jamaica. They are regular performers at Esquina Carlos Gardel and are invited to CITA every year. A 2018 documentary, *Nito y Elba: Nobleza de Arrabal*, captures their story.

**Influence:** Graciela González counts them among her formative teachers. They are often called "The Masters of Masters."

### Roberto Segarra — A Life Measured in Tandas

**Full Name:** Roberto Segarra
**Born:** September 16, 1920
**Died:** November 21, 2018 (aged 98)
**Style:** Social tango / milonguero

**Life & Tango:** Roberto Segarra was one of the oldest milongueros — a man who began dancing during his teenage years with no aspiration for it to become a profession. For people like Roberto, tango was not merely a dance but an integral part of the collective life in Buenos Aires, Montevideo, and Rosario. He frequented milongas with relatives and friends, formed connections, met his partners, and returned to the dance even through separation, divorce, and loss.

**Partners:** His longtime partner was Olga Gómez. Those who knew them loved watching them dance together — described as "a wonderful couple, simple but absolutely with the music and with each other. All what tango should be."

**Final Days:** Marta Famá, whose milonga he attended at Salón Rodríguez, went to see him at home during his final days. He was remembered for dancing with great energy even late in life — including memorable tandas of valses at Obelisco tango and salsa tandas at Lo de Celia.

**References:**
- [Tango Chamuyo — Roberto Segarra](https://jantango.wordpress.com/2018/11/23/roberto-segarra-10/)

### Elba Biscay — Milonguera of Salon Canning

**Full Name:** Elba Biscay
**Style:** Traditional milonguera

**What We Know:** Elba Biscay is recognized as a milonguera — an experienced social tango dancer from the traditional Buenos Aires milonga scene. She is frequently referenced alongside her partner Alberto Dassieu as exemplifying the authentic, music-focused style of tango. Dancing to "Valsecito criollo" by D'Arienzo, Dassieu and Biscay demonstrated pure social tango: no ganchos, volcadas, high boleos, or flashy figures — just the music, the walk, and the embrace, with ornamentation used sparingly.

She also appeared in a tandem tango exhibition at Salon Canning (2009), alongside Adela Galeazzi and a young male dancer (possibly her son), performing vals.

### Florencia Taccetti — Technique and Adornment

**Note:** The name on the original list was "Paola Taccetti." Research did not find a tango figure by that exact name. The most likely match is **Florencia Taccetti**, a well-known tango dancer and teacher.

**Full Name:** Florencia Taccetti
**Style:** Traditional salon with refined follower technique

**Life & Tango:** Florencia trained in ballet and modern dance before turning to tango in 1985. She became a protégé of Graciela González and developed an expertise in the art of adornment and follower technique. She is listed among the "Masters of the New Generation" alongside Gustavo Naveira, Chicho Frumboli, and Osvaldo Zotto.

**Teaching:** She produced instructional videos on "Technique and Adornment for Followers," covering posture, balance, ochos, turns, boleos, amagues, and ganchos. She has co-taught and performed internationally with dancers including Alex Krebs and Luciana Valle.

## Additional Notable Figures of this Era

These dancers were frequently mentioned alongside those on your list and are worth knowing:

- **Antonio Todaro** — Bricklayer and milonguero whose classes filled with new students during the revival. His tango featured both partners standing upright with precise, complex figures. A foundational teacher for Miguel Angel Zotto, Graciela González, and many others.
- **Lampazo (José Vázquez)** — Great dancer and teacher who ran Club Sin Rumbo and brought Carlos Pérez back to tango. His death created the opening for Carlos & Rosa at Sunderland.
- **Carlos Gavito** — Famous for his dramatic, theatrical style with deep forward lean. International stage star.
- **Susana Miller** — Codified "estilo milonguero" after studying with Tete Rusconi. Her school at Club Almagro trained a generation.
- **Mingo Pugliese** — Important teacher who influenced Alberto Dassieu, Myriam Pincen, and others. Known for his expertise in dancing to Pugliese's music.
- **Martha Anton & Manolo "El Gallego" Salvador** — Referred to as "mi madre de tango" by Graciela González. Key figures in the transmission of traditional tango.
- **Luis "Milonguita" Lemos** — Co-founder (with El Turco José) of the Urquiza style. Known as "el Misterio de Urquiza." Came from "La Siberia," the poorest area of Villa Urquiza.
- **Jorge Dispari & María del Carmen** — Important Villa Urquiza style dancers. Dispari's note about tango traditions was preserved by Myriam Pincen.
- **Petróleo (Carlos Estévez)** — One of the legendary Villa Urquiza masters who perfected the style's elegance alongside Portalea.
- **Alberto Dassieu** — Born 1936. Milonguero who danced with Elba Biscay and Paulina Spinoso. Studied under Mingo Pugliese and developed a synthesis of old milonguero foundations with contemporary movement awareness.

## The Key Venues

**Club Sin Rumbo** (Villa Urquiza) — One of the oldest milongas in Buenos Aires. Run for decades by Julio Dupláa. UNESCO-selected for safeguarding traditional milonga culture (2013). Birthplace of the Urquiza style.

**Club Sunderland** (Villa Urquiza) — Founded 1919 as a football club. Saturday nights became "La Milonga del Mundo." Carlos Pérez & Rosa Forte's home base. Also UNESCO-listed (2013).

**El Beso** — Downtown Buenos Aires. Home milonga for Ricardo Vidort, Osvaldo y Coca Cartery, Pocho y Nelly, and many others.

**Confitería Ideal** — Iconic downtown venue dating to the early 20th century. Featured in the BBC documentary *La Confitería Ideal: The Tango Salon* (2005) with Pupi Castello.

**Lo de Celia** (Barrio Constitución) — Where Ismael El Jalil, Roberto Segarra, and other old milongueros danced.

**Salon Canning** — Historic venue for exhibitions and social dancing. Where Adela Galeazzi and Elba Biscay performed.

**Salon Rodriguez** — Marta Famá's milonga, frequented by Roberto Segarra.

## Summary: The Pre-Nuevo World

The world these dancers inhabited was profoundly different from today's global tango scene. There were no technique seminars, no festival circuits, no online tutorials. Tango was learned the hard way — years of watching, months of practicing man-to-man, and then the trial-by-fire of the social dance floor where your reputation was everything.

These were working people. Gerardo Portalea was a gravedigger. Pupi Castello was a laborer. Carlos Pérez had a family and a regular job. They didn't dance tango for fame or money — they danced because tango was woven into the fabric of their community, their identity, their way of being in the world.

When the military government crushed nightlife in the 1950s, most of them simply stopped — for 20 or 30 years. They got married, raised children, lived ordinary lives. But the dance was still inside them. And when democracy returned in the 1980s and the tango revival began, they came back to the floor and found that their bodies still remembered everything.

The window for learning from these masters was heartbreakingly brief. Finito died in 1987 at 55. Pepito Avellaneda died in 1996. Ricardo Vidort in 2006. Pupi Castello and Gerardo Portalea both in 2007. Tete Rusconi in 2010. Roberto Segarra lasted until 2018, dancing until nearly the end at age 98.

What they left behind is not a syllabus or a curriculum — it's a way of moving, a way of listening, a way of holding another person. Their tango was about the walk, the music, the embrace, and the connection. Everything else was decoration.

The styles that emerged from their world — Villa Urquiza, milonguero, salon, orillero — are not competing brands but different expressions of the same fundamental truth: tango is danced from the inside out.
